# Module 08 — Distributions and Limit Theorems

> After this module you can look at a column of data — clicks, waiting times, pixel noise, defect counts — name the distribution that generated it, compute any probability about it by hand or with `scipy.stats`, and explain *why* averages become Gaussian, how fast, and when they do not.

## Why this matters for ML and AI

A loss function is a distribution in disguise. Mean-squared error is the negative log-likelihood of Gaussian noise; binary cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli label; the softmax-cross-entropy that trains every classifier and every large language model is the negative log-likelihood of a Categorical distribution over classes or tokens. Poisson regression predicts counts, Gamma and Beta distributions are the priors inside Bayesian A/B tests and the parameters of Thompson-sampling bandits, and the Student-t distribution is what you quietly rely on every time you report a confidence interval from a small validation set. Knowing the dozen named distributions in this module means being able to read the generative story behind each of these objectives instead of memorising them.

The second half of the module is about what happens when you *average*. Every number you compute from data — a training loss over a mini-batch, an accuracy on a test set, a Monte Carlo estimate of an expectation — is an average of random quantities. The law of large numbers says such averages settle down to the true expectation, which is the only reason minimising the empirical risk on a finite dataset says anything about the true risk. The central limit theorem says the averaging error is approximately Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, which is why the noise in stochastic gradient descent looks Gaussian, why the error bars on a benchmark result can be computed at all, and why a 1,000-person poll has a margin of error of about three percentage points. The theorem also fails in instructive ways: heavy-tailed data break it, and an engineer who has not seen that failure will trust an average that is quietly meaningless.

The third thread is Monte Carlo. The expectation you need is almost never computable in closed form — the expected loss over a data distribution, the expected return of a policy, the integral in a Bayesian posterior — so you estimate it by sampling and averaging, and the limit theorems tell you exactly how accurate the estimate is. Monte Carlo is how diffusion models are trained, how variational autoencoders estimate their objective, how reinforcement-learning agents estimate value, how insurers price catastrophe risk and how every ray-traced film frame is rendered. This module builds all of it from a seeded NumPy generator.

## 1. Why named distributions: a distribution is a modelling assumption

**Intuition.** Nobody observes a distribution; you observe data and *choose* a distribution as a compact description of how the data could have been generated. The choice is a modelling assumption, and the right way to make it is to ask for the **story**: how does nature produce one data point? "Each of 20 customers independently buys with probability 0.1 and I count the buyers" is a story, and it pins down the Binomial distribution before a single formula is written. The named distributions in this module are the stories that recur so often that they earned names: repeated independent trials, waiting for a first success, rare events in a window, accumulated small errors. Each name comes with a small set of **parameters** that is all you need to estimate from data — a Normal is fully described by two numbers, no matter how many observations you have — and that compression is the whole point of a *parametric family*.

**Definition.** A **parametric family** is a set of distributions $\{P_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\}$ indexed by a finite-dimensional parameter $\theta$. For a discrete family the member $P_\theta$ has PMF $p(x \mid \theta) = P(X = x)$; for a continuous family it has PDF $f(x \mid \theta)$ with $P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x \mid \theta)\,dx$. Two parameters recur: a **location** parameter shifts the distribution ($f(x - \mu)$) and a **scale** parameter stretches it ($\frac{1}{\sigma} f(x/\sigma)$). A **rate** is the reciprocal of a scale. `scipy.stats` exposes every family through the same five methods, which you should memorise once:

| Method | Meaning | Discrete example |
|---|---|---|
| `pmf(k)` / `pdf(x)` | $P(X = k)$ / density $f(x)$ | `binom.pmf(2, n=20, p=0.1)` |
| `cdf(x)` | $F(x) = P(X \le x)$ | `binom.cdf(2, 20, 0.1)` |
| `sf(x)` | survival function $1 - F(x) = P(X > x)$, accurate in the tail | `binom.sf(2, 20, 0.1)` |
| `ppf(q)` | quantile function $F^{-1}(q)$ | `binom.ppf(0.95, 20, 0.1)` |
| `rvs(size, random_state)` | random samples | `binom.rvs(20, 0.1, size=1000, random_state=rng)` |

Two conventions trip everyone up. First, `scipy.stats` parametrises continuous families by `loc` and `scale`, so the Exponential with rate $\lambda$ is `expon(scale=1/lam)` and the Normal with standard deviation $\sigma$ is `norm(loc=mu, scale=sigma)` — never pass a variance. Second, SciPy's `geom` and NumPy's `rng.geometric` both count the trial *on which* the first success occurs ($k \ge 1$), whereas many textbooks — and SciPy's `nbinom` — count the *failures before* a success ($k \ge 0$); always check the support before trusting a formula.

**Worked example.** Take the simplest continuous family, $X \sim \text{Uniform}(a, b)$ with density $f(x) = \frac{1}{b-a}$ on $[a, b]$. With $a = 2$, $b = 8$: the density is $1/6$ everywhere on $[2, 8]$, so $P(3 < X < 4) = \int_3^4 \frac{1}{6}\,dx = \frac{1}{6}$. The mean is the midpoint, $\mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{a+b}{2} = 5$; the variance is $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12} = \frac{36}{12} = 3$ (compute $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \frac{1}{6}\int_2^8 x^2\,dx = \frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{512 - 8}{3} = 28$ and subtract $25$); the standard deviation is $\sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$. The CDF is $F(x) = \frac{x - 2}{6}$, so the 90th percentile solves $\frac{x-2}{6} = 0.9$, giving $x = 7.4$. In SciPy this family is `uniform(loc=2, scale=6)` — `scale` is the *width*, not the upper end — and `uniform(loc=2, scale=6).ppf(0.9)` returns `7.4`.

**Real-life example (manufacturing).** A machining process is specified to produce shafts between 2.00 and 8.00 mm from some reference; if the process has no systematic preference inside the tolerance band, the Uniform is the honest first model, and it says the mean deviation from the nominal 5 mm is zero but the standard deviation is 1.73 mm. Later measurement might show the shafts cluster near the middle, at which point the Normal becomes the better story — and the module's point is that you should be able to say *which* assumption you are making and *what data* would overturn it.

**In ML/AI this is …** every output layer. A model does not predict a number; it predicts the parameters of a distribution — a Bernoulli probability for a sigmoid unit, a Categorical vector for a softmax, a Gaussian mean (and sometimes variance) for a regression head, a Poisson rate for a count model — and the loss is the negative log of that distribution's PMF or PDF evaluated at the observed label. `torch.distributions` and `tfp.distributions` mirror the `scipy.stats` interface almost method for method (`log_prob`, `cdf`, `icdf`, `sample`), and the Uniform is the raw material of all of them: every random number a computer draws starts as a `Uniform(0, 1)`.

## 2. Bernoulli, Binomial and Categorical/Multinomial

**Intuition.** One yes/no trial is a Bernoulli variable. Count the yeses in $n$ independent, identical trials and you have a Binomial. Replace yes/no by one of $K$ categories and the two distributions become the Categorical (one trial) and the Multinomial (counts over $n$ trials). These are the distributions of *labels*: a click or no click, a defective chip or a good one, the class of an image, the next token in a sentence.

**Definition.** $X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$ takes the value 1 with probability $p$ and 0 with probability $1 - p$, so $\mathbb{E}[X] = p$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X) = p(1-p)$ (because $X^2 = X$, $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = p$). If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent $\text{Bernoulli}(p)$ then $S = \sum_i X_i \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$ with

$$P(S = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}, \quad k = 0, 1, \dots, n, \qquad \mathbb{E}[S] = np, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S) = np(1-p).$$

The binomial coefficient counts the arrangements of $k$ successes among $n$ positions, and $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ is the probability of any one arrangement. The mean and variance follow from linearity and from the fact that variances of independent variables add — no sum over $k$ is needed. A **Categorical** variable with probability vector $\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K)$, $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$, takes category $k$ with probability $\pi_k$; written as a **one-hot** vector $y \in \{0, 1\}^K$ its PMF is $p(y \mid \pi) = \prod_k \pi_k^{y_k}$, which simply picks out the entry for the observed class. Counts $(N_1, \dots, N_K)$ over $n$ independent Categorical trials are **Multinomial**:

$$P(N_1 = n_1, \dots, N_K = n_K) = \frac{n!}{n_1! \cdots n_K!}\,\pi_1^{n_1} \cdots \pi_K^{n_K}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[N_k] = n\pi_k, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(N_k) = n\pi_k(1 - \pi_k), \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(N_j, N_k) = -n\pi_j\pi_k .$$

Each $N_k$ is marginally $\text{Binomial}(n, \pi_k)$, and the negative covariance says that one count can only grow at the expense of the others.

**Worked example.** A product page converts 10% of visitors; 20 visitors arrive. With $n = 20$, $p = 0.1$:

| $k$ | $\binom{20}{k}$ | $0.1^k \cdot 0.9^{20-k}$ | $P(S = k)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | $0.9^{20} = 0.1216$ | 0.1216 |
| 1 | 20 | $0.1 \times 0.9^{19} = 0.01351$ | 0.2702 |
| 2 | 190 | $0.01 \times 0.9^{18} = 0.001501$ | 0.2852 |
| 3 | 1140 | $0.001 \times 0.9^{17} = 0.0001668$ | 0.1901 |

So $P(S \le 2) = 0.1216 + 0.2702 + 0.2852 = 0.6769$ and $P(S \ge 3) = 0.3231$. The mean is $np = 2$ purchases, the variance $np(1-p) = 1.8$, the standard deviation $1.34$ — the mode is 2 but 3 or more purchases happen a third of the time, and 5 or more happen with probability `binom.sf(4, 20, 0.1)` $= 0.043$. For the Multinomial: a support team labels 10 tickets as "bug", "question" or "feature request" with $\pi = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1)$. The probability of exactly $(7, 2, 1)$ is $\frac{10!}{7!\,2!\,1!}\,0.7^7\,0.2^2\,0.1^1 = 360 \times 0.08235 \times 0.04 \times 0.1 = 0.1186$ — the single most likely split, yet it happens only 12% of the time.

**Real-life example (manufacturing / quality).** A fab tests 100 chips from a lot whose true defect rate is 2%. The number of defective chips is $\text{Binomial}(100, 0.02)$: $P(0) = 0.98^{100} = 0.133$, $P(\le 1) = 0.403$, and $P(\ge 5) = 0.051$. An acceptance rule "reject the lot if 5 or more fail" therefore rejects a perfectly normal 2%-defect lot one time in twenty — the producer's risk — and the same table, computed at a 6% defect rate, gives the consumer's risk of accepting a bad lot. The whole discipline of acceptance sampling is Binomial tables.

**In ML/AI this is …** the loss functions. The negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli label $y$ under predicted probability $\hat p$ is $-[y \log \hat p + (1-y)\log(1 - \hat p)]$ — binary cross-entropy. For a one-hot Categorical label it is $-\sum_k y_k \log \hat\pi_k = -\log \hat\pi_{\text{true class}}$ — the cross-entropy loss behind `nn.CrossEntropyLoss` and every language model's next-token objective, with the softmax outputs $\hat\pi$ as the Categorical parameters ([Module 11](../../part-4-statistics/11-likelihood-and-bayesian-inference/README.md) derives this). Dropout masks are Bernoulli samples; the variance $p(1-p)$ of a Bernoulli is why the standard error of an accuracy estimate is $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$; and `MultinomialNB` is a Multinomial likelihood over word counts.

## 3. Geometric and Negative Binomial

**Intuition.** Instead of fixing the number of trials and counting successes, fix the number of successes and count the trials. "How many visitors until the first purchase?" is Geometric. "How many until the third?" is Negative Binomial. The Geometric has a startling property: if you have already waited 5 trials without a success, the number of *further* trials you must wait has exactly the same distribution as when you started — the trials carry no memory.

**Definition.** With independent $\text{Bernoulli}(p)$ trials, let $X$ be the number of the trial on which the first success occurs. Then $X \sim \text{Geometric}(p)$ on $\{1, 2, \dots\}$:

$$P(X = k) = (1-p)^{k-1} p, \qquad P(X > k) = (1-p)^k, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{1}{p}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{1-p}{p^2}.$$

The survival function is immediate — $X > k$ means the first $k$ trials all failed — and it gives **memorylessness**: $P(X > m + k \mid X > m) = \frac{(1-p)^{m+k}}{(1-p)^m} = (1-p)^k = P(X > k)$. The Geometric is the only discrete distribution with this property. (Some texts count the failures *before* the first success, $Y = X - 1$ on $\{0, 1, \dots\}$, with mean $(1-p)/p$; SciPy's `geom` and NumPy's `rng.geometric` use the "trial number" convention.) The **Negative Binomial** counts the failures $Y$ before the $r$-th success:

$$P(Y = k) = \binom{k + r - 1}{k} p^r (1-p)^k, \qquad \mathbb{E}[Y] = \frac{r(1-p)}{p}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \frac{r(1-p)}{p^2},$$

which is the sum of $r$ independent failures-before-success Geometrics (mean and variance add). Its variance always exceeds its mean — the defining feature of **over-dispersed** counts — and it is often re-parametrised by its mean $\mu$ and a dispersion $r$, with $\operatorname{Var} = \mu + \mu^2/r$; as $r \to \infty$ it becomes the Poisson of Section 4.

**Worked example.** Visitors convert independently with $p = 0.1$. The probability that the first purchase is made by the 5th visitor is $0.9^4 \times 0.1 = 0.0656$. The probability that nobody has bought after 10 visitors is $0.9^{10} = 0.349$, so $P(X \le 10) = 0.651$. The expected wait is $1/p = 10$ visitors with standard deviation $\sqrt{0.9}/0.1 = 9.49$ — nearly as large as the mean, so "about 10" is a poor summary; the median solves $0.9^k = 0.5$, $k = \ln 0.5 / \ln 0.9 \approx 6.6$, so half the time the first buyer arrives by the 7th visitor. Memorylessness: $P(X > 8 \mid X > 5) = 0.9^3 = 0.729 = P(X > 3)$. For the third purchase, the number of non-buyers seen first is Negative Binomial with $r = 3$: mean $3 \times 0.9 / 0.1 = 27$, variance $270$, standard deviation $16.4$, and $P(Y = 0) = 0.1^3 = 0.001$ — the mean of 27 sits with a huge spread, and `nbinom.cdf(10, 3, 0.1)` $= 0.134$ says that seeing the third buyer within the first 13 visitors is unusual.

**Real-life example (manufacturing / reliability).** A machine passes a daily inspection with probability 0.98 independently of previous days; the day of the first failure is $\text{Geometric}(0.02)$ with mean 50 days. A manager who argues "it has run 60 days clean, it is overdue" commits the gambler's fallacy: memorylessness says the remaining time to failure is still Geometric(0.02) with mean 50 days. Wear-out *does* exist, but then the Geometric is the wrong model and the hazard rate of Section 5 must rise with age — the model has to say so explicitly.

**In ML/AI this is …** the Negative Binomial likelihood used for over-dispersed count data: single-cell RNA-sequencing read counts (the `scVI` and `DESeq2` models), insurance claim frequencies and demand forecasts all have variance far above their mean, so a Poisson likelihood would be badly over-confident and a Negative Binomial head on a neural network is the fix. The Geometric distribution is the length distribution of a sequence generated by a model with a fixed per-step stopping probability (an `<EOS>` token emitted with probability $p$), and the number of rejection-sampling proposals until acceptance ([Module 18](../../part-6-deep-learning-and-modern-ai/18-sampling-mcmc-and-generative-models/README.md)) is Geometric with mean $1/(\text{acceptance rate})$.

## 4. Poisson and the Poisson process

**Intuition.** Count rare events in a fixed window — calls arriving at a help desk in an hour, typos on a page, mutations in a stretch of DNA, photons hitting a camera pixel during an exposure. Each tiny sub-interval has a tiny chance of containing an event, there are very many sub-intervals, and the events do not interact. The count is then Poisson, with a single parameter: the expected number of events. The Poisson is what the Binomial becomes when "many trials, each unlikely" is pushed to its limit, and its signature is that the *variance equals the mean*.

**Definition.** $N \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$, $\lambda > 0$, has

$$P(N = k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}, \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, \qquad \mathbb{E}[N] = \operatorname{Var}(N) = \lambda .$$

The probabilities sum to 1 because $\sum_k \lambda^k/k! = e^\lambda$. *Derivation as a Binomial limit.* Let $S_n \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \lambda/n)$ — $n$ trials, each with success probability $\lambda/n$, so the mean is always $\lambda$. Then

$$P(S_n = k) = \binom{n}{k}\Big(\frac{\lambda}{n}\Big)^k\Big(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\Big)^{n-k} = \frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k}}_{\to\, 1}\cdot\underbrace{\Big(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\Big)^{n}}_{\to\, e^{-\lambda}}\cdot\underbrace{\Big(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\Big)^{-k}}_{\to\, 1} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}.$$

The practical rule: a $\text{Binomial}(n, p)$ with large $n$ and small $p$ is well approximated by $\text{Poisson}(np)$; the variance $np(1-p) \approx np$ shows why the mean and variance merge. The **Poisson process** with rate $\lambda$ per unit time is the continuous-time version of the story: the number of events in any interval of length $t$ is $\text{Poisson}(\lambda t)$, counts in disjoint intervals are independent, and — the link to Section 5 — the waiting time between consecutive events is $\text{Exponential}(\lambda)$, because $P(\text{no event in } (0, t]) = e^{-\lambda t}$. Two independent Poisson variables add to a Poisson with the summed rate (Section 8 proves it with MGFs), and thinning a Poisson process by keeping each event independently with probability $q$ gives a Poisson process of rate $q\lambda$.

**Worked example.** A help desk receives $\lambda = 3$ calls per hour on average. In one hour:

| $k$ | $e^{-3}\,3^k/k!$ | $P(N = k)$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | $0.04979 \times 1$ | 0.0498 |
| 1 | $0.04979 \times 3$ | 0.1494 |
| 2 | $0.04979 \times 4.5$ | 0.2240 |
| 3 | $0.04979 \times 4.5$ | 0.2240 |

Hence $P(N \ge 4) = 1 - 0.6472 = 0.3528$; four or more calls arrive in more than a third of all hours even though the mean is 3. The standard deviation is $\sqrt{3} = 1.73$. Over an 8-hour shift the count is $\text{Poisson}(24)$, with standard deviation $4.9$. Compare with the Binomial story "180 customers per hour, each calls with probability $1/60$": $P(S = 0) = (59/60)^{180} = 0.0485$ versus $e^{-3} = 0.0498$, and $P(S = 2) = 0.2247$ versus $0.2240$ — the largest discrepancy across all $k$ is $0.0019$. Finally, the gap between two calls is $\text{Exponential}(3 \text{ per hour})$, with mean 20 minutes and $P(\text{gap} > 30 \text{ min}) = e^{-1.5} = 0.223$.

**Real-life example (genomics / healthcare).** In DNA sequencing, each base of a genome is covered by a random number of reads; with an average depth of $5\times$ the coverage at a given base is approximately $\text{Poisson}(5)$, so a fraction $e^{-5} = 0.0067$ of bases receive no read at all — about 20 million bases of a 3-billion-base human genome. At $30\times$ depth the uncovered fraction drops to $e^{-30} \approx 10^{-13}$, which is why clinical whole-genome sequencing uses about $30\times$. The same arithmetic sizes an epidemiological surveillance system: if a rare adverse event occurs at a background rate of 2 per 100,000 patient-years, then in a cohort of 50,000 patient-years the count is $\text{Poisson}(1)$, and observing 4 or more cases has probability $0.019$ — a signal worth investigating.

**In ML/AI this is …** Poisson regression, the generalised linear model that predicts $\log \lambda = w^\top x$ and trains with the Poisson negative log-likelihood $\lambda - y\log\lambda$ (`PoissonRegressor` in scikit-learn, [Module 13](../../part-5-learning-theory/13-linear-models-and-regularization/README.md)); the Poisson shot-noise model of image sensors that denoising networks are trained to invert; and the diagnostic for over-dispersion — if the sample variance of your counts is far above the sample mean, a Poisson output layer is the wrong assumption and the Negative Binomial of Section 3 is the fix.

## 5. Uniform and Exponential

**Intuition.** The Uniform is the distribution of "no information": every value in the interval is equally plausible, and it is the raw material from which all other random numbers are built. The Exponential is the continuous analogue of the Geometric — the waiting time until an event when events arrive at a constant rate and the past carries no information about the future. Its key property is again memorylessness: a component whose lifetime is Exponential does not age.

**Definition.** $U \sim \text{Uniform}(a, b)$ has density $f(u) = 1/(b - a)$ on $[a, b]$, mean $(a+b)/2$ and variance $(b-a)^2/12$ (Section 1). The **Exponential** with rate $\lambda > 0$ has

$$f(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t} \ (t \ge 0), \qquad F(t) = 1 - e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[T] = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(T) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}.$$

The mean follows from integration by parts: $\int_0^\infty t\,\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt = [-te^{-\lambda t}]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\,dt = 1/\lambda$. The median is $\ln 2/\lambda \approx 0.693/\lambda$, well below the mean because the density is skewed to the right. **Memorylessness**: $P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = e^{-\lambda(s+t)}/e^{-\lambda s} = e^{-\lambda t}$; the Exponential is the only continuous distribution with this property. The **hazard rate** $h(t) = f(t)/P(T > t)$ is the instantaneous failure rate of an item that has survived to $t$; for the Exponential it is the constant $\lambda$, and a rising or falling hazard (wear-out, infant mortality) calls for a different family — the Weibull, whose hazard is a power of $t$, or the Gamma of Section 7. **Sampling**: if $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$ then $T = -\ln(U)/\lambda$ is $\text{Exponential}(\lambda)$, because $P(-\ln U/\lambda > t) = P(U < e^{-\lambda t}) = e^{-\lambda t}$ — the inverse-CDF method, which the lesson notebook verifies.

**Worked example.** Buses arrive as a Poisson process at $\lambda = 0.2$ per minute (one every 5 minutes on average). Your wait $T$ is $\text{Exponential}(0.2)$. $P(T > 5) = e^{-1} = 0.368$ — you wait longer than the average more than a third of the time. The median wait is $\ln 2 / 0.2 = 3.47$ minutes. $P(2 < T < 4) = e^{-0.4} - e^{-0.8} = 0.6703 - 0.4493 = 0.221$. The 90th percentile solves $1 - e^{-0.2 t} = 0.9$, so $t = -\ln(0.1)/0.2 = 11.5$ minutes. Memorylessness: having already waited 5 minutes, $P(T > 10 \mid T > 5) = e^{-1} = 0.368$ — exactly the probability of waiting more than 5 minutes from scratch. The standard deviation equals the mean, 5 minutes.

**Real-life example (physics / engineering).** Radioactive decay is the canonical Exponential: a carbon-14 nucleus decays at rate $\lambda = \ln 2 / 5730 \approx 1.21 \times 10^{-4}$ per year, so the probability that a given nucleus survives 10,000 years is $e^{-1.21} = 0.298$ and, by the law of large numbers over $10^{20}$ nuclei, almost exactly 29.8% of a sample remains. The half-life *is* the median of the lifetime distribution. In reliability engineering, the constant-hazard assumption is what makes "mean time between failures" meaningful for electronics in their useful-life phase; it fails for bearings and tyres, whose hazard grows with wear, and a Weibull with shape parameter above 1 is used instead.

**In ML/AI this is …** the `Uniform(0,1)` behind every `rng.random()` call, and inverse-CDF sampling $T = -\ln U/\lambda$, which is how `rng.exponential` works and the first sampler built in [Module 18](../../part-6-deep-learning-and-modern-ai/18-sampling-mcmc-and-generative-models/README.md). The Exponential is the holding-time distribution in continuous-time Markov chains and the Poisson-process model of event streams (clicks, spikes in neural data) that temporal point-process models learn; the hazard function is the object that survival-analysis models (Cox regression, DeepSurv) predict, used for customer churn and clinical time-to-event prediction; and uniform initialisation, `nn.init.uniform_`, is the Uniform turned into a prior over weights.

## 6. The Normal distribution

**Intuition.** Add up many small, independent contributions — measurement errors, genetic and environmental influences on height, pixel noise from thousands of photon arrivals — and the result has a bell-shaped distribution regardless of what the contributions themselves looked like. That is the central limit theorem (Section 10), and it is why the Normal is everywhere. The Normal is also the "most random" distribution with a given mean and variance (it has maximum entropy, [Module 14](../../part-5-learning-theory/14-information-theory/README.md)), and it stays in the family under linear maps, addition *and* conditioning ([Module 09](../09-multivariate-probability/README.md)), which is why Gaussian models stay tractable where others do not.

**Definition.** $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ has density

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big), \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \sigma^2 .$$

The CDF has no closed form; it is written $\Phi$ for the **standard Normal** $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and tabulated or computed numerically (`norm.cdf`). Every Normal reduces to the standard one by **standardisation**: $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$, so $P(X \le x) = \Phi\big(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\big)$. The **68–95–99.7 rule**: $P(|X - \mu| \le \sigma) = 0.6827$, $P(|X - \mu| \le 2\sigma) = 0.9545$, $P(|X - \mu| \le 3\sigma) = 0.9973$; the exact two-sided 95% point is $1.96\sigma$. **Closure under linear maps**: $aX + b \sim \mathcal{N}(a\mu + b, a^2\sigma^2)$, and if $X_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ and $X_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$ are independent then $X_1 + X_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$ — the sum is *exactly* Normal, not approximately (Section 8 proves it in one line with MGFs). Hence the average of $n$ independent $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ variables is exactly $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$. The **log-normal**: if $\ln Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ then $Y$ is log-normal, with $\mathbb{E}[Y] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$ (note: *not* $e^\mu$), median $e^\mu$ and mode $e^{\mu - \sigma^2}$. It is the distribution of a *product* of many small independent factors — multiplicative noise — and it is right-skewed with a heavy upper tail.

**Worked example.** Adult heights in a population are $\mathcal{N}(165, 7^2)$ cm. The fraction taller than 180 cm: $z = (180 - 165)/7 = 2.14$, so $P(X > 180) = 1 - \Phi(2.14) = 0.0161$, about 1 in 62. The 99th percentile: $\Phi^{-1}(0.99) = 2.326$, so $165 + 2.326 \times 7 = 181.3$ cm. Two-sided: 95% of people lie within $165 \pm 1.96 \times 7 = [151.3, 178.7]$. Sum: the combined height of two randomly chosen people is $\mathcal{N}(330, 98)$, standard deviation $9.9$ cm. Log-normal: if $\ln(\text{income})$ is $\mathcal{N}(\ln 50{,}000, 0.5^2)$, the median income is 50,000, the mean is $50{,}000 \times e^{0.125} = 56{,}657$, the mode is $50{,}000 \times e^{-0.25} = 38{,}940$, and $P(\text{income} > 100{,}000) = 1 - \Phi(\ln 2 / 0.5) = 1 - \Phi(1.386) = 0.083$ — mean above median above mode, the signature of right skew.

**Real-life example (manufacturing / quality).** A filling machine dispenses $\mathcal{N}(500, 4^2)$ grams into packets labelled 500 g. The probability of a packet below 490 g is $\Phi(-2.5) = 0.0062$, about 6 per thousand; the fraction within $\pm 5$ g is $\Phi(1.25) - \Phi(-1.25) = 0.789$. To guarantee that at most 0.1% of packets fall below 490 g the engineer needs $\Phi(-10/\sigma) = 0.001$, i.e. $10/\sigma = 3.09$, $\sigma \le 3.24$ g — a reduction of 19% in the machine's variability, which is a concrete engineering target. Statistical process control charts are the 68–95–99.7 rule drawn as horizontal lines: a measurement outside $\pm 3\sigma$ happens with probability 0.0027 under control, so it signals a problem.

**In ML/AI this is …** the squared-error loss: if $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ then $-\log p(y \mid x) = \frac{(y - f(x))^2}{2\sigma^2} + \text{const}$, so minimising MSE is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise ([Module 11](../../part-4-statistics/11-likelihood-and-bayesian-inference/README.md)). Gaussian initialisation (`nn.init.normal_`, Xavier and He init choose the $\sigma^2$, [Module 16](../../part-6-deep-learning-and-modern-ai/16-neural-networks-from-scratch/README.md)); a Gaussian prior on weights, whose log-density $-\|w\|^2/(2\sigma^2)$ is L2 regularisation; the Gaussian latent $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ of a VAE and the Gaussian forward process of diffusion models, both of which rely on closure under addition to add noise in one step ([Module 18](../../part-6-deep-learning-and-modern-ai/18-sampling-mcmc-and-generative-models/README.md)); and batch normalisation, which standardises activations to $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ scale.

## 7. Gamma, Beta, Chi-square and Student-t

**Intuition.** Four workhorses built from the distributions above. The **Gamma** is the waiting time for the $k$-th event of a Poisson process — a sum of Exponentials — and, because it lives on $(0, \infty)$, the natural distribution for a rate or a variance. The **Beta** lives on $(0, 1)$ and is the natural distribution for a *probability*: it is what you believe about a conversion rate after seeing some successes and failures. The **Chi-square** is the sum of squared standard Normals, which is what a sample variance is. The **Student-t** is what a standardised Normal average becomes when you must estimate the standard deviation from the same small sample — a bell curve with heavier tails, and a robust alternative to the Normal wherever outliers occur.

**Definition.** The **Gamma** with shape $\alpha > 0$ and rate $\beta > 0$ has density

$$f(x) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}x^{\alpha - 1}e^{-\beta x}, \quad x > 0, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2},$$

where $\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty t^{\alpha - 1}e^{-t}\,dt$ generalises the factorial ($\Gamma(k) = (k-1)!$). $\text{Gamma}(1, \beta)$ is $\text{Exponential}(\beta)$, and the sum of $k$ independent $\text{Exponential}(\beta)$ variables is $\text{Gamma}(k, \beta)$. SciPy uses a *scale* $\theta = 1/\beta$: `gamma(a=alpha, scale=1/beta)`. The **Beta** with parameters $a, b > 0$ has density

$$f(\theta) = \frac{\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}}{B(a, b)}, \quad 0 < \theta < 1, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\theta] = \frac{a}{a+b}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\theta) = \frac{ab}{(a+b)^2(a+b+1)},$$

with $B(a, b) = \Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)$. Its shapes: $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ is Uniform; $a, b > 1$ gives a hump with mode $\frac{a-1}{a+b-2}$; $a, b < 1$ gives a U-shape; $a < 1 < b$ piles mass near 0. The **Beta–Binomial conjugacy** that [Module 11](../../part-4-statistics/11-likelihood-and-bayesian-inference/README.md) develops: if your prior for a success probability is $\text{Beta}(a, b)$ and you observe $s$ successes and $f$ failures, the posterior is $\text{Beta}(a + s, b + f)$ — you just add the counts, so $a$ and $b$ behave as "pseudo-counts" of prior successes and failures. The **Chi-square** with $k$ degrees of freedom is $\chi^2_k = Z_1^2 + \dots + Z_k^2$ for independent standard Normals; it equals $\text{Gamma}(k/2, 1/2)$, with mean $k$ and variance $2k$. For a sample of $n$ from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, $(n-1)s^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$. The **Student-t** with $\nu$ degrees of freedom is the distribution of $Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}$ with $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $V \sim \chi^2_\nu$ independent; its density is proportional to $(1 + t^2/\nu)^{-(\nu+1)/2}$, so the tails decay like a *power* $|t|^{-(\nu+1)}$ rather than like $e^{-t^2/2}$. It has mean 0 (for $\nu > 1$), variance $\nu/(\nu - 2)$ (for $\nu > 2$), approaches $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ as $\nu \to \infty$, and is the Cauchy for $\nu = 1$. The sample mean of $n$ Normals standardised by the *sample* standard deviation, $\frac{\bar X - \mu}{s/\sqrt{n}}$, is $t_{n-1}$ — the fact behind the t-test and small-sample confidence intervals ([Module 10](../../part-4-statistics/10-estimation-and-sampling/README.md), [Module 12](../../part-4-statistics/12-hypothesis-testing-and-experiments/README.md)).

**Worked example.** *Gamma.* Buses arrive at rate $0.2$ per minute; the time until the third bus is $\text{Gamma}(3, 0.2)$ with mean $15$ minutes and standard deviation $\sqrt{3}/0.2 = 8.66$. Its density at $x = 10$ is $\frac{0.2^3}{2!}\,10^2 e^{-2} = 0.004 \times 100 \times 0.1353 = 0.0541$. $P(\text{third bus after 20 min}) = P(\text{at most 2 arrivals in 20 min}) = e^{-4}(1 + 4 + 8) = 0.238$ — a Gamma tail *is* a Poisson CDF. *Beta.* Start from the mild prior $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$ for a conversion rate, observe 7 conversions in 50 visits: posterior $\text{Beta}(9, 45)$ with mean $9/54 = 0.1667$, mode $8/52 = 0.154$, standard deviation $\sqrt{9 \times 45 / (54^2 \times 55)} = 0.050$; the posterior probability that the rate exceeds 20% is `beta.sf(0.2, 9, 45)` $= 0.241$, and a 95% credible interval is $[0.081, 0.276]$. The raw estimate $7/50 = 0.14$ has been pulled slightly toward the prior mean $0.5$. *Chi-square.* With $n = 5$ observations the scaled sample variance is $\chi^2_4$ (mean 4), and `chi2.ppf(0.95, 4)` $= 9.49$: the sample variance exceeds $2.37\sigma^2$ one time in twenty. *Student-t.* With $\nu = 3$, $P(|T| > 3) = 0.058$ versus $0.0027$ for the Normal — 21 times more mass beyond 3 standard units; $P(|T| > 5) = 0.015$ versus $6 \times 10^{-7}$. The 97.5% quantile is $2.78$ for $\nu = 4$, $2.26$ for $\nu = 9$, $2.05$ for $\nu = 29$ and $1.96$ in the Normal limit, which is why a confidence interval from 5 observations must be 42% wider than the "$\pm 1.96$" rule suggests.

**Real-life example (e-commerce / Bayesian A/B testing).** A marketing team runs two landing pages. After 50 visits each, page A converted 7 times and page B 12 times. With $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$ priors the posteriors are $\text{Beta}(9, 45)$ and $\text{Beta}(14, 40)$, with means $0.167$ and $0.259$. Drawing 100,000 samples from each posterior and counting how often B's rate exceeds A's gives $P(\theta_B > \theta_A) \approx 0.89$ — a direct statement of the probability the team cares about, with no p-value in sight. The same Beta machinery drives Thompson sampling in a recommender: each arm keeps a Beta posterior, each request samples one rate per arm and shows the arm with the highest draw.

**In ML/AI this is …** Thompson sampling with Beta posteriors over click-through rates ([Module 19](../../part-6-deep-learning-and-modern-ai/19-markov-decision-processes-and-rl/README.md)); the Beta and Dirichlet (its $K$-category generalisation) as the conjugate priors behind Laplace smoothing and topic models; the Gamma prior on a precision $1/\sigma^2$ in Bayesian regression and as the mixing distribution that turns a Poisson into a Negative Binomial; the Chi-square behind the chi-square test of independence and the goodness-of-fit test ([Module 12](../../part-4-statistics/12-hypothesis-testing-and-experiments/README.md)); and the Student-t as a robust likelihood — a regression trained with $t$-distributed noise, or the Student-t loss in some robust estimators, down-weights outliers that would dominate a Gaussian fit, and the t-SNE algorithm uses a $t_1$ (Cauchy) kernel in the low-dimensional space precisely because its heavy tail lets dissimilar points sit far apart.

## 8. Moments, MGFs and tails

**Intuition.** The mean locates a distribution and the variance measures its width; the third and fourth moments describe its *shape* — whether it leans (skewness) and how much of its mass lives far from the centre (kurtosis). The moment generating function packs *all* the moments into one function, and it has a magic property: the MGF of a sum of independent variables is the product of their MGFs, which turns hard questions about sums into easy algebra. Tails matter because the rare, extreme values decide whether an average is trustworthy: a Normal's tails die like $e^{-x^2/2}$ and a $4\sigma$ event is a once-in-16,000 affair, whereas a power-law tail produces $4\sigma$ events routinely and may not even have a finite variance.

**Definition.** The $k$-th **moment** of $X$ is $\mathbb{E}[X^k]$ and the $k$-th **central moment** is $\mathbb{E}[(X - \mu)^k]$. The standardised third and fourth central moments are the **skewness** $\gamma_1 = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^3]/\sigma^3$ (zero for symmetric distributions, positive for a long right tail) and the **excess kurtosis** $\gamma_2 = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^4]/\sigma^4 - 3$, defined so that the Normal has $\gamma_2 = 0$; positive excess kurtosis means heavier tails than the Normal. The **moment generating function** is

$$M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}] = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^k}{k!}\,\mathbb{E}[X^k], \qquad \text{so} \qquad \mathbb{E}[X^k] = M_X^{(k)}(0),$$

when the expectation is finite for $t$ in a neighbourhood of 0. Two facts make it useful: if $M_X = M_Y$ on such a neighbourhood then $X$ and $Y$ have the same distribution (uniqueness), and for independent $X, Y$, $M_{X+Y}(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}e^{tY}] = M_X(t)M_Y(t)$. *Derivation — the sum of independent Poissons.* For $N \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$,

$$M_N(t) = \sum_{k \ge 0} e^{tk}\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!} = e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k \ge 0}\frac{(\lambda e^t)^k}{k!} = e^{-\lambda}e^{\lambda e^t} = \exp\big(\lambda(e^t - 1)\big).$$

If $N_1 \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1)$ and $N_2 \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_2)$ are independent, $M_{N_1 + N_2}(t) = \exp\big(\lambda_1(e^t - 1)\big)\exp\big(\lambda_2(e^t - 1)\big) = \exp\big((\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)(e^t - 1)\big)$, which is the MGF of $\text{Poisson}(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)$; by uniqueness, that is the distribution of the sum. The same two lines with $M(t) = \exp(\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2)$ prove that sums of independent Normals are Normal, and with $M(t) = (1 - t/\beta)^{-\alpha}$ that sums of Gammas with a common rate are Gamma. Tail behaviour: a distribution is **light-tailed** if its MGF is finite near 0 (equivalently its tails decay at least exponentially — Normal, Exponential, Poisson, anything bounded) and **heavy-tailed** otherwise (log-normal, Student-t, Pareto with $P(X > x) \propto x^{-\alpha}$, Cauchy). For a Pareto tail with index $\alpha$ the variance is infinite when $\alpha \le 2$ and even the mean is infinite when $\alpha \le 1$. Two distribution-free reminders from [Module 07](../07-probability-foundations/README.md) bound tails from moments alone: **Markov**, $P(X \ge a) \le \mathbb{E}[X]/a$ for $X \ge 0$, and **Chebyshev**, $P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le 1/k^2$.

**Worked example.** The Exponential(1) has $\mathbb{E}[X^k] = k!$, so $M(t) = \sum_k t^k = \frac{1}{1-t}$ for $t < 1$ — finite near 0, hence light-tailed. Its central moments give skewness $\gamma_1 = 2$ and excess kurtosis $\gamma_2 = 6$; the Uniform has $\gamma_2 = -1.2$ (no tails at all), the $t_5$ has $\gamma_2 = 6$ and the $t_4$ has infinite kurtosis. A $\text{Bernoulli}(p)$ has skewness $(1 - 2p)/\sqrt{p(1-p)}$, which is $0$ at $p = 0.5$ but $2.67$ at $p = 0.1$ and $9.85$ at $p = 0.01$ — the number that will govern how slowly the CLT works in Section 10. Tails: for a Pareto with $\alpha = 1.5$, $P(X > 10x_{\min}) = 10^{-1.5} = 0.032$ and $P(X > 100x_{\min}) = 0.001$; its mean is finite but its variance is infinite, so Chebyshev's inequality says nothing about it. Markov on the Exponential(1): $P(X \ge 5) \le 1/5 = 0.2$, while the truth is $e^{-5} = 0.0067$ — valid, but 30 times too loose.

**Real-life example (finance).** Daily stock-index returns have sample excess kurtosis far above the Gaussian value of 0 — typically well into the single digits, and much higher in samples that include a crash — and the October 1987 crash was a drop of more than 20 standard deviations of the then-prevailing daily volatility — an event a Gaussian model assigns probability below $10^{-80}$, roughly "never in the age of the universe". Risk models that assumed Normal returns were calibrated on the centre of the distribution and were blind to the tail that actually destroys portfolios. Student-t or generalised-Pareto tails are now standard in value-at-risk models, and the moment that matters is the fourth one, not the second.

**In ML/AI this is …** gradient clipping, which exists because the distribution of gradient norms in deep networks is heavy-tailed and a single huge gradient can wreck training; the heavy-tailed noise that motivates Adam's per-coordinate normalisation and that makes the Huber loss and $t$-distributed likelihoods more robust than MSE; the cumulant-generating function $\log M(t)$ at the core of the Chernoff and Hoeffding concentration bounds in [Module 15](../../part-5-learning-theory/15-generalization-and-learning-theory/README.md); and the kurtosis-based contrast function that independent component analysis maximises to separate mixed signals.

## 9. The Law of Large Numbers

**Intuition.** Average enough independent copies of a random quantity and the average stops being random: it settles on the expectation. This is the fact that makes an insurance company, a casino, a Monte Carlo simulation and a machine-learning training set work. It does *not* say that a run of bad luck will be "balanced out" by good luck — the past is never compensated; it is diluted.

**Definition (weak law of large numbers).** Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be independent and identically distributed with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, and let $\bar X_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$. Then for every $\varepsilon > 0$,

$$P\big(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon\big) \;\le\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty .$$

*Proof.* By linearity $\mathbb{E}[\bar X_n] = \mu$, and by independence $\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n) = \sigma^2/n$. Chebyshev's inequality applied to $\bar X_n$ with $k\sigma/\sqrt{n} = \varepsilon$ gives exactly the displayed bound. $\square$ The law holds even without a finite variance as long as the mean exists (that needs a different proof), and the **strong law** says more: with probability 1 the sequence $\bar X_n$ converges to $\mu$ — not just "probably close for each fixed large $n$" but "the whole trajectory eventually stays close". What the law does **not** say: it does not say $\sum_i X_i$ gets close to $n\mu$ (the *sum* wanders further and further, like $\sqrt{n}$); it does not say that after 10 heads a tail is "due" (the next flip is still 50/50 — the fraction converges because 10 flips are swamped by the following 10,000, not corrected); and it says nothing at all when the mean does not exist, as for the Cauchy distribution, whose sample mean has the *same* Cauchy distribution for every $n$ and never settles down.

**Worked example.** Fair coin, $\mu = 0.5$, $\sigma^2 = 0.25$. How many flips guarantee $P(|\bar X_n - 0.5| \ge 0.01) \le 0.05$? Chebyshev: $\frac{0.25}{n \times 0.0001} \le 0.05$ gives $n \ge 50{,}000$. (The CLT in Section 10 will show that about $9{,}600$ suffice — Chebyshev is a guarantee, not an estimate.) Gambler's fallacy in numbers: after 100 flips with 60 heads the proportion is 0.60; if the next 900 flips are perfectly typical (450 heads), the proportion becomes $510/1000 = 0.51$ — the excess of 10 heads is untouched, it has merely been diluted from 10% to 1%. The sum $S_n - n/2$ has standard deviation $\sqrt{n}/2$: 5 after 100 flips, 50 after 10,000. The *count* drifts away while the *proportion* converges.

**Real-life example (insurance / casino).** An insurer writes 10,000 identical policies; each has a 1% chance of a USD 20,000 claim, so the expected claim per policy is USD 200 and the standard deviation per policy is $\sqrt{0.01 \times 0.99} \times 20{,}000 = 1{,}990$ — ten times the mean, hopeless for a single policy. For the pool, the average claim has standard deviation $1{,}990/\sqrt{10{,}000} = 19.9$, about 10% of the mean, so a premium of USD 230 leaves a margin of 1.5 standard deviations and the business is viable; with only 100 policies the standard deviation would be 199 and the same premium would be a coin toss. European roulette pays $-1/37 \approx -0.027$ per unit bet on red, with standard deviation $0.9996$; over a million bets the casino's average take per bet has standard deviation $0.001$, so the house edge is 27 standard deviations from zero and the chance that the casino loses money over the million bets is for all practical purposes nil. Neither business "predicts" any individual outcome; both rely entirely on the law of large numbers.

**In ML/AI this is …** the justification for empirical risk minimisation: the training loss $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \ell(f_\theta(x_i), y_i)$ converges to the true risk $\mathbb{E}[\ell(f_\theta(x), y)]$ for each fixed $\theta$ — the starting point of [Module 15](../../part-5-learning-theory/15-generalization-and-learning-theory/README.md), where the hard part is making the statement hold for all $\theta$ at once. The mini-batch gradient is an average of per-example gradients and converges to the full gradient at rate $\sigma/\sqrt{B}$ as the batch size $B$ grows; and the running averages inside Adam, batch-norm statistics and exponential moving averages of weights are all LLN estimators of expectations that change slowly.

## 10. The Central Limit Theorem

**Intuition.** The law of large numbers says the average converges to $\mu$; the central limit theorem says *how* it fluctuates around $\mu$ on the way: magnify the error $\bar X_n - \mu$ by $\sqrt{n}$ and its distribution becomes the bell curve, whatever the distribution of the individual $X_i$ — dice, coin flips, exponential waiting times, anything with a finite variance. The individual distribution leaves one fingerprint: the more skewed it is, the larger $n$ must be before the bell appears.

**Definition (CLT).** With $X_i$ i.i.d., mean $\mu$ and variance $0 < \sigma^2 < \infty$,

$$Z_n = \frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{\sum_i X_i - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} \;\xrightarrow{\ d\ }\; \mathcal{N}(0, 1), \qquad \text{i.e.} \quad P(Z_n \le z) \to \Phi(z) \text{ for every } z .$$

In practice: $\bar X_n \approx \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ and $\sum_i X_i \approx \mathcal{N}(n\mu, n\sigma^2)$. The proof hinges on MGFs: $\log M_{Z_n}(t) = n\log M_{(X-\mu)/\sigma}(t/\sqrt{n})$, and a second-order Taylor expansion of the inner log-MGF gives $n\,(t^2/2n + O(n^{-3/2})) \to t^2/2$, the log-MGF of $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. The rate of convergence is quantified by the **Berry–Esseen theorem**: $\sup_z |P(Z_n \le z) - \Phi(z)| \le C\,\rho/(\sigma^3\sqrt{n})$ with $\rho = \mathbb{E}|X - \mu|^3$ and $C < 0.48$, so the error of the Normal approximation shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$ and is proportional to the standardised third absolute moment — essentially the skewness. The skewness of $\bar X_n$ is $\gamma_1/\sqrt{n}$, which gives the working rule: you need $n \gtrsim (\gamma_1/0.3)^2$ before the average looks roughly symmetric. When the variance is infinite the theorem fails outright: averages of Cauchy variables are Cauchy, and averages of Pareto variables with $1 < \alpha < 2$ converge to a *stable* law whose fluctuations shrink only like $n^{-(1 - 1/\alpha)}$ — slower than $n^{-1/2}$ — with no bell in sight.

**Worked example.** *Dice.* The mean of $n = 30$ fair-die rolls has $\mu = 3.5$ and $\sigma/\sqrt{n} = 1.708/5.477 = 0.312$, so $P(\bar X_{30} > 4) \approx 1 - \Phi(0.5/0.312) = 1 - \Phi(1.60) = 0.055$. *Coin.* The number of heads in 100 flips is $\text{Binomial}(100, 0.5) \approx \mathcal{N}(50, 25)$; $P(\ge 60 \text{ heads}) \approx 1 - \Phi\big(\frac{59.5 - 50}{5}\big) = 1 - \Phi(1.9) = 0.0287$ with the continuity correction, against the exact $0.0284$ (without the correction, $1 - \Phi(2) = 0.0228$, noticeably worse). *How fast.* For an Exponential source ($\gamma_1 = 2$) the mean of 30 has skewness $2/\sqrt{30} = 0.37$ and the Berry–Esseen bound is $0.4748 \times 2.415/\sqrt{30} = 0.21$ (the actual maximum CDF error is about $0.03$ — the bound is pessimistic, but its $1/\sqrt{n}$ scaling is right); 400 observations bring the skewness down to $0.1$. For a $\text{Bernoulli}(0.01)$ source ($\gamma_1 = 9.85$) the mean of 30 has skewness $1.80$ — the "average" is a count of 0, 1 or 2 rare events, nothing like a bell — and you need $n \approx 9{,}700$ for skewness $0.1$. A Bernoulli(0.5) source, by contrast, is symmetric and already bell-shaped at $n = 30$.

**Real-life example (polling / A/B testing).** A poll asks 1,000 voters and finds 52% for candidate A. Each answer is a $\text{Bernoulli}(p)$, so the sample proportion is approximately $\mathcal{N}(p, p(1-p)/1000)$ with standard deviation $\sqrt{0.52 \times 0.48/1000} = 0.0158$; the 95% **margin of error** is $1.96 \times 0.0158 = \pm 3.1$ percentage points, and the headline "52%" is consistent with anything from 49% to 55%. Since $p(1-p) \le 1/4$, the margin is at most $1.96 \times 0.5/\sqrt{n} \approx 1/\sqrt{n}$, which is why "a thousand respondents, three points" is the industry standard and why halving the margin needs four times the sample ($n = 2{,}401$ for $\pm 2$ points). An A/B test with 10,000 users per arm comparing conversion rates of 5.0% and 5.5% has standard error $\sqrt{0.05 \times 0.95/10^4 + 0.055 \times 0.945/10^4} = 0.00315$ for the difference, so the observed gap of $0.005$ is $z = 1.59$ standard errors — not significant at the 5% level. Detecting that half-point lift with 80% power needs about 31,000 users per arm ([Module 12](../../part-4-statistics/12-hypothesis-testing-and-experiments/README.md) derives the formula); the CLT is what makes the arithmetic possible before the experiment is run.

**In ML/AI this is …** why the noise in a mini-batch gradient is approximately Gaussian — it is an average of $B$ per-example gradients — which is the assumption behind modelling SGD as a stochastic differential equation and behind the linear scaling rule for the learning rate; why a reported test accuracy carries a standard error $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$ and two models differing by less than two such standard errors cannot be ranked; why the sum of many independent weight contributions makes pre-activations in a wide randomly-initialised network Gaussian (the starting point of neural-network Gaussian processes and of the $\sqrt{d_k}$ scaling in attention, [Module 17](../../part-6-deep-learning-and-modern-ai/17-mathematics-of-transformers/README.md)); and why the bootstrap distribution of a statistic in [Module 10](../../part-4-statistics/10-estimation-and-sampling/README.md) usually looks Normal.

## 11. Monte Carlo methods

**Intuition.** If you cannot compute an expectation, simulate it: draw samples from the distribution, apply the function, average. The law of large numbers says the average converges to the expectation and the central limit theorem says the error is Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ — so you can report an error bar without knowing the true answer. The approach is indifferent to dimension (a 1,000-dimensional integral costs the same per sample as a 1-dimensional one), which is why it dominates wherever grids fail, and its one weakness — the slow $1/\sqrt{n}$ rate — can be softened by clever sampling.

**Definition.** To estimate $\theta = \mathbb{E}[g(X)]$, draw $X_1, \dots, X_n$ i.i.d. from the distribution of $X$ and compute

$$\hat\theta_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n g(X_i), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat\theta_n] = \theta, \qquad \operatorname{sd}(\hat\theta_n) = \frac{\sigma_g}{\sqrt{n}}, \quad \sigma_g^2 = \operatorname{Var}(g(X)),$$

and report $\hat\theta_n \pm 1.96\,\hat\sigma_g/\sqrt{n}$ with $\hat\sigma_g$ the sample standard deviation of the $g(X_i)$ — the **standard error**, justified by the CLT. An integral $\int_a^b h(x)\,dx$ becomes an expectation by writing it as $(b-a)\,\mathbb{E}[h(U)]$ with $U \sim \text{Uniform}(a, b)$. **Variance reduction** keeps $n$ fixed and shrinks $\sigma_g$. *Antithetic variates*: if $g$ is monotone, $g(U)$ and $g(1-U)$ are negatively correlated, so averaging the pair $\frac{1}{2}[g(U_i) + g(1 - U_i)]$ has variance $\frac{1}{2}[\sigma_g^2 + \operatorname{Cov}(g(U), g(1-U))] < \sigma_g^2/2$ — fewer than half the variance of two independent draws, for the same cost. Other tools, previewed in [Module 18](../../part-6-deep-learning-and-modern-ai/18-sampling-mcmc-and-generative-models/README.md): *importance sampling* draws from a convenient $q$ and re-weights by $p/q$; *control variates* subtract a correlated quantity with known mean. **Generating the samples** rests on the inverse-CDF method: $X = F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$, so $-\ln(U)/\lambda$ is Exponential, $\lfloor \ln U / \ln(1-p) \rfloor + 1$ is Geometric, and `rng.choice` with weights is inverse-CDF on a discrete CDF.

**Worked example.** Estimate $I = \int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,dx = 0.74682$ (no elementary antiderivative). Write $I = \mathbb{E}[e^{-U^2}]$, $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$. The integrand has $\sigma_g^2 = \int_0^1 e^{-2x^2}\,dx - I^2 = 0.59814 - 0.55774 = 0.0404$, $\sigma_g = 0.201$, so $n = 10{,}000$ samples give standard error $0.0020$: a typical run reports something like $0.7475 \pm 0.0020$. One more decimal place ($\pm 0.0002$) costs $n = 10^6$; two more cost $10^8$ — the $1/\sqrt{n}$ tax. Antithetic variates: $e^{-x^2}$ is monotone on $[0, 1]$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(g(U), g(1-U)) = -0.0388$, so the pair average has variance $\frac{1}{2}(0.0404 - 0.0388) = 0.0008$ against $0.0202$ for two independent draws — a 25-fold reduction, equivalent to 25 times the samples for free. The estimated standard error from the sample standard deviation is itself accurate to a few per cent at $n = 10{,}000$, which is what makes the error bar trustworthy.

**Real-life example (finance / computer graphics / project risk).** A European call option pays $\max(S_T - K, 0)$ at expiry; under the standard log-normal model of the stock price $S_T$ its fair price is the discounted expectation of the payoff, which for this simple contract has the Black–Scholes closed form (USD 7.13 for $S_0 = 100$, $K = 105$, volatility 20%, rate 3%, one year). For path-dependent contracts — an option on the *average* price over the year, a basket of 30 correlated stocks — there is no formula, and banks price them by simulating 100,000 price paths and averaging the discounted payoff, with antithetic paths as a routine variance reduction. Every physically-based rendered image is a Monte Carlo estimate too: the colour of a pixel is an integral over all light paths reaching it, estimated by tracing a few hundred random rays; the characteristic grain of a low-sample render is exactly $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ noise, and denoisers trained on rendered images are learning to remove it. And the project-schedule risk model of [Module 07](../07-probability-foundations/README.md) is the same idea with sums and maxima of task durations.

**In ML/AI this is …** stochastic gradient descent, whose mini-batch gradient is a Monte Carlo estimate of the full gradient with standard error $\propto 1/\sqrt{B}$; the single-sample estimate of the ELBO in a variational autoencoder, made low-variance by the reparameterisation trick; the REINFORCE policy-gradient estimator and its baseline, which is a control variate; the randomly-sampled noise level and timestep in every diffusion-model training step; dropout and MC-dropout uncertainty estimates; and the expected-improvement integrals in Bayesian optimisation. Whenever a paper says "we estimate the expectation with $K$ samples", Section 11 tells you the error is $\sigma/\sqrt{K}$ and whether $K = 1$ (as in most VAEs) can possibly be enough.

## Common pitfalls and misconceptions

1. **"The sample mean of anything becomes Normal if $n$ is large."** → The CLT needs a finite variance and i.i.d. (or weakly dependent) summands. Cauchy and Pareto-with-$\alpha \le 2$ averages never become Normal, and strongly correlated observations (time series, repeated measurements of the same user) behave like a far smaller $n$. → Check the tails (a Q–Q plot, the sample kurtosis as $n$ grows) and the dependence before trusting a Normal error bar.
2. **"$n = 30$ is enough for the CLT."** → The required $n$ scales with the squared skewness: an Exponential source (skewness 2) is fine at 30–50, a log-normal with $\sigma = 1$ (skewness about 6) needs several hundred, and a $\text{Bernoulli}(0.01)$ (skewness about 10) needs thousands. → Use the rule $n \gtrsim (\gamma_1/0.3)^2$, or simulate the sampling distribution for your actual data.
3. **Memorylessness means "a failure is due".** → It means the opposite: for Geometric and Exponential lifetimes the remaining wait is independent of the elapsed wait, so a component that has survived 60 days is exactly as reliable as a new one. → If experience says old parts fail more often, the hazard is not constant and the Exponential is the wrong model; use a Weibull or Gamma.
4. **Variance equals mean for all count data.** → That is a Poisson property, and real counts (claims, reads, clicks per user) are routinely over-dispersed. A Poisson model then understates every standard error. → Compare the sample variance with the sample mean; if it is much larger, switch to a Negative Binomial.
5. **`scipy.stats` takes the variance / the rate.** → Continuous families take `loc` and `scale`: `norm(mu, sigma)` not `norm(mu, sigma**2)`, `expon(scale=1/lam)` not `expon(lam)`, `gamma(a, scale=1/beta)`. → Check `.mean()` and `.var()` of the object against the formula before you compute anything else.
6. **The LLN says bad luck is evened out.** → The proportion converges because early deviations are *diluted*, not compensated; the absolute deviation of the sum grows like $\sqrt{n}$. → Think "the next 10,000 flips swamp the first 10", never "tails are due".
7. **Monte Carlo error falls like $1/n$.** → It falls like $1/\sqrt{n}$: 100 times the samples for one extra decimal. → Report every estimate with its standard error $\hat\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, and use antithetic or importance sampling to shrink $\hat\sigma$ instead of inflating $n$.
8. **Sums of Normals are only approximately Normal.** → They are *exactly* Normal (MGF proof in Section 8); it is sums of *non*-Normals that are only approximately Normal by the CLT. Likewise $\mathbb{E}[e^X] = e^{\mathbb{E}X}$ fails: a log-normal's mean is $e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$, not $e^\mu$. → Keep "exact closure" (Normal, Poisson, Gamma with common rate) separate from "asymptotic approximation" (CLT).

## Real-world case studies

### Case study 1: sizing an intensive-care unit

A hospital's ICU admits on average 3 patients per day, and a patient's stay lasts on average 4 days. Admissions arrive as a Poisson process and stays are roughly Exponential, and a classical queueing result (the $M/G/\infty$ queue) says that in steady state the number of occupied beds is $\text{Poisson}(\lambda \times \text{mean stay}) = \text{Poisson}(12)$, regardless of the exact shape of the stay distribution. The mean occupancy is 12 beds with standard deviation $\sqrt{12} = 3.46$. If the unit has 12 beds, `poisson.sf(12, 12)` says demand exceeds capacity on 42% of days — a bed count equal to the mean is a disaster. With 16 beds the overflow probability is $0.101$, about 37 days a year; with 18 beds it is $0.037$ (14 days); reaching 1% needs `poisson.ppf(0.99, 12) = 21` beds, an occupancy of only 57%. The planning committee now has the real trade-off in numbers: every bed beyond the mean buys a smaller and smaller reduction in overflow days, and the "obvious" choice of running near full occupancy guarantees frequent diversions of critically ill patients. Two refinements follow from this module. The Normal approximation $\mathcal{N}(12, 12)$ with continuity correction gives $0.097$ for 16 beds and $0.030$ for 18 — close, but it underestimates the right tail because $\text{Poisson}(12)$ is still skewed ($\gamma_1 = 1/\sqrt{12} = 0.29$). And if admissions are higher on some weekdays or after an epidemic wave, the count is over-dispersed and the Poisson understates the tail further; the Negative Binomial with the observed variance is the honest replacement. The same arithmetic sizes call centres (the mini-project), cloud server pools and emergency-room staffing.

### Case study 2: how many test examples does a benchmark need?

A team reports that model B scores 91.0% accuracy on a 2,000-example test set versus 90.0% for model A and wants to claim an improvement. Each prediction is a Bernoulli trial, so by the CLT an accuracy of $p = 0.9$ estimated from $n = 2{,}000$ examples has standard error $\sqrt{0.9 \times 0.1/2000} = 0.0067$, i.e. a 95% interval of $\pm 1.3$ points. Treating the two accuracies as independent, the standard error of their difference is $\sqrt{0.0067^2 + 0.0064^2} = 0.0093$, so the observed 1-point gap is $z = 1.08$ — well inside noise. The models were, however, scored on the *same* examples, which lets you do better: only the discordant examples matter. If B is right where A is wrong on 90 examples and A is right where B is wrong on 70, the paired difference is $(90 - 70)/2000 = 0.01$ with standard error $\sqrt{(90 + 70) - 20^2/2000}/2000 = 0.0063$, giving $z = 1.58$ — stronger, still not conclusive ([Module 12](../../part-4-statistics/12-hypothesis-testing-and-experiments/README.md) turns this into McNemar's test). To detect a 1-point difference between two independent 90%-accuracy models with 80% power you would need roughly $13{,}500$ examples per model. The tail of the story is a rare-error metric: if a safety classifier's false-negative rate is around 1% and the test set has 1,000 positives, the expected number of misses is 10 with standard deviation $3.1$ — the estimate "1.0%" has a 95% interval of about $[0.5\%, 1.8\%]$ (the exact Binomial interval, which is asymmetric because 10 events are too few for the CLT), and pinning the rate down to $\pm 0.2$ points requires about $9{,}500$ positives. Every leaderboard gap smaller than two standard errors is a coin toss, and the standard error is computable in one line before the experiment is run.

### Case study 3: when the law of large numbers quietly fails

An insurer models individual claim sizes two ways. Model E is Exponential with mean USD 3,000; model P is Pareto with tail index $\alpha = 1.5$ and the *same* mean of USD 3,000 — a realistic shape for liability or catastrophe claims. Both have a finite mean, so the LLN applies to both and the average claim over 10,000 policies converges to 3,000 in either model. But the Pareto has infinite variance, so the CLT does not apply to it, and simulation makes the difference brutal. Under E the average of 10,000 claims has standard deviation $3{,}000/\sqrt{10{,}000} = 30$ (0.03 of the mean), the single largest claim is about 0.1% of the total, and the 99.9th percentile of the total claim bill is USD 30.9 million against a mean of 30 million — a 3% capital buffer covers it. Under P the sample average over the same 10,000 policies has a simulated standard deviation of about 470 — fifteen times larger, and it does not shrink like $1/\sqrt{n}$ as $n$ grows; across 2,000 simulated portfolios 90% of the averages fell between 2,780 and 3,310, but the worst one reached 19,240; the single largest claim typically accounts for 2% of the entire bill and occasionally for most of it; and the 99.9th percentile of the total is about USD 91 million, three times the mean. A claim above USD 900,000 (300 times the mean) has probability $300^{-1.5} = 1.9 \times 10^{-4}$ under P — about two such claims per 10,000 policies — and probability $e^{-300} \approx 10^{-130}$ under E. An actuary who estimates the mean from last year's 10,000 claims and prices accordingly will be right on average and bankrupt in the wrong year. The fixes are those of Sections 7 and 8: model the tail explicitly (generalised Pareto fits to exceedances over a high threshold), reinsure the tail, and report quantiles of the total rather than its mean. The same lesson applies to ML metrics with heavy-tailed losses: the mean training loss on a batch containing one wildly mislabelled example is dominated by that example, which is why gradient clipping and robust losses exist.

## Self-assessment

1. **A model's validation accuracy is 0.85 on 400 examples. What is the standard error, and which distribution did you just use?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Each prediction is a Bernoulli(0.85) trial, the number of correct predictions is Binomial(400, 0.85), and the accuracy is its mean divided by $n$, with standard error $\sqrt{0.85 \times 0.15/400} = 0.018$. The Normal shape of the error bar comes from the CLT; the Binomial supplies the variance. A difference of one or two points between two such models is within noise.

   </details>
2. **Calls arrive at 6 per hour. What is the probability of no call in the next 20 minutes, and how is this a statement about two different distributions?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The count in 20 minutes is Poisson(2), so $P(0) = e^{-2} = 0.135$. Equivalently the waiting time to the next call is Exponential(6 per hour) and $P(T > 1/3\text{ h}) = e^{-6/3} = e^{-2}$. "No events in the window" and "the first gap exceeds the window" are the same event, which is exactly how the Exponential inter-arrival distribution is derived from the Poisson process.

   </details>
3. **Why does the Binomial(n, p) converge to a Poisson, and what single condition must hold?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Fix the mean $\lambda = np$ and let $n \to \infty$ with $p = \lambda/n \to 0$. In $\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ the factor $\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k} \to 1$, $(1-\lambda/n)^n \to e^{-\lambda}$ and $(1-\lambda/n)^{-k} \to 1$, leaving $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$. The condition is "many trials, each rare, fixed expected count": $n$ large with $np$ moderate.

   </details>
4. **A colleague says the Exponential is a good model for the lifetime of car tyres because "it is the standard lifetime distribution". What is wrong?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The Exponential has a constant hazard rate: a tyre with 60,000 km on it would be exactly as likely to fail in the next 1,000 km as a new tyre. Tyres wear out, so their hazard increases with age, and the Exponential's memorylessness is precisely the wrong property. A Weibull with shape parameter above 1 (increasing hazard) or a Gamma with shape above 1 is appropriate; the Exponential fits components in their useful-life phase, such as electronics, not wear-out failures.

   </details>
5. **You have a Beta(3, 7) belief about a click-through rate and observe 10 clicks in 40 impressions. What is the posterior, its mean, and how does it compare with the raw rate?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Add successes to $a$ and failures to $b$: Beta(13, 37). Its mean is $13/50 = 0.26$, between the raw rate $10/40 = 0.25$ and the prior mean $3/10 = 0.3$, pulled slightly toward the prior because the prior counts as 10 pseudo-observations against 40 real ones. The standard deviation is $\sqrt{13 \times 37/(50^2 \times 51)} = 0.061$. As data accumulate the prior's influence fades.

   </details>
6. **Show in two lines that the sum of two independent Normals is Normal.**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   $M_{X_1}(t)M_{X_2}(t) = \exp(\mu_1 t + \sigma_1^2t^2/2)\exp(\mu_2 t + \sigma_2^2 t^2/2) = \exp\big((\mu_1+\mu_2)t + (\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)t^2/2\big)$, which is the MGF of $\mathcal{N}(\mu_1+\mu_2, \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)$; by uniqueness of MGFs the sum has that distribution. Note this is *exact*, unlike the CLT.

   </details>
7. **The CLT seems to work for averages of 30 dice but not for averages of 30 Bernoulli(0.01) variables. Why?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Convergence speed is governed by skewness (Berry–Esseen: error $\propto \rho/(\sigma^3\sqrt{n})$). A die is symmetric ($\gamma_1 = 0$) and the mean of 30 is already bell-shaped. A Bernoulli(0.01) has skewness $0.98/\sqrt{0.0099} = 9.85$; the mean of 30 has skewness $9.85/\sqrt{30} = 1.8$ and is essentially a count of 0, 1 or 2 rare events. You need $n$ in the thousands — roughly $(9.85/0.3)^2 \approx 1{,}000$ for skewness 0.3, about $9{,}700$ for skewness 0.1.

   </details>
8. **Daily losses of a trading desk are averaged over 250 days and the average is reported with a Normal confidence interval. Under what circumstances is that interval meaningless?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   When the loss distribution has infinite variance (a power-law tail with $\alpha \le 2$, as heavy-tailed financial losses may), the CLT does not apply: the average is dominated by the largest few losses, its fluctuations do not shrink like $1/\sqrt{n}$, and the sample standard deviation is itself unstable, so the interval has no coverage guarantee. Also when the days are strongly dependent (volatility clustering), the effective sample size is far below 250. Check the tail with a log–log plot of exceedances and report quantiles rather than the mean.

   </details>
9. **A Monte Carlo estimate with 10,000 samples has standard error 0.02. You need 0.002. How many samples, and name one cheaper route.**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The standard error falls like $1/\sqrt{n}$, so a tenfold reduction needs 100 times the samples: $n = 10^6$. Cheaper: reduce $\sigma_g$ instead — antithetic variates for a monotone integrand, a control variate with known mean, or importance sampling from a distribution that concentrates samples where $g$ is large. In the lesson notebook antithetic sampling of $e^{-U^2}$ cuts the variance 25-fold, equivalent to 25 times the samples.

   </details>
10. **Why does minimising mean-squared error correspond to assuming Gaussian noise, and what would you minimise under Laplace noise?**
    <details><summary>Answer</summary>

    If $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, then $-\log p(y \mid x) = (y - f(x))^2/(2\sigma^2) + \text{const}$, so the maximum-likelihood fit minimises the sum of squared errors. Laplace noise has density $\propto e^{-|\varepsilon|/b}$, whose negative log is $|y - f(x)|/b$: maximum likelihood then minimises the mean *absolute* error, which is why MAE is the robust choice when outliers are expected — the Laplace has heavier tails than the Normal.

    </details>
