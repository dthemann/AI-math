# Module 09 — Multivariate Probability

> After this module you can describe several uncertain quantities *at once* — joint, marginal and conditional distributions, covariance matrices, the multivariate Gaussian — and use one operation, Gaussian conditioning, to predict a quantity you did not measure, track a moving object through noisy readings (a Kalman filter) and fit a curve with honest uncertainty bands (Gaussian-process regression).

## Why this matters for ML and AI

A data point is almost never a single number. A patient is a vector of lab values, an image is a million correlated pixels, a sentence is a sequence of tokens whose probabilities depend on each other, and a portfolio is a bundle of assets that crash together. [Module 07](../07-probability-foundations/README.md) and [Module 08](../08-distributions-and-limit-theorems/README.md) treated one random variable at a time; this module is about the *relationships* between variables, which is where almost all the information in a dataset lives. A classifier is a conditional distribution $P(y \mid x)$. A language model is a joint distribution over sequences, factorised by the chain rule into conditionals. A recommender system learns a joint distribution of (user, item) from a sparse table of its own marginals and conditionals.

The covariance matrix is the first object of this module, and it is everywhere: PCA is its eigendecomposition ([Module 03](../../part-1-linear-algebra/03-eigenvalues-svd-and-pca/README.md)), batch normalisation uses its diagonal, the Mahalanobis distance uses its inverse, portfolio risk is the quadratic form $w^\top \Sigma w$, and the reason an ensemble of ten models is not ten times better than one is the off-diagonal of the covariance matrix of their errors. The second object is the multivariate Gaussian, the only distribution for which every operation you want — marginalise, condition, transform linearly — has a closed form you can write down. The latent space of a variational autoencoder, the noise schedule of a diffusion model, the prior of a Gaussian process and the belief state of a Kalman filter are all multivariate Gaussians, and the formula for conditioning one Gaussian on another is the single most reused equation in probabilistic machine learning.

The third theme is interpretation. Correlation measures *linear* association only, uncorrelated does not mean independent, and an association that holds in the whole population can reverse inside every subgroup (Simpson's paradox). Each of these is a trap that has produced wrong medical conclusions, wrong model evaluations and, in 2008, a wrong price for several trillion dollars of mortgage securities. Knowing exactly what a correlation coefficient does and does not say is a professional skill, and this module builds it with worked numbers.

## Learning objectives

After working through this module you will be able to:

- Build a joint distribution table, read off marginals by summing and conditionals by renormalising, and test independence as factorisation.
- Compute covariance and correlation by hand, state their algebraic rules, and explain with a counterexample why zero correlation does not imply independence.
- Form the covariance matrix of a random vector, prove it is symmetric positive semidefinite, and compute the variance of any linear combination with $a^\top \Sigma a$.
- Write down the multivariate Gaussian density, read its geometry from the eigendecomposition of $\Sigma$, and sample from it with a Cholesky factor.
- Derive and apply the Gaussian marginalisation and conditioning formulas, including the Schur-complement conditional covariance.
- Explain why $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]$ is the best mean-squared-error predictor and decompose a variance with the law of total variance.
- Diagnose Simpson's paradox in a contingency table, name the confounder, and say when stratifying is and is not the right fix.
- Implement a one-dimensional Kalman filter and a Gaussian-process regressor as two instances of the same conditioning formula.
- Compute the Mahalanobis distance and use it for anomaly detection on correlated features.

## 1. Joint, marginal and conditional distributions

**Intuition.** A joint distribution is the full table of "how often does *this* combination occur". Once you have it, every other question is a bookkeeping operation. Want to forget one variable? Add up over its values — *marginalise*. Want to focus on the cases where one variable has a known value? Take that row of the table and rescale it to sum to one — *condition*. Independence is the special case where the table has no information beyond its row and column totals: every cell is just the product of a row total and a column total.

**Definition.** For discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$, the **joint PMF** is $p_{X,Y}(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y)$, with $p_{X,Y} \ge 0$ and $\sum_x \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x, y) = 1$. The **marginals** are obtained by summing out the other variable,

$$p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{X,Y}(x, y), \qquad p_Y(y) = \sum_x p_{X,Y}(x, y),$$

and the **conditional PMF** of $Y$ given $X = x$ is the renormalised row

$$p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x, y)}{p_X(x)} \quad (p_X(x) > 0).$$

Rearranged, $p_{X,Y}(x, y) = p_X(x)\,p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)$ — the **chain rule** — and for $n$ variables $p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})$. Bayes' theorem is the same identity read the other way: $p_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = p_{X,Y}(x, y)/p_Y(y)$. $X$ and $Y$ are **independent** when $p_{X,Y}(x, y) = p_X(x)\,p_Y(y)$ for all $x, y$, equivalently $p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = p_Y(y)$: the conditional does not depend on what you condition on. In matrix language, the joint table of independent variables is the outer product $p_X p_Y^\top$ and therefore has rank 1. For continuous variables replace sums by integrals: a **joint density** $f_{X,Y} \ge 0$ with $\iint f_{X,Y} = 1$, marginal $f_X(x) = \int f_{X,Y}(x, y)\,dy$ and conditional $f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = f_{X,Y}(x, y)/f_X(x)$, which is a legitimate density in $y$ for each fixed $x$.

**Worked example.** An online shop records a customer's age group $X$ and the number of purchases $Y$ in the last month. The joint PMF, estimated from 100,000 customers:

| $p_{X,Y}$ | $Y$ = none | $Y$ = one | $Y$ = several | $p_X$ (row sum) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $X$ = 18–34 | 0.20 | 0.10 | 0.05 | **0.35** |
| $X$ = 35–54 | 0.15 | 0.15 | 0.10 | **0.40** |
| $X$ = 55+ | 0.15 | 0.07 | 0.03 | **0.25** |
| $p_Y$ (column sum) | **0.50** | **0.32** | **0.18** | 1.00 |

The nine cells sum to 1. Marginalising: 40% of customers are 35–54, and 18% of all customers bought several items. Conditioning on $X$ = 35–54 renormalises the middle row by $0.40$: $p_{Y \mid X}(\cdot \mid 35\text{–}54) = (0.375, 0.375, 0.25)$. For the youngest group the row is $(0.20, 0.10, 0.05)/0.35 = (0.571, 0.286, 0.143)$. The two conditionals differ, so age and purchasing are dependent; the independence test on a single cell confirms it: $p_X(35\text{–}54)\,p_Y(\text{several}) = 0.40 \times 0.18 = 0.072 \ne 0.10$. Bayes in table form: a customer who bought several items is in the 55+ group with probability $p_{X \mid Y}(55{+} \mid \text{several}) = 0.03/0.18 = 1/6$, less than the 25% prior.

*A continuous case.* Let $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = x + y$ on the unit square $[0, 1]^2$. It integrates to $\int_0^1\int_0^1 (x + y)\,dx\,dy = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$. The marginal is $f_X(x) = \int_0^1 (x + y)\,dy = x + \tfrac12$, and the conditional density is $f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = (x + y)/(x + \tfrac12)$. At $x = 0.5$ this is $0.5 + y$, giving $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = 0.5] = \int_0^1 y(0.5 + y)\,dy = 0.25 + \tfrac13 = 0.583$; at $x = 0$ it is $2y$, giving $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = 0] = \tfrac23$. The conditional changes with $x$, so the variables are dependent — and indeed $x + y \ne (x + \tfrac12)(y + \tfrac12)$.

**Real-life example (climate / weather).** A weather station's historical table of (daily maximum temperature band, rain yes/no) is a joint distribution. Its marginals answer "how often does it rain here" and "how often is it above 25 °C"; the conditional $P(\text{rain} \mid \text{temperature band})$ is the forecast you actually want on a day whose temperature is known. In a temperate climate the rain probability is typically highest in the cool bands and lowest in the hot ones, so the conditional varies with the row and temperature and rain are dependent. The same shape — a co-occurrence table whose rows are renormalised — is a recommender system's "customers who bought $x$ also bought $y$": $p(y \mid x)$ read from the purchase table, with $p(y)$ (plain popularity) as the baseline it must beat.

**In ML/AI this is …** the softmax output $P(y \mid x)$ of every classifier — a conditional distribution over classes for each input; the chain-rule factorisation $P(w_1, \dots, w_T) = \prod_t P(w_t \mid w_{<t})$ that an autoregressive language model implements; marginalisation over latent variables in mixture models and the evidence $p(x) = \int p(x \mid z)p(z)\,dz$ of a VAE; and the conditional-independence factorisation of naive Bayes and Bayesian networks. In code the operations are literal: `J.sum(axis=1)` marginalises, `J[i] / J[i].sum()` conditions, and `np.outer(pX, pY)` is the independent table to compare against.

## 2. Covariance and correlation

**Intuition.** When two variables are dependent, the simplest question is *do they move together?* Covariance answers it with a single number: average the product of the two deviations from the mean. If large $X$ tends to come with large $Y$, the products are mostly positive; if large $X$ comes with small $Y$, mostly negative; if there is no linear tendency, they cancel. Correlation is covariance with the units divided out, so that $-1$ and $+1$ mean "perfectly on a line" regardless of whether you measured in grams or tonnes. The price of this simplicity is that covariance only sees *linear* co-movement — a perfectly deterministic U-shaped relationship can have correlation zero.

**Definition.** With $\mu_X = \mathbb{E}[X]$ and $\mu_Y = \mathbb{E}[Y]$,

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\big] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mu_X \mu_Y, \qquad \rho_{XY} = \operatorname{Corr}(X, Y) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}.$$

Rules, each a one-line consequence of linearity of expectation: $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$; symmetry $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(Y, X)$; **bilinearity** $\operatorname{Cov}(aX + bY, Z) = a\operatorname{Cov}(X, Z) + b\operatorname{Cov}(Y, Z)$; shifts do nothing, $\operatorname{Cov}(X + c, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$; and $\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$. Independence implies $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$ and hence $\operatorname{Cov} = 0$; the converse is false (below). Correlation is invariant to positive affine rescaling, $\operatorname{Corr}(aX + b, cY + d) = \operatorname{sign}(ac)\operatorname{Corr}(X, Y)$, and always lies in $[-1, 1]$. The proof is short: standardise $U = (X - \mu_X)/\sigma_X$ and $V = (Y - \mu_Y)/\sigma_Y$, so $\operatorname{Var}(U) = \operatorname{Var}(V) = 1$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(U, V) = \rho$. Then $0 \le \operatorname{Var}(U - V) = 1 + 1 - 2\rho$ gives $\rho \le 1$, and $0 \le \operatorname{Var}(U + V) = 2 + 2\rho$ gives $\rho \ge -1$. Equality holds exactly when $U - V$ (or $U + V$) has zero variance, i.e. when $Y$ is an exact affine function of $X$ — this is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|\mathbb{E}[UV]| \le \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[U^2]\,\mathbb{E}[V^2]}$ in disguise. For a sample $(x_i, y_i)_{i=1}^n$ the **sample covariance** is $s_{xy} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)$ and the sample correlation $r = s_{xy}/(s_x s_y)$, in which the $n - 1$ cancels.

**Worked example.** Heights (cm) and weights (kg) of five people: $h = (160, 165, 170, 175, 180)$, $w = (55, 62, 66, 70, 82)$. Means: $\bar h = 170$, $\bar w = 67$. Deviations: $h - \bar h = (-10, -5, 0, 5, 10)$ and $w - \bar w = (-12, -5, -1, 3, 15)$. Products: $120, 25, 0, 15, 150$, summing to $310$. Sums of squares: $\sum (h_i - \bar h)^2 = 250$, $\sum (w_i - \bar w)^2 = 404$. Hence

$$s_{hw} = \frac{310}{4} = 77.5\ \text{cm·kg}, \qquad r = \frac{310}{\sqrt{250 \times 404}} = \frac{310}{317.8} = 0.975.$$

(Sample standard deviations $s_h = \sqrt{250/4} = 7.91$ and $s_w = \sqrt{404/4} = 10.05$ give the same $r = 77.5/(7.91 \times 10.05)$.) The covariance 77.5 has awkward units and tells you little on its own; the correlation 0.975 says the five points lie very close to a straight line. *Zero correlation without independence:* let $X$ be uniform on $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and $Y = X^2$. Then $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X^3] = \frac{-1 + 0 + 1}{3} = 0$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$. But $P(X = 1, Y = 1) = \tfrac13$ while $P(X = 1)P(Y = 1) = \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac23 = \tfrac29$: dependent, and in fact $Y$ is a deterministic function of $X$. The same happens for $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Y = X^2$, since $\mathbb{E}[X^3] = 0$. For *monotone* but non-linear relationships, **Spearman's rank correlation** — Pearson's $r$ computed on the ranks — is the usual repair: for $x = 1, \dots, 7$ and $y = e^x$, Pearson gives $0.81$ and Spearman gives exactly $1$.

**Real-life example (finance).** Daily returns of two large banks typically have correlation around $0.6$–$0.8$: they are exposed to the same interest rates and the same economy. An airline and an oil producer tend to be negatively correlated, because fuel is the airline's largest cost. Portfolio managers care about this because of the sum rule: holding two assets with volatility 20% each in equal weight gives $\operatorname{Var} = 0.25(0.04) + 0.25(0.04) + 2(0.25)(0.04\rho) = 0.02(1 + \rho)$, i.e. a portfolio volatility of 14.1% when $\rho = 0$, 18.4% when $\rho = 0.7$, and 20% (no diversification at all) when $\rho = 1$. Correlations estimated from calm years are notoriously low; in a crisis they jump towards 1, which is exactly when diversification was supposed to help. Section 9 returns to this.

**In ML/AI this is …** the feature correlation matrix (`df.corr()`) you inspect before fitting a linear model, where a pair with $|r| > 0.9$ signals redundancy and the multicollinearity that inflates coefficient variance ([Module 13](../../part-5-learning-theory/13-linear-models-and-regularization/README.md)); the Pearson or Spearman filter in feature selection; and the covariance of *model errors* in an ensemble. If $n$ models have errors with equal variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$, their average has error variance $\sigma^2\big(\rho + \frac{1-\rho}{n}\big)$ (derived in the case studies) — so bagging and random feature subsets in random forests exist to drive $\rho$ down, not $\sigma^2$.

## 3. Random vectors and covariance matrices

**Intuition.** With $d$ variables there are $d$ variances and $\binom{d}{2}$ covariances; the covariance matrix stores all of them in one symmetric $d \times d$ array. Its real power is that linear algebra works on it. The variance of *any* weighted combination of the variables is a quadratic form in the weights, a linear transformation of the data transforms the matrix by a sandwich product, and its eigenvectors are the directions in which the data vary independently — the axes of the cloud. Because a variance can never be negative, the matrix can never have a negative eigenvalue; this is the geometric meaning of "positive semidefinite".

**Definition.** A **random vector** $X = (X_1, \dots, X_d)^\top$ has **mean vector** $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X] \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (componentwise) and **covariance matrix**

$$\Sigma = \operatorname{Cov}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mu)(X - \mu)^\top\big], \qquad \Sigma_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j), \quad \Sigma_{ii} = \operatorname{Var}(X_i).$$

Properties: (i) $\Sigma$ is **symmetric**, since $\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_j, X_i)$. (ii) For any fixed $a \in \mathbb{R}^d$, $\operatorname{Var}(a^\top X) = a^\top \Sigma a$. Proof: $a^\top X - a^\top\mu = a^\top(X - \mu)$ is a scalar, so $\operatorname{Var}(a^\top X) = \mathbb{E}[a^\top(X - \mu)(X - \mu)^\top a] = a^\top \Sigma a$. (iii) Hence $a^\top \Sigma a \ge 0$ for every $a$: $\Sigma$ is **positive semidefinite**, all its eigenvalues are $\ge 0$ ([Module 03](../../part-1-linear-algebra/03-eigenvalues-svd-and-pca/README.md)), and it is positive *definite* unless some non-trivial combination $a^\top X$ is constant. (iv) **Affine transformation**: for a matrix $A$ and vector $b$, $\mathbb{E}[AX + b] = A\mu + b$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(AX + b) = A\Sigma A^\top$, because $AX + b - (A\mu + b) = A(X - \mu)$ and $\mathbb{E}[A(X - \mu)(X - \mu)^\top A^\top] = A\Sigma A^\top$. (v) The **correlation matrix** is $R = D^{-1/2}\Sigma D^{-1/2}$ with $D = \operatorname{diag}(\Sigma)$; it has ones on the diagonal and must itself be PSD. (vi) The eigendecomposition $\Sigma = U\Lambda U^\top$ gives the **principal axes** (columns of $U$) and the variances along them ($\lambda_i$); $\operatorname{tr}\Sigma = \sum_i \lambda_i$ is the total variance. (vii) **Whitening**: with $\Sigma^{-1/2} = U\Lambda^{-1/2}U^\top$ (or $L^{-1}$ from the Cholesky factor $\Sigma = LL^\top$), the vector $Z = \Sigma^{-1/2}(X - \mu)$ has $\operatorname{Cov}(Z) = \Sigma^{-1/2}\Sigma\Sigma^{-1/2} = I$: uncorrelated, unit-variance coordinates. The **sample covariance** of $n$ observations stacked as rows of $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ is $S = \frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{X}_c^\top \mathbf{X}_c$ with $\mathbf{X}_c$ the column-centred data; the $n - 1$ makes $\mathbb{E}[S] = \Sigma$ (one degree of freedom is spent estimating the mean), and $S$ has rank at most $n - 1$, so it is *singular* whenever $d \ge n$ — a problem for anything that needs $\Sigma^{-1}$ in high dimensions.

**Worked example (portfolio with three assets).** Three assets have annual volatilities $\sigma = (0.20, 0.10, 0.30)$ and correlations $\rho_{12} = 0.3$, $\rho_{13} = 0.5$, $\rho_{23} = 0$. Since $\Sigma_{ij} = \rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j$,

$$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 0.040 & 0.006 & 0.030 \\ 0.006 & 0.010 & 0 \\ 0.030 & 0 & 0.090 \end{pmatrix}.$$

For weights $w = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$ compute $\Sigma w$ first: row 1 gives $0.020 + 0.0018 + 0.006 = 0.0278$, row 2 gives $0.003 + 0.003 + 0 = 0.006$, row 3 gives $0.015 + 0 + 0.018 = 0.033$. Then $w^\top\Sigma w = 0.5(0.0278) + 0.3(0.006) + 0.2(0.033) = 0.0139 + 0.0018 + 0.0066 = 0.0223$, a portfolio volatility of $\sqrt{0.0223} = 14.9\%$. Compare: the weighted average of the volatilities is $0.19$, and if the assets were uncorrelated the volatility would be $\sqrt{0.01 + 0.0009 + 0.0036} = 12.0\%$. The two positive correlations add almost three percentage points of risk. Is $\Sigma$ a valid covariance matrix? Its eigenvalues are $0.0083, 0.0276, 0.1041$, all positive, so yes. Not every symmetric matrix qualifies: the "correlation matrix" with $\rho_{12} = \rho_{13} = 0.9$ and $\rho_{23} = -0.9$ has determinant $1 + 2(0.9)(0.9)(-0.9) - 3(0.81) = -2.89 < 0$ and therefore a negative eigenvalue. Intuitively, if asset 1 moves with 2 and with 3, then 2 and 3 cannot move against each other. In a crisis scenario where all three correlations rise to $0.8$, the same weights give $w^\top\Sigma w = 0.0318$ and volatility $17.8\%$ — the diversification benefit shrinks from 4.1 to 1.2 percentage points.

**Real-life example (finance).** This is the calculation behind every risk report. A bank's trading book has thousands of positions; its one-day 99% value-at-risk under a Gaussian model is $2.33\sqrt{w^\top\Sigma w}$, and $\Sigma$ is estimated from a window of past returns. With $d = 2{,}000$ instruments and $n = 500$ trading days the sample covariance is singular (rank $\le 499$), so practitioners use *factor models* ($\Sigma = BFB^\top + D$, a low-rank-plus-diagonal structure) or shrink $S$ towards a diagonal target — the same low-rank idea as PCA and the same shrinkage idea as ridge regression.

**In ML/AI this is …** the matrix that PCA diagonalises ([Module 03](../../part-1-linear-algebra/03-eigenvalues-svd-and-pca/README.md)): `PCA(n_components=k)` computes the top-$k$ eigenvectors of $S$, and the explained-variance ratio is $\lambda_i/\operatorname{tr} S$. It is the $\Sigma$ in the **Mahalanobis distance** $d^2(x) = (x - \mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)$ used for anomaly detection and inside linear/quadratic discriminant analysis. **Batch normalisation** computes the diagonal of $\Sigma$ for a mini-batch of activations and standardises each feature; full whitening with $\Sigma^{-1/2}$ (ZCA whitening of image patches, "decorrelated batch norm") is the off-diagonal-aware version. And `np.cov(X.T)` — note the transpose: NumPy expects variables in rows — uses `ddof=1`, while `np.var` defaults to `ddof=0`; the mismatch is a classic source of small, baffling discrepancies.

## 4. The multivariate Gaussian

**Intuition.** The multivariate Gaussian is a bell shape stretched and rotated: its level sets are ellipsoids centred at $\mu$, with axes along the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ and lengths proportional to the square roots of the eigenvalues. Two numbers describe each point: its Mahalanobis distance from the centre (how many "ellipse radii" out it lies) and nothing else — the density depends on the point only through that distance. It is the maximum-entropy distribution for a given mean and covariance, it is closed under every linear operation, and it is the limit the CLT delivers for sums of random vectors. That is why it is the default model for noise in several dimensions.

**Definition.** $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$ with $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and $\Sigma$ symmetric positive definite has density

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\,|\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12 (x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)\Big),$$

where $|\Sigma|$ is the determinant and $\Delta^2(x) = (x - \mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)$ is the squared **Mahalanobis distance**. Facts: (i) $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu$, $\operatorname{Cov}(X) = \Sigma$. (ii) Level sets $\Delta^2 = c$ are ellipsoids with semi-axes $\sqrt{c\lambda_i}$ along the eigenvectors $u_i$ of $\Sigma = U\Lambda U^\top$. (iii) $\Delta^2(X) \sim \chi^2_d$, so in two dimensions the ellipse $\Delta^2 = 5.99$ contains 95% of the probability. (iv) If $\Sigma$ is diagonal the density factorises into $d$ univariate normals — for Gaussians, and *only* for Gaussians, uncorrelated means independent; if $\Sigma = \sigma^2 I$ the distribution is **isotropic** (spherical). (v) **Affine closure**: $AX + b \sim \mathcal{N}(A\mu + b, A\Sigma A^\top)$ for any matrix $A$ (even non-square), so marginals, sums and projections of Gaussians are Gaussian. (vi) **Sampling**: if $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and $\Sigma = LL^\top$ is the Cholesky factorisation ($L$ lower-triangular), then $X = \mu + LZ \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$, by (v) with $\operatorname{Cov}(LZ) = L I L^\top = \Sigma$. This is how `rng.multivariate_normal` works internally.

**Worked example.** Take $\mu = 0$ and $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Determinant $|\Sigma| = 2 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 1 = 1$, inverse $\Sigma^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, correlation $\rho = 1/\sqrt{2 \cdot 1} = 0.707$. For $x = (1, 1)$: $\Sigma^{-1}x = (1 - 1, -1 + 2) = (0, 1)$, so $\Delta^2 = x^\top(0, 1) = 1$ and $f(1, 1) = \frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-1/2} = 0.0965$. For $x = (1, -1)$, the same Euclidean length: $\Sigma^{-1}x = (1 + 1, -1 - 2) = (2, -3)$, $\Delta^2 = 2 + 3 = 5$, $f(1, -1) = \frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-5/2} = 0.0131$. The point along the correlation direction is $e^2 \approx 7.4$ times more probable than the point across it. Geometry: the eigenvalues solve $\lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 1 = 0$, giving $\lambda_1 = (3 + \sqrt5)/2 = 2.618$ and $\lambda_2 = (3 - \sqrt5)/2 = 0.382$; for $\lambda_1$, $(\Sigma - \lambda_1 I)u = 0$ gives $u_2 = 0.618\,u_1$, so the major axis points along $(0.851, 0.526)$, at $31.7°$ to the $x_1$-axis, and the one-standard-deviation ellipse has semi-axes $\sqrt{2.618} = 1.618$ and $\sqrt{0.382} = 0.618$. The 95% ellipse scales both by $\sqrt{5.99}$: semi-axes $3.96$ and $1.51$. Sampling: the Cholesky factor is $L = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt2 & 0 \\ 1/\sqrt2 & 1/\sqrt2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.414 & 0 \\ 0.707 & 0.707 \end{pmatrix}$ — check $LL^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0.5 + 0.5 \end{pmatrix}$ — so a standard-normal draw $z = (0.5, 1.2)$ becomes $x = Lz = (0.707,\ 0.354 + 0.849) = (0.707, 1.202)$. The first coordinate is scaled by $\sqrt2$; the second mixes in $0.707$ of the first, which is where the correlation comes from.

**Real-life example (physics / engineering).** A GNSS receiver's horizontal position error is well modelled as a two-dimensional Gaussian whose covariance depends on where the satellites are in the sky. With satellites spread evenly the error is nearly isotropic; in an urban canyon, where only satellites along the street are visible, the error ellipse is elongated across the street — the eigenvector of the large eigenvalue points in the direction that is poorly constrained. Receivers report exactly this matrix, and a navigation display's "95% confidence ellipse" is the level set $\Delta^2 = 5.99$. Sensor fusion (Section 8) then combines it with the covariance of the inertial sensors.

**In ML/AI this is …** the Gaussian noise model behind mean-squared-error loss ($y \sim \mathcal{N}(f(x), \sigma^2 I)$, [Module 11](../../part-4-statistics/11-likelihood-and-bayesian-inference/README.md)); the VAE, whose prior is $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and whose encoder outputs a diagonal Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu(x), \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2(x))$ sampled by the reparameterisation $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon$ — the Cholesky recipe with a diagonal $L$; the diffusion forward step $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\,x_{t-1}, \beta_t I)$ ([Module 18](../../part-6-deep-learning-and-modern-ai/18-sampling-mcmc-and-generative-models/README.md)); Gaussian discriminant analysis, where a shared $\Sigma$ gives linear decision boundaries (LDA) and class-specific $\Sigma_k$ give quadratic ones (QDA); Gaussian mixture models; and the isotropic initialisation $W_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2/n_{\text{in}})$ of He initialisation. `scipy.stats.multivariate_normal` evaluates the density; in practice you compute $\log f$ via a Cholesky factor (`np.linalg.cholesky`, `scipy.linalg.solve_triangular`) rather than inverting $\Sigma$.

## 5. Marginalisation and conditioning in Gaussians

**Intuition.** Split a Gaussian vector into a part you observe and a part you do not. Forgetting the observed part is free: the unobserved part is Gaussian with its own block of $\mu$ and $\Sigma$. Learning the observed part is almost as easy: the unobserved part stays Gaussian, its mean shifts by an amount *linear* in the observation, and its covariance shrinks by a fixed amount that does not depend on what you observed. Every fitted regression line, every Kalman update and every Gaussian-process prediction is this one computation.

**Definition.** Partition $X = (X_1, X_2)$ with

$$\mu = \begin{pmatrix} \mu_1 \\ \mu_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12} \\ \Sigma_{21} & \Sigma_{22} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \Sigma_{21} = \Sigma_{12}^\top .$$

**Marginalisation:** $X_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \Sigma_{11})$ — just read off the block (proof: $X_1 = [I\ \ 0]\,X$ is an affine map, so Section 4 (v) applies). **Conditioning:** given $X_2 = x_2$,

$$X_1 \mid X_2 = x_2 \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\big(\mu_{1 \mid 2},\ \Sigma_{1 \mid 2}\big), \qquad \mu_{1 \mid 2} = \mu_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2 - \mu_2), \qquad \Sigma_{1 \mid 2} = \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}.$$

The matrix $\Sigma_{1 \mid 2}$ is the **Schur complement** of $\Sigma_{22}$ in $\Sigma$. Three things to notice. The conditional mean is an *affine function of the observation* with coefficient matrix $\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}$ — the regression coefficients. The conditional covariance *does not depend on $x_2$*: you know in advance how much an observation will tell you. And since $\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}$ is PSD, $\Sigma_{1 \mid 2} \preceq \Sigma_{11}$ — conditioning never increases uncertainty. In two dimensions with correlation $\rho$:

$$\mathbb{E}[X_1 \mid X_2 = x_2] = \mu_1 + \rho\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2}(x_2 - \mu_2), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_1 \mid X_2 = x_2) = \sigma_1^2(1 - \rho^2).$$

*Derivation (the short way).* Define $Y = X_1 - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}X_2$. By bilinearity, $\operatorname{Cov}(Y, X_2) = \Sigma_{12} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{22} = 0$, and $(Y, X_2)$ is jointly Gaussian (an affine image of $X$), so $Y$ is *independent* of $X_2$. Hence conditioning on $X_2 = x_2$ leaves $Y$'s distribution unchanged while $X_1 = Y + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}x_2$ becomes $Y$ plus a constant. Its mean is $\mathbb{E}[Y] + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}x_2 = \mu_1 - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\mu_2 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}x_2$, which is $\mu_{1 \mid 2}$, and its covariance is $\operatorname{Cov}(Y) = \Sigma_{11} - 2\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{22}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} = \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}$. The whole argument hinges on "uncorrelated Gaussians are independent", which is why it fails for other distributions.

**Worked example.** With $\mu = 0$ and $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ from Section 4, observe $X_2 = 1.5$: $\mu_{1 \mid 2} = 0 + 1 \cdot 1^{-1}(1.5 - 0) = 1.5$ and $\Sigma_{1 \mid 2} = 2 - 1 \cdot 1^{-1} \cdot 1 = 1$, so $X_1 \mid X_2 = 1.5 \sim \mathcal{N}(1.5, 1)$: variance halved from 2. Observe instead $X_1 = 2$: $\mu_{2 \mid 1} = 1 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 2 = 1$ and $\Sigma_{2 \mid 1} = 1 - 1 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = 0.5$, so $X_2 \mid X_1 = 2 \sim \mathcal{N}(1, 0.5)$. In both directions the variance is multiplied by $1 - \rho^2 = 0.5$. *Height and weight:* in an adult population let height have mean 170 cm and standard deviation 10, weight mean 70 kg and standard deviation 12, correlation $0.6$, so $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 100 & 72 \\ 72 & 144 \end{pmatrix}$. For a person of height 185: $\mathbb{E}[W \mid H = 185] = 70 + \frac{72}{100}(185 - 170) = 70 + 10.8 = 80.8$ kg and $\operatorname{Var}(W \mid H = 185) = 144 - \frac{72^2}{100} = 144 - 51.84 = 92.16$, standard deviation $9.6 = 12\sqrt{1 - 0.36}$. Notice **regression to the mean**: 185 cm is $1.5$ standard deviations tall, but the predicted weight is only $0.9$ standard deviations heavy ($0.9 = 0.6 \times 1.5$). The reverse prediction, height from weight 90 kg: $170 + \frac{72}{144}(20) = 180$ cm with standard deviation $\sqrt{100 - 36} = 8$. The two regression slopes are $0.72$ kg/cm and $0.50$ cm/kg, whose product is $\rho^2 = 0.36$, not 1 — regressing $Y$ on $X$ is *not* the inverse of regressing $X$ on $Y$.

**Real-life example (climate).** A rural weather station fails for a week. A station 20 km away kept recording. Daily mean temperatures at the two sites are close to jointly Gaussian with correlation $\rho \approx 0.95$ (they share the weather) and standard deviation about 6 °C each. The conditional formula imputes the missing values: the predicted temperature at the failed station is its climatological mean plus $0.95$ times the neighbour's anomaly, with standard deviation $6\sqrt{1 - 0.95^2} = 1.9$ °C — a four-fold reduction of uncertainty relative to using the climatological mean alone. With several neighbouring stations, $\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}$ automatically weights them by how informative each one is *given the others*, discounting two neighbours that are nearly copies of each other. This is Gaussian conditioning as data imputation, and it is also, in a different vocabulary, what geostatisticians call kriging.

**In ML/AI this is …** linear regression. The population least-squares slope is $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)/\operatorname{Var}(X) = \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}$ and the residual variance is $\sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2)$ — `LinearRegression` estimates exactly the conditional mean of a Gaussian ([Module 13](../../part-5-learning-theory/13-linear-models-and-regularization/README.md)); $R^2 = \rho^2$ is the fraction of variance that conditioning removes. The same formula is the Kalman update and the Gaussian-process posterior (Section 8), the posterior of Bayesian linear regression ([Module 11](../../part-4-statistics/11-likelihood-and-bayesian-inference/README.md)), the reverse-process conditional $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$ that diffusion models are trained to match ([Module 18](../../part-6-deep-learning-and-modern-ai/18-sampling-mcmc-and-generative-models/README.md)), and the conditional-mean imputation of missing features.

## 6. Conditional expectation and the law of total variance

**Intuition.** $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]$ is "the best guess of $Y$ once you know $X$", written as a function of $X$. Treat it as a random variable in its own right — it inherits randomness from $X$ — and two facts follow. Averaging the best guesses over $X$ returns the overall average of $Y$. And the total variability of $Y$ splits cleanly into two parts: how much $Y$ wobbles *around* the best guess (noise you cannot remove by knowing $X$) and how much the best guess itself moves as $X$ changes (variation that knowing $X$ explains). Every regression model, and every analysis-of-variance table, is an attempt to make the second part as large as possible.

**Definition.** The **conditional expectation** $m(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x] = \sum_y y\,p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)$ (or $\int y\,f_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)\,dy$) is a function of $x$; the random variable $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X] = m(X)$ is the **regression function**. The **tower property** (law of iterated expectation) says $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\big] = \mathbb{E}[Y]$, which is the law of total probability in expectation form. **Best predictor:** among all functions $g$, $m$ minimises the mean squared error $\mathbb{E}[(Y - g(X))^2]$. Proof: write $Y - g(X) = (Y - m(X)) + (m(X) - g(X))$ and expand the square. The cross term is $2\,\mathbb{E}\big[(Y - m(X))\,h(X)\big]$ with $h = m - g$; conditioning on $X$ inside, $\mathbb{E}\big[h(X)\,\mathbb{E}[Y - m(X) \mid X]\big] = \mathbb{E}[h(X) \cdot 0] = 0$. Therefore

$$\mathbb{E}\big[(Y - g(X))^2\big] = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(Y - m(X))^2\big]}_{\text{irreducible}} + \mathbb{E}\big[(m(X) - g(X))^2\big] \;\ge\; \mathbb{E}\big[(Y - m(X))^2\big].$$

Finally the **law of total variance**:

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\big] + \operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]\big),$$

"within" plus "between". Proof in three lines: $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}[Y^2] - (\mathbb{E}Y)^2$; by the tower property $\mathbb{E}[Y^2] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[Y^2 \mid X]\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X) + m(X)^2\big]$; and $(\mathbb{E}Y)^2 = (\mathbb{E}[m(X)])^2$, so $\mathbb{E}[m(X)^2] - (\mathbb{E}[m(X)])^2 = \operatorname{Var}(m(X))$.

**Worked example.** An insurer's customers fall into segment A (60%) and segment B (40%). Annual claim totals (in thousands) have $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid A] = 10$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid A) = 4$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid B] = 20$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid B) = 9$. Tower: $\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0.6(10) + 0.4(20) = 14$. Within-segment variance: $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)] = 0.6(4) + 0.4(9) = 6$. Between-segment variance: $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]) = 0.6(10 - 14)^2 + 0.4(20 - 14)^2 = 9.6 + 14.4 = 24$. Total: $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 30$. Knowing the segment explains $24/30 = 80\%$ of the variance and cuts the standard deviation of the unexplained part from $\sqrt{30} = 5.5$ to $\sqrt{6} = 2.4$. *Gaussian check* with $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$: $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 \mid X_2) = 1$ (constant) and $\mathbb{E}[X_1 \mid X_2] = X_2$ has variance 1, so $\operatorname{Var}(X_1) = 1 + 1 = 2$, which is $\Sigma_{11}$. In general for a Gaussian, the "between" part is $\rho^2\sigma_1^2$ and the "within" part is $(1 - \rho^2)\sigma_1^2$.

**Real-life example (e-commerce / experimentation).** An online retailer runs an A/B test on revenue per visitor. Revenue is wildly variable ($\sigma$ about USD 40 on a mean of USD 8), so detecting a 2% lift needs hundreds of thousands of visitors. But each visitor's revenue in the *previous* month is known and correlates with the current month at $\rho \approx 0.5$. Conditioning on it — the "CUPED" variance-reduction technique used at Microsoft, Netflix and Booking.com — removes the between part $\rho^2\sigma^2$ and leaves $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X) = (1 - 0.25)\sigma^2$: a 25% reduction in variance, hence 25% fewer visitors for the same power ([Module 12](../../part-4-statistics/12-hypothesis-testing-and-experiments/README.md)). Stratified sampling in surveys and blocking in agricultural trials are the same idea: pay for the between-group variance with design rather than with sample size.

**In ML/AI this is …** the target of every regressor trained with mean squared error: the function $f^*(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]$ is what `LinearRegression`, `GradientBoostingRegressor` and an MLP with MSE loss all estimate, and the irreducible term $\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$ is the noise floor no model can beat (the first term of the bias–variance decomposition in [Module 13](../../part-5-learning-theory/13-linear-models-and-regularization/README.md)). For classification, $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{Y = k\} \mid X] = P(Y = k \mid X)$, so a network trained with cross-entropy is estimating the same object. When the conditional distribution is multimodal — a self-driving car at a fork, where $\mathbb{E}[\text{steering} \mid \text{image}]$ points straight into the barrier — the conditional *mean* is the wrong summary, which is why mixture-density networks and generative models exist. The law of total variance also underlies variance-reduction baselines in policy-gradient RL ([Module 19](../../part-6-deep-learning-and-modern-ai/19-markov-decision-processes-and-rl/README.md)).

## 7. Simpson's paradox and correlation versus causation

**Intuition.** Compare two treatments in a table and A wins. Split the same table by patient severity and B wins in *every* row. Nothing is wrong with the arithmetic: a conditional relationship can reverse when you marginalise, because the marginal mixes the rows with weights that differ between the treatments. Whenever a third variable influences both which group a unit lands in and the outcome, the aggregate comparison compares apples with oranges. The paradox is a warning that "the data" cannot tell you which table to believe — only knowledge of how the data came about can.

**Definition.** For outcome $R$, treatment $T$ and stratum $S$, the **aggregate** success rate is a mixture of the stratified rates weighted by each treatment's *own* stratum distribution:

$$P(R \mid T) = \sum_s P(R \mid T, s)\,P(s \mid T).$$

**Simpson's paradox** is the situation $P(R \mid A, s) > P(R \mid B, s)$ for every $s$ yet $P(R \mid A) < P(R \mid B)$. It can only happen if $P(s \mid A) \ne P(s \mid B)$: the treatments were given to differently composed groups. A variable $S$ that influences both the treatment assignment and the outcome is a **confounder**; the **standardised** (adjusted) rate $\sum_s P(R \mid T, s)\,P(s)$ replaces each treatment's own mix with a common one and removes the confounding. **Randomisation** makes $P(s \mid T) = P(s)$ for every $S$, measured or not, by design — the reason randomised trials and A/B tests are the gold standard ([Module 12](../../part-4-statistics/12-hypothesis-testing-and-experiments/README.md)). Two cautions. If $S$ is a *mediator* — an effect of the treatment that in turn affects the outcome — stratifying on it *removes* part of the real effect, so the aggregate table is the right one. And conditioning on a **collider**, a variable caused by both $T$ and $R$ (or by their causes), creates association where none exists: among hospitalised patients, two unrelated diseases look negatively associated because having either one gets you admitted (Berkson's paradox). Which table to trust is a causal question, not a statistical one.

**Worked example.** Two treatments, 100 patients each, stratified by severity:

| | Mild: treated | Mild: recovered | Severe: treated | Severe: recovered | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Treatment A | 20 | 18 (**90%**) | 80 | 48 (**60%**) | 66/100 = **66%** |
| Treatment B | 80 | 68 (85%) | 20 | 10 (50%) | 78/100 = **78%** |

A beats B among mild patients ($90\% > 85\%$) and among severe patients ($60\% > 50\%$), yet B wins overall ($78\% > 66\%$). The confounder is severity: A was given mostly to severe patients ($P(\text{severe} \mid A) = 0.8$ versus $P(\text{severe} \mid B) = 0.2$), and severe patients recover less often whatever they receive. Standardising to a 50/50 mix: A $= 0.5(0.90) + 0.5(0.60) = 75\%$, B $= 0.5(0.85) + 0.5(0.50) = 67.5\%$ — the stratified verdict. The arithmetic of the reversal: $P(R \mid A) = 0.9(0.2) + 0.6(0.8) = 0.66$ while $P(R \mid B) = 0.85(0.8) + 0.5(0.2) = 0.78$; the *weights* did it. *Real data*: in a 1986 comparison of kidney-stone treatments (Charig et al.), open surgery succeeded in 93% of small stones (81/87) and 73% of large ones (192/263), overall 78% (273/350); percutaneous nephrolithotomy succeeded in 87% of small (234/270) and 69% of large (55/80), overall 83% (289/350). Open surgery is better for each size and worse overall, because surgeons sent the hard, large stones to open surgery.

**Real-life examples (healthcare, admissions, climate).** In the 1973 Berkeley graduate admissions data, about 44% of male and 35% of female applicants were admitted, which looked like discrimination; within departments, women were admitted at rates equal to or higher than men in most cases. Women had applied disproportionately to the most competitive departments (Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell, 1975). Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths are strongly correlated across the months of a year; the confounder is summer, and no ice-cream ban would save a swimmer. In each case the fix is the same: identify the variable that drives both, then compare like with like.

**In ML/AI this is …** confounded training data: a pneumonia detector that learned to recognise the portable X-ray machine used on the sickest patients, a skin-lesion classifier that used the dermatologist's ruler as a feature. It is also a failure mode of **model evaluation**: a model can be better on every sub-population and worse on the aggregate if the test sets of the two models have different mixes — compare per-stratum, on the same data, and report subgroup metrics in fairness audits. In **recommender systems**, the historical click table is confounded by what was shown (exposure bias), and in **A/B testing** an imbalance in assignment (more mobile users in variant B) produces exactly this table; randomisation and stratified analysis are the remedies. Methods that re-weight $P(s \mid T)$ to $P(s)$ — inverse-propensity weighting, doubly robust estimators — are the statistical machinery of causal inference.

## 8. Two applications of Gaussian conditioning: the Kalman filter and Gaussian-process regression

**Intuition.** Section 5's formula says: a Gaussian belief plus a Gaussian-related observation gives a new Gaussian belief with a shifted mean and a reduced variance. Apply it *over time*, alternating with a prediction step, and you have the Kalman filter, which tracks a hidden state through noisy measurements. Apply it *over space* — treat the unknown values of a function at many inputs as one big Gaussian vector — and you have Gaussian-process regression, which fits a curve to data and tells you how sure it is at every point. Same equation, two of the most used algorithms in engineering and machine learning.

**Definition (a): the one-dimensional Kalman filter.** A hidden position $x_t$ evolves as $x_t = x_{t-1} + v + w_t$ with known velocity $v$ and process noise $w_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, q)$; a sensor returns $z_t = x_t + r_t$ with $r_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, r)$. Suppose the current belief is $x_{t-1} \mid z_{1:t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, P)$. **Predict** (affine transformation, Section 4): $\mu^- = \mu + v$, $P^- = P + q$. **Update**: before seeing $z_t$, the pair $(x_t, z_t)$ is jointly Gaussian with mean $(\mu^-, \mu^-)$ and covariance $\begin{pmatrix} P^- & P^- \\ P^- & P^- + r \end{pmatrix}$, because $\operatorname{Cov}(x_t, z_t) = \operatorname{Cov}(x_t, x_t + r_t) = P^-$. Conditioning on $z_t$ with the Section 5 formula gives

$$K = \frac{P^-}{P^- + r}, \qquad \mu = \mu^- + K(z_t - \mu^-), \qquad P = (1 - K)P^- ,$$

where $K$ is the **Kalman gain**: close to 1 when the sensor is precise relative to the prediction ($r \ll P^-$), close to 0 when it is noisy. Equivalently precisions add, $1/P = 1/P^- + 1/r$. The filter is recursive: $O(1)$ work per step, no need to store the past.

**Definition (b): Gaussian-process regression.** A **Gaussian process** $f \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k)$ is a distribution over functions such that for any inputs $x_1, \dots, x_n$ the vector $(f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n))$ is $\mathcal{N}(0, K)$ with $K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$. The **RBF (squared-exponential) kernel** $k(x, x') = \sigma_f^2 \exp\!\big(-\frac{(x - x')^2}{2\ell^2}\big)$ says that nearby inputs have strongly correlated outputs, with $\ell$ the length-scale over which the correlation decays. Given noisy observations $y = f(X) + \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_n^2 I)$, the joint of training outputs and a test value $f_* = f(x_*)$ is Gaussian with covariance $\begin{pmatrix} K + \sigma_n^2 I & k_* \\ k_*^\top & k(x_*, x_*) \end{pmatrix}$ where $(k_*)_i = k(x_i, x_*)$; conditioning on $y$ gives the posterior

$$\mathbb{E}[f_* \mid y] = k_*^\top (K + \sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} y, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(f_* \mid y) = k(x_*, x_*) - k_*^\top (K + \sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} k_* .$$

The mean is a weighted sum of the observations, the variance is the prior variance minus what the data explain, and both come straight from $\mu_{1 \mid 2}$ and $\Sigma_{1 \mid 2}$. The cost is the $n \times n$ solve, $O(n^3)$, which is why plain GPs are used for up to a few thousand points.

**Worked example.** *Kalman:* belief $\mathcal{N}(10, 4)$, velocity $v = 1$, process noise $q = 1$, sensor noise $r = 3$, measurement $z = 13$. Predict: $\mu^- = 11$, $P^- = 5$. Gain $K = 5/(5 + 3) = 0.625$. Update: $\mu = 11 + 0.625(13 - 11) = 12.25$, $P = 0.375 \times 5 = 1.875$. Check via precisions: $1/5 + 1/3 = 8/15$, so $P = 15/8 = 1.875$. The estimate moved five-eighths of the way towards the measurement, and the standard deviation fell from $\sqrt5 = 2.24$ (prediction) to $1.37$ — better than either the prediction or the sensor ($\sqrt3 = 1.73$) alone. In the long run $P^-$ settles where $P^- = \frac{P^- r}{P^- + r} + q$, i.e. $P^- = \frac{q + \sqrt{q^2 + 4qr}}{2} = \frac{1 + \sqrt{13}}{2} = 2.30$; the steady-state gain is $K_\infty = 0.434$ and the filtered standard deviation $\sqrt{1.30} = 1.14$, a 34% improvement over the raw sensor's $1.73$ for ever after. *GP:* one observation $y = 1$ at $x = 0$, with $\ell = 1$, $\sigma_f = 1$, $\sigma_n^2 = 0.1$. At $x_* = 0.5$: $k_* = e^{-0.125} = 0.8825$, so the posterior mean is $0.8825 \times 1/1.1 = 0.802$ and the variance is $1 - 0.8825^2/1.1 = 1 - 0.708 = 0.292$ (standard deviation $0.54$). At $x_* = 2$: $k_* = e^{-2} = 0.135$, mean $0.123$, variance $0.983$ — two length-scales away, the posterior has almost returned to the prior. That growth of the error bars away from the data is the GP's most valuable feature.

**Real-life example (engineering).** Every smartphone fuses GNSS fixes (accurate to a few metres, once a second) with accelerometer and gyroscope readings (precise over a tenth of a second, drifting over minutes) in a Kalman filter; the gain automatically trusts the inertial prediction in a tunnel, where $r$ is effectively infinite, and snaps back to the satellite fix when it returns. The filter was first deployed in the Apollo guidance computer and now runs in aircraft, cars, robots and weather-data assimilation. Gaussian-process regression began as **kriging** in South African gold mining in the 1950s: the ore grade at an un-drilled location is predicted from nearby boreholes, with the posterior variance telling the engineer where the *next* borehole is most informative — the same "sample where uncertainty is highest" logic that drives modern experimental design.

**In ML/AI this is …** `sklearn.gaussian_process.GaussianProcessRegressor` and the surrogate model inside **Bayesian optimisation** (hyperparameter tuning in Optuna, scikit-optimize, Vizier), where the posterior mean and variance are combined into an acquisition function that balances exploration and exploitation; the exact-inference backbone of robotics (SLAM uses extended and unscented Kalman filters for nonlinear motion) and of the state-space models that preceded RNNs — an LSTM's hidden state is a learned, nonlinear analogue of the filter's $(\mu, P)$. There is also a deep theoretical link: an infinitely wide neural network with Gaussian weights *is* a Gaussian process (Neal, 1996), which is why GP kernels are used to understand what wide networks can learn.

## 9. Dependence beyond Gaussians

**Intuition.** A Gaussian's dependence is fully described by one correlation coefficient per pair, and that dependence is *mild in the tails*: two jointly Gaussian variables with $\rho = 0.9$ almost never have extreme values together. Real data often do — floods on neighbouring rivers, defaults on neighbouring mortgages, failures of components sharing a power supply. To talk about dependence in general you need tools that separate *how each variable is distributed* from *how they are tied together*, and that detect any kind of dependence rather than only the linear kind.

**Definition.** **Sklar's theorem**: any joint CDF can be written $F_{X,Y}(x, y) = C\big(F_X(x), F_Y(y)\big)$, where the **copula** $C$ is a joint CDF on $[0, 1]^2$ with uniform marginals. The copula carries all the dependence and none of the marginal information. The **Gaussian copula** is the copula of a bivariate normal with correlation $\rho$, attached to arbitrary marginals; the **$t$-copula** comes from a bivariate Student-$t$. **Tail dependence** $\lambda_U = \lim_{u \to 1} P\big(Y > F_Y^{-1}(u) \mid X > F_X^{-1}(u)\big)$ measures the probability of a joint extreme; it is $0$ for the Gaussian copula whenever $\rho < 1$ and strictly positive for the $t$-copula. Rank correlations (Spearman, Kendall) depend only on the copula, which is why they are invariant to monotone transformations. The general-purpose measure of dependence is **mutual information**

$$I(X; Y) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x, y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}\right] = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p_{X,Y} \,\|\, p_X p_Y\big) \ge 0,$$

which is zero *if and only if* $X$ and $Y$ are independent, and for a bivariate Gaussian equals $-\tfrac12\log(1 - \rho^2)$ ([Module 14](../../part-5-learning-theory/14-information-theory/README.md)).

**Worked example.** For the Gaussian of Section 4, $\rho = 0.707$ gives $I = -\tfrac12 \log_2(1 - 0.5) = 0.5$ bits: knowing $X_2$ tells you half a bit about $X_1$. For $X$ uniform on $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and $Y = X^2$, the correlation is 0 (Section 2), but $I(X; Y) = H(Y) = H(\tfrac13, \tfrac23) = 0.918$ bits, because $Y$ is a function of $X$ and $H(Y \mid X) = 0$. Mutual information sees the dependence that correlation misses. Conversely, take two variables with standard-normal marginals and correlation parameter $\rho = 0.5$, once tied together by a Gaussian copula and once by a $t_3$-copula. Their Pearson correlations agree to within a few hundredths, yet the conditional probability $P(Y \text{ below its } q\text{-quantile} \mid X \text{ below its } q\text{-quantile})$ behaves completely differently as $q \to 0$: under the Gaussian copula it is $0.24$ at $q = 5\%$, $0.13$ at $1\%$ and $0.05$ at $0.1\%$, heading to zero; under the $t$-copula it is $0.37$, $0.33$ and $0.33$, settling at a positive limit ($0.31$). At the $0.1\%$ level a joint crash is about six times more likely under the $t$-copula — the lesson notebook simulates it.

**Real-life example (finance).** In 2000 David X. Li proposed pricing portfolios of credit risk with a Gaussian copula, and by 2005 it was the market standard for collateralised debt obligations: mortgage defaults were given their own marginals and tied together by a single correlation estimated from a few calm years. A Gaussian copula has zero tail dependence, so the model put a negligible probability on thousands of mortgages defaulting *together*; when house prices fell nationwide in 2007–2008 exactly that happened, and securities rated AAA lost most of their value. Felix Salmon's 2009 Wired article "Recipe for Disaster" tells the story; the technical moral is that the dependence structure is a modelling choice with consequences, and that correlation estimated in normal times says nothing about the tails.

**In ML/AI this is …** mutual-information estimation and maximisation: the InfoNCE loss of contrastive representation learning (SimCLR, CLIP) is a lower bound on $I(\text{view}_1; \text{view}_2)$, the information bottleneck frames deep learning as compressing $X$ while retaining $I(Z; Y)$, and `mutual_info_classif` ranks features by $I(X_j; Y)$ instead of correlation. Copulas appear in synthetic tabular-data generators (the Gaussian-copula synthesiser) and in risk models; normalising flows and diffusion models learn arbitrary joint distributions — arbitrary copulas — directly. And the very question "is this pair of features dependent beyond what correlation shows" is why practitioners plot the data before trusting `df.corr()`.

## Common pitfalls and misconceptions

1. **Uncorrelated means independent.** → False in general: $Y = X^2$ with symmetric $X$ has zero correlation and complete dependence. True only for *jointly Gaussian* variables. → Check scatter plots or mutual information; treat $\rho = 0$ as "no linear relationship", nothing more.
2. **The marginals determine the joint.** → Infinitely many joints share the same marginals: $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ marginals with $\rho = 0$, $\rho = 0.9$, or a $t$-copula are all different distributions. → Always ask what ties the variables together (the copula), not just how each is distributed.
3. **Normal marginals make a joint normal.** → Take $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Y = SX$ with $S = \pm 1$ an independent fair sign: both marginals are standard normal and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = 0$, yet $X + Y = 0$ with probability $\tfrac12$, so $(X, Y)$ is not jointly Gaussian and $X, Y$ are dependent ($|X| = |Y|$). → "Jointly Gaussian" means every linear combination is Gaussian; test that, not the marginals.
4. **Any symmetric matrix with ones on the diagonal is a correlation matrix.** → It must be positive semidefinite; $\rho_{12} = \rho_{13} = 0.9$, $\rho_{23} = -0.9$ is impossible. A sample covariance with $d \ge n$ is singular and its inverse does not exist. → Check eigenvalues (`np.linalg.eigvalsh`), shrink or regularise before inverting, and never hand-edit a correlation matrix.
5. **Conditioning on an extreme observation increases uncertainty.** → In a Gaussian the conditional covariance $\Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}$ does not depend on the observed value at all; only the mean moves. → Heteroscedastic data (variance that does depend on $x$) is a sign the Gaussian model is wrong, not a property of conditioning.
6. **Regressing $Y$ on $X$ and inverting the line regresses $X$ on $Y$.** → The two slopes are $\rho\sigma_Y/\sigma_X$ and $\rho\sigma_X/\sigma_Y$ with product $\rho^2 < 1$; both regress towards the mean. → Decide which variable you are predicting *before* fitting, and never read regression to the mean as a causal effect ("the strict teacher's punishment worked").
7. **If every subgroup agrees, the aggregate must agree.** → Simpson's paradox: weights $P(s \mid T)$ that differ between groups can reverse the aggregate. → Compare like with like (stratify or standardise) when the third variable is a confounder — but *not* when it is a mediator or a collider; that is a causal judgement.
8. **`np.cov` and `np.var` use the same normalisation.** → `np.cov` divides by $n - 1$ by default, `np.var` and `np.std` by $n$; `np.cov(X)` also treats *rows* as variables. → Pass `ddof` explicitly and use `np.cov(X, rowvar=False)` or `np.cov(X.T)` for an $n \times d$ data matrix.

## Real-world case studies

### Case study 1: anomaly detection on correlated sensors (manufacturing)

A chemical reactor is monitored by a temperature sensor and a pressure sensor. In normal operation both are standardised to $z$-scores and are strongly coupled, with correlation $\rho = 0.9$: hotter means higher pressure. A simple rule flags any reading more than, say, 2.8 standard units from the centre in Euclidean distance. Consider two readings on the same shift: $a = (2, 2)$ — hot and high pressure — and $b = (2, -2)$ — hot and *low* pressure. Both have Euclidean length $\sqrt8 = 2.83$, so the simple rule treats them identically. The Mahalanobis distance does not. With $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.9 \\ 0.9 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 - 0.81}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -0.9 \\ -0.9 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 5.26\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -0.9 \\ -0.9 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $\Delta^2(v) = 5.26\,(v_1^2 - 1.8 v_1 v_2 + v_2^2)$. For $a$: $5.26(4 - 7.2 + 4) = 4.21$, which under $\chi^2_2$ has tail probability $0.12$ — an ordinary hot day. For $b$: $5.26(4 + 7.2 + 4) = 80.0$, tail probability $4 \times 10^{-18}$ — physically, a hot reactor at low pressure means a leak or a failed sensor. The 99% control limit in two dimensions is $\chi^2_{2, 0.99} = 9.21$, so $a$ passes and $b$ is flagged by a huge margin. The Euclidean rule, by contrast, would either flag both (and drown operators in false alarms every warm afternoon) or neither (and miss the leak). This is Hotelling's $T^2$ control chart, the multivariate version of statistical process control, and in scikit-learn it is `EllipticEnvelope`, which fits $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ robustly and thresholds $\Delta^2$. The same geometry is why LDA whitens by $\Sigma^{-1}$ before measuring distances to class means, and why "distance" in a correlated feature space should never be Euclidean without a reason.

### Case study 2: Simpson's paradox in a model comparison (healthcare / ML evaluation)

Two teams submit sepsis-prediction models, each evaluated on 1,000 patient records drawn from two hospitals. Hospital 1 is a community hospital with mostly straightforward cases; Hospital 2 is a tertiary centre with the hardest cases. Model A was evaluated on 200 records from Hospital 1 (accuracy 95%) and 800 from Hospital 2 (70%); Model B on 800 from Hospital 1 (93%) and 200 from Hospital 2 (65%). Aggregate accuracies: A $= (190 + 560)/1000 = 75.0\%$, B $= (744 + 130)/1000 = 87.4\%$. The procurement committee picks B by twelve points. Yet A is better *in both hospitals*: 95 against 93, and 70 against 65. The confounder is case mix, $P(\text{hospital} \mid \text{model})$, which differed between the two evaluations because each team sampled its own test set. Standardising to a 50/50 mix gives A $= 82.5\%$ and B $= 79.0\%$; standardising to the committee's actual deployment population (say 70% community cases) gives A $= 87.5\%$ and B $= 84.6\%$. Either way A is the better model, and the aggregate number was measuring the test sets, not the models. The remedies are procedural: evaluate all candidates on the *same* held-out set; always report per-stratum metrics (hospital, sex, age band, device) alongside the aggregate; and when the deployment mix is known, report the standardised accuracy for it. The same arithmetic produces misleading leaderboards whenever models are compared on different benchmark subsets, and it is the reason fairness audits insist on disaggregated metrics: a model can improve the overall AUC while getting worse for every subgroup, if the subgroup composition of the evaluation data shifts.

### Case study 3: why averaging helps less than you think (ensembles and portfolios)

Suppose $n$ models make prediction errors $E_1, \dots, E_n$, each with mean zero and variance $\sigma^2$, and each pair with correlation $\rho$. Their covariance matrix is $\Sigma = \sigma^2\big[(1 - \rho)I + \rho\,\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top\big]$. The ensemble averages the predictions, so its error is $a^\top E$ with $a = \frac1n\mathbf{1}$, and Section 3 gives

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar E) = a^\top\Sigma a = \frac{1}{n^2}\big[n\sigma^2 + n(n - 1)\rho\sigma^2\big] = \sigma^2\Big(\rho + \frac{1 - \rho}{n}\Big).$$

With $\rho = 0$ the variance falls like $\sigma^2/n$, the familiar independent-average rate. With $\rho = 0.6$ — typical of ten gradient-boosted models trained on the same data with different seeds — ten models give $\sigma^2(0.6 + 0.04) = 0.64\sigma^2$, a 36% reduction, and a hundred models give $0.604\sigma^2$: the floor $\rho\sigma^2$ is reached almost immediately. With $\rho = 0.1$, ten models give $0.19\sigma^2$. This is the entire design rationale of random forests: bootstrap resampling and random feature subsets at every split make the individual trees *worse* (larger $\sigma^2$) but far less correlated (smaller $\rho$), and the second effect wins. It is also why ensembling architecturally different models (a tree model plus a neural network) helps more than ensembling seeds, and why "just train more copies" saturates. The identical formula governs portfolios. Thirty stocks with volatility 25% and average pairwise correlation 0.3 in equal weights give a portfolio variance of $0.0625(0.3 + 0.7/30) = 0.0202$, volatility 14.2%; the limit as $n \to \infty$ is $\sqrt{0.3} \times 25\% = 13.7\%$, the **systematic risk** that no amount of diversification removes. Ten stocks already achieve 15.2%, so the marginal benefit of the eleventh is tiny — the quantitative content of the finance textbook claim that "most of the diversification benefit comes from the first 10–20 stocks". In both domains the off-diagonal of $\Sigma$, not the diagonal, decides how much averaging buys you.

## Self-assessment

1. **A joint table has $p(x, y) = 0.12$, $p_X(x) = 0.3$ and $p_Y(y) = 0.4$. Can $X$ and $Y$ be independent? What is $p(y \mid x)$?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Independence would require $p(x, y) = 0.3 \times 0.4 = 0.12$, which matches — so this one cell is consistent with independence, but independence requires the factorisation in *every* cell, so you cannot conclude it from one. The conditional is $p(y \mid x) = 0.12/0.3 = 0.4 = p_Y(y)$, again consistent with (but not proof of) independence.

   </details>
2. **$X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $Y = X^2$. Compute $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)$ and explain whether $X$ and $Y$ are independent.**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[X^3] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0 - 0 = 0$ by the symmetry of the normal. They are maximally dependent — $Y$ is a function of $X$ — which shows that zero correlation only rules out *linear* association. Mutual information would be infinite here (a deterministic continuous relationship), while Spearman's correlation is also zero because the relationship is not monotone.

   </details>
3. **Why must a covariance matrix be positive semidefinite? Give the one-line proof and a $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrix that is *not* a valid covariance matrix.**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   For any $a$, $a^\top\Sigma a = \operatorname{Var}(a^\top X) \ge 0$, and a symmetric matrix whose quadratic form is non-negative has non-negative eigenvalues. $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is symmetric but has eigenvalues $3$ and $-1$ — it would imply a correlation of 2, and $\operatorname{Var}(X_1 - X_2) = 1 + 1 - 4 < 0$.

   </details>
4. **For $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, find the directions and lengths of the axes of the one-standard-deviation ellipse.**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Eigenvalues solve $(4 - \lambda)^2 = 4$, so $\lambda = 6$ and $\lambda = 2$ with eigenvectors $(1, 1)/\sqrt2$ and $(1, -1)/\sqrt2$. The ellipse's major semi-axis is $\sqrt6 = 2.45$ along the diagonal $x_1 = x_2$ and the minor semi-axis is $\sqrt2 = 1.41$ along $x_1 = -x_2$. Correlation is $2/4 = 0.5$.

   </details>
5. **In a bivariate Gaussian with $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1$ and $\rho = 0.8$, you observe $X_2 = 2$. What is the distribution of $X_1$? How much has the variance shrunk, and does the answer change if you had observed $X_2 = 0.1$?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   $X_1 \mid X_2 = 2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0.8 \times 2,\ 1 - 0.64) = \mathcal{N}(1.6, 0.36)$: the variance shrinks by a factor $1 - \rho^2 = 0.36$, i.e. by 64%. For $X_2 = 0.1$ the mean would be $0.08$ but the variance would still be $0.36$ — in a Gaussian the conditional variance never depends on the observed value.

   </details>
6. **Derive the Kalman gain from the Gaussian conditioning formula, and explain what $K \to 1$ and $K \to 0$ mean.**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   With predicted state $\mathcal{N}(\mu^-, P^-)$ and measurement $z = x + r$, $r \sim \mathcal{N}(0, r)$, the pair $(x, z)$ has covariance $\begin{pmatrix} P^- & P^- \\ P^- & P^- + r \end{pmatrix}$. Conditioning: $\mu = \mu^- + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(z - \mu^-) = \mu^- + \frac{P^-}{P^- + r}(z - \mu^-)$, so $K = P^-/(P^- + r)$. $K \to 1$ when $r \ll P^-$ (trust the precise sensor, ignore the prediction); $K \to 0$ when $r \gg P^-$ (ignore the noisy sensor, keep the prediction).

   </details>
7. **Explain the law of total variance in terms of a regression model's training error and noise floor.**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)] + \operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X])$. The second term is the variance that a perfect regressor $f^*(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]$ explains; the first is the noise around $f^*$, the irreducible error that even the perfect model incurs. A model's MSE equals this noise floor plus $\mathbb{E}[(f^*(X) - \hat f(X))^2]$, which is the part that bias and estimation variance contribute. $R^2$ is the explained fraction $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X])/\operatorname{Var}(Y)$.

   </details>
8. **A drug shows a higher recovery rate than placebo in the whole trial, but a lower rate among both men and women. Which conclusion is right, and what single feature of the trial design would have prevented the paradox?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Sex is a confounder: the drug must have been given disproportionately to the sex with the better baseline recovery, and the aggregate reflects that imbalance rather than the drug. The stratified comparison (drug worse in both sexes) is the trustworthy one, since sex cannot be an effect of the treatment. Randomised assignment makes $P(\text{sex} \mid \text{treatment}) = P(\text{sex})$ and removes the paradox by design for *every* confounder, measured or not.

   </details>
9. **A Gaussian-process posterior has variance almost equal to the prior variance at some test point. What does that tell you, and what would you do about it in Bayesian optimisation?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The test point is more than a few length-scales from every training input, so $k_*$ is nearly zero and the data say nothing about $f$ there: the posterior has reverted to the prior. In Bayesian optimisation this is where exploration happens — an acquisition function such as upper confidence bound $\mu(x) + \kappa\sigma(x)$ rewards exactly such points, trading off against exploiting regions with a high posterior mean.

   </details>
10. **Two assets have standard-normal returns tied together by a Gaussian copula with $\rho = 0.5$. What happens to the probability that both fall below their 1st percentile if you replace the Gaussian copula by a $t_3$-copula with the same $\rho$, and why does this matter?**
    <details><summary>Answer</summary>

    It rises — from about $0.13\%$ to about $0.33\%$, a factor of $2.5$, and the factor keeps growing further out in the tail (about 6 at the 0.1% level): the Gaussian copula has zero tail dependence (joint extremes become relatively rarer the further out you go), whereas the $t$-copula has positive tail dependence, so extreme losses cluster. The marginals and the correlation coefficient are nearly unchanged, so no univariate check and no correlation matrix would reveal the difference. This is the mechanism behind the mispricing of mortgage-backed securities before 2008.

    </details>

**`lesson.ipynb`** walks through the module in code: the customer joint table with marginals by `sum`, conditionals by renormalising rows and the outer-product independence test; scatter plots with correlation values including the $Y = X^2$ zero-correlation case and Spearman's repair; the sample covariance matrix built from scratch and matched to `np.cov`, the three-asset portfolio variance, a positive-semidefiniteness check and whitening; sampling a two-dimensional Gaussian with a hand-built Cholesky factor, drawing its one-, two- and 95%-ellipses from the eigendecomposition of $\Sigma$, and matching a from-scratch log-density to `scipy.stats`; Gaussian conditioning visualised as a slice through the density, with the narrower conditional checked against filtered samples; the law of total variance verified by simulation and the conditional mean shown to beat any other predictor; Simpson's paradox as a table and as a scatter plot where every cluster slopes one way and the cloud slopes the other; a one-dimensional Kalman filter tracking a noisy position with its gain converging to the steady state; Gaussian-process regression with an RBF kernel on six points with a 95% band; and a short look at Gaussian versus $t$ copulas and a mutual-information estimate that sees what correlation misses.

**`exercises.ipynb`** has twelve exercises with instant self-checks: marginals, conditionals and an independence test from a joint table; covariance and correlation from scratch, including the zero-correlation counterexample; a conceptual question on why uncorrelated is not independent; the sample covariance matrix, a PSD check and $a^\top\Sigma a$ for a portfolio; the multivariate Gaussian log-density by Cholesky, checked against SciPy; the Gaussian conditioning formulas in any dimension; a conceptual question diagnosing a wrong claim about conditional variance; Cholesky sampling with an empirical covariance check; Simpson's paradox rates and standardisation; Mahalanobis-distance anomaly detection against a $\chi^2$ threshold; Gaussian-process posterior mean and variance; and a mini-project that implements a one-dimensional Kalman filter for a noisy sensor track and compares its RMSE with the raw measurements and a moving average. `solutions.ipynb` contains reference implementations with explanations.
