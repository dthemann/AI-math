# Module 18 — Sampling, MCMC and Generative Models

> After this module you can draw samples from a distribution you can only evaluate up to a constant, judge whether those samples can be trusted, and read the loss function of a VAE or a diffusion model as the direct consequence of three or four lines of probability.

## Why this matters for ML and AI

Most of the quantities that matter in machine learning are expectations that nobody can integrate. The expected loss of a model under the data distribution, the posterior probability of a parameter given data, the marginal likelihood $p(x) = \int p(x \mid z)\,p(z)\,dz$ of a latent-variable model, the expected return of a policy: each is an integral over a space with thousands or millions of dimensions. The only general tool for such integrals is to *sample*. Draw $x_1, \dots, x_n$ from the right distribution and the average $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i f(x_i)$ converges to $\mathbb{E}[f]$ with error $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ regardless of dimension ([Module 08](../../part-3-probability/08-distributions-and-limit-theorems/README.md)). The catch is the phrase "from the right distribution". For a posterior you can usually evaluate $p(\theta)\,p(\text{data} \mid \theta)$ but not the normalising constant, and no textbook sampler covers that case directly. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), the technique behind Stan, PyMC and every Bayesian hierarchical model in epidemiology, pharmacology and finance, is the answer: a random walk designed so that, in the long run, it visits every region in proportion to the target's probability.

The same mathematics runs the other way in generative AI. A diffusion model such as Stable Diffusion or Imagen *is* a sampler: it starts from pure Gaussian noise and applies a learned version of Langevin dynamics, the "noisy gradient ascent on $\log p$" that this module derives, for a few dozen steps until an image appears. A variational autoencoder replaces sampling with optimisation, maximising a lower bound on $\log p(x)$ that falls straight out of Jensen's inequality from [Module 14](../../part-5-learning-theory/14-information-theory/README.md); its famous reparameterisation trick is the one-line sampler $x = \mu + \sigma z$ introduced in Section 2. Importance sampling, which lets you estimate an expectation under one distribution using samples from another, is the mechanism of off-policy evaluation in reinforcement learning ([Module 19](../19-markov-decision-processes-and-rl/README.md)), and rejection sampling, the simplest sampler of all, reappears as *speculative decoding* in production language-model serving.

The practical skill is quality control. A chain that has not mixed, an importance sampler with one dominant weight, a VAE whose latent space has collapsed, a diffusion model whose noise schedule leaves signal in the "pure noise" endpoint — all produce output that looks plausible and is wrong. Diagnostics such as acceptance rates, autocorrelation, effective sample size and the evidence–ELBO gap exist to catch exactly these failures, and they are as much a part of this module as the algorithms themselves.

## 1. Why sampling

**Intuition.** Suppose you want the average height of the trees in a forest. You could, in principle, measure every tree (integrate), but with ten million trees you will not. Instead you walk to a few hundred *randomly chosen* trees, measure those and average. The random choice is what makes the shortcut honest: a sample picked by convenience is biased, a sample picked by the distribution you care about is not. Sampling in ML is the same idea applied to integrals over parameter spaces, latent spaces and pixel spaces that are far too large to enumerate — and, when the distribution you sample from is a *model* rather than a forest, the samples are the product itself: a generated image, a sentence, a molecule.

**Definition.** Given a target density $p(x)$ on $\mathbb{R}^d$ and a function $f$, the **Monte Carlo estimator** of $\mu = \mathbb{E}_p[f(X)] = \int f(x)\,p(x)\,dx$ from independent draws $X_1, \dots, X_n \sim p$ is

$$\hat\mu_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(X_i), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat\mu_n] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_n) = \frac{\sigma_f^2}{n}, \quad \sigma_f^2 = \operatorname{Var}_p(f(X)).$$

The rate $\sigma_f/\sqrt{n}$ does not involve $d$; a deterministic grid with $k$ points per axis needs $k^d$ evaluations and is hopeless beyond $d \approx 5$. Three situations force sampling: (i) $p$ is known but $\int f p$ has no closed form (risk of a portfolio, expected loss of a model); (ii) $p$ is known only up to a constant, $p(x) = \tilde p(x)/Z$ with $Z = \int \tilde p$ unknown — every Bayesian posterior $p(\theta \mid D) \propto p(D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)$ is of this form; (iii) $p$ is the thing being *learned* and the sample is the output (generative modelling). The samplers of this module fall into four families: **exact transforms** of uniform random numbers (Section 2), **accept/reject and reweighting** schemes that correct samples from a convenient proposal (Sections 3–4), **Markov chain methods** that produce correlated samples whose long-run distribution is $p$ (Sections 5–7), and **learned generators** — VAEs, diffusion models, flows, GANs — that train a neural network to turn noise into samples (Sections 8–12).

**Worked example.** Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $f(x) = e^{-x^2}$. The expectation has a closed form you can check against: $\mathbb{E}[e^{-X^2}] = \int \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2 - x^2/2}\,dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{3}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt 3} \approx 0.5774$. With $n = 10{,}000$ standard-normal draws the notebook obtains $\hat\mu \approx 0.5784$. The sample standard deviation of $f(X_i)$ is about $0.34$, so the standard error is $0.34/\sqrt{10{,}000} = 0.0034$, and the estimate is within one standard error of the truth. To get one more decimal digit you need $n = 10^6$: the $1/\sqrt n$ law is indifferent to whether $f$ is a one-line formula or a ten-million-parameter network.

**Real-life example (finance / insurance).** A bank holds a portfolio of 2,000 loans whose defaults are correlated through a common economic factor. The loss distribution has no closed form, but the model is easy to *simulate*: draw the factor, draw each default given the factor, add up the losses. Repeating this 100,000 times gives the loss histogram, and the 99.9th percentile — the regulatory capital — is read off the empirical quantile. The same workflow prices path-dependent options, sizes catastrophe reinsurance and stress-tests pension funds; in every case the model is a sampler and the answer is a Monte Carlo average or quantile.

**In ML/AI this is …** the mini-batch in stochastic gradient descent (a Monte Carlo estimate of the full-data gradient); the single latent sample $z \sim q_\phi(z \mid x)$ that estimates the VAE objective; `torch.multinomial` or `rng.choice` sampling the next token from a language model's softmax; and the entire forward pass of a diffusion sampler, which is a Markov chain run for 20–1,000 steps.

## 2. Pseudo-random numbers and exact samplers

**Intuition.** Computers cannot produce randomness, only long deterministic sequences that *pass every statistical test* for uniformity on $[0, 1)$. NumPy's default generator (PCG64) has a period of $2^{128}$ and is seeded by an integer, which is why `np.random.default_rng(42)` gives reproducible "randomness" — the property that makes every hash in this course's exercises possible. Everything else is built on top of uniforms by transformation: if you can write $X$ as a deterministic function of one or more $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$, you have an *exact* sampler for $X$.

**Definition (inverse-CDF method).** Let $F$ be a CDF with quantile function $F^{-1}(u) = \min\{x : F(x) \ge u\}$. If $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$ then $X = F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$. Proof for continuous strictly increasing $F$: $P(X \le x) = P(F^{-1}(U) \le x) = P(U \le F(x)) = F(x)$, the last step because $P(U \le u) = u$. For the Exponential($\lambda$) distribution, $F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$, so solving $u = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$ gives

$$X = -\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln(1 - U) \qquad (\text{equivalently } -\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\ln U, \text{ since } 1 - U \text{ is also uniform}).$$

**Discrete sampling** is the same method on a step function: for probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_K$ with cumulative sums $c_k = \sum_{j \le k} p_j$, draw $U$ and return the smallest $k$ with $c_k \ge U$ — in NumPy, `np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(p), u)`. The interval $(c_{k-1}, c_k]$ has length exactly $p_k$, which is the whole proof.

**Box–Muller** produces two independent standard normals from two uniforms. Write a pair $(Z_1, Z_2)$ of independent standard normals in polar coordinates: the joint density $\frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}$ depends only on the radius, so the angle $\Theta$ is uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$ and is independent of $R^2 = Z_1^2 + Z_2^2$. The density of $R^2$ is found by change of variables: $P(R^2 \le r) = \int_0^{\sqrt r} \rho\,e^{-\rho^2/2}\,d\rho = 1 - e^{-r/2}$, so $R^2 \sim \text{Exponential}(1/2)$ and by the inverse-CDF method $R = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}$. Hence

$$Z_1 = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}\,\cos(2\pi U_2), \qquad Z_2 = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}\,\sin(2\pi U_2).$$

Finally, the **location–scale reparameterisation**: if $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ then $X = \mu + \sigma Z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, and in $d$ dimensions $X = \mu + L Z$ with $LL^\top = \Sigma$ (Cholesky factor) gives $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)$ ([Module 09](../../part-3-probability/09-multivariate-probability/README.md)). The point to remember for Section 8: the randomness lives in $Z$, and $X$ depends on the parameters $(\mu, \sigma)$ through a differentiable formula.

**Worked example.** Exponential with $\lambda = 2$ (mean $0.5$): $U = 0.3$ gives $X = -\frac12\ln 0.7 = 0.1783$, and $U = 0.9$ gives $X = -\frac12\ln 0.1 = 1.1513$; most uniforms land on small $X$, the few near 1 produce the long right tail. Discrete: $p = (0.2, 0.5, 0.3)$ has cumulative sums $(0.2, 0.7, 1.0)$; $U = 0.65$ falls in $(0.2, 0.7]$, so category 2 is returned, as it will be for exactly 50% of uniforms. Box–Muller with $U_1 = 0.5$, $U_2 = 0.25$: $R = \sqrt{-2\ln 0.5} = \sqrt{1.3863} = 1.1774$, $\Theta = \pi/2$, so $Z_1 = 1.1774\cos(\pi/2) = 0$ and $Z_2 = 1.1774$. Reparameterisation: with $\mu = 3$, $\sigma = 2$, the draw $Z_2 = 1.1774$ becomes $X = 3 + 2 \times 1.1774 = 5.355$.

**Real-life example (manufacturing).** A discrete-event simulation of a packaging line needs the time between machine faults, modelled as Exponential with a mean of 40 minutes, and the fault *type*, a categorical variable with probabilities (jam 0.6, sensor 0.3, motor 0.1). Both are generated from a single stream of uniforms — one inverse-CDF call for the time, one `searchsorted` for the type — which is what makes the simulation reproducible across runs when engineers compare maintenance policies with the same seed ("common random numbers" removes the sampling noise from the *difference* between policies).

**In ML/AI this is …** `rng.choice(vocab, p=softmax_probs)`, which samples the next token by the cumulative-sum method; `scipy.stats.<dist>.rvs`, which uses inverse-CDF or Box–Muller-type transforms internally; `rng.multivariate_normal`, which draws $z$ and applies a Cholesky factor; the top-$k$/nucleus filters of an LLM decoder, which zero out part of $p$ before the cumulative sum; and the reparameterisation trick $z = \mu_\phi(x) + \sigma_\phi(x) \odot \varepsilon$ in every VAE implementation.

## 3. Rejection sampling

**Intuition.** Draw a picture of the target density $p(x)$ and cover it with a taller, simpler curve $M q(x)$ you *can* sample from. Throw darts uniformly at the region under the tall curve; keep the darts that land under $p$. Kept darts are uniformly distributed under $p$, so their $x$-coordinates follow $p$ exactly. The price is the wasted darts: the fraction kept is the area of $p$ (which is 1) divided by the area under $Mq$ (which is $M$).

**Definition.** Let the target be known up to a constant, $p(x) = \tilde p(x)/Z$, and let $q$ be a **proposal** density you can sample from, with a constant $M$ such that the **envelope condition** $M q(x) \ge \tilde p(x)$ holds for all $x$. Repeat: draw $x \sim q$ and $u \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$; **accept** $x$ if

$$u \le \frac{\tilde p(x)}{M q(x)} .$$

Accepted samples have density exactly $p$. Proof: the joint density of $(x, u)$ is $q(x)$ on $u \in [0, 1]$; the acceptance region has probability $\int q(x)\,\frac{\tilde p(x)}{M q(x)}\,dx = Z/M$; the density of accepted $x$ is $q(x)\,\frac{\tilde p(x)}{Mq(x)}\big/(Z/M) = \tilde p(x)/Z = p(x)$. The **acceptance rate** is $Z/M$ ($= 1/M$ when $\tilde p$ is normalised), so the expected number of proposals per accepted sample is $M/Z$, and the best $M$ is the smallest one that still satisfies the envelope, $M = \sup_x \tilde p(x)/q(x)$.

**Why it dies in high dimension.** Take target $\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ and proposal $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_d)$ with $\sigma$ slightly above 1 (the proposal must have heavier tails or the ratio is unbounded). The best constant is $M = \sigma^d$, so the acceptance rate is $\sigma^{-d}$: with $\sigma = 1.1$ it is $0.39$ at $d = 10$ but $7 \times 10^{-5}$ at $d = 100$ — one acceptance per 14,000 proposals. Any mismatch between proposal and target is raised to the power $d$. In 1,000 dimensions no practical envelope exists, which is why rejection sampling is a building block (inside samplers for Gamma, Beta and truncated normals in `scipy.stats`) rather than a tool for posteriors.

**Worked example (Beta(2, 5) with a uniform envelope).** The target density is $p(x) = 30\,x(1 - x)^4$ on $[0, 1]$, and the proposal is $q = \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$, $q(x) = 1$. The mode solves $p'(x) = 30(1 - x)^4 - 120x(1 - x)^3 = 0$, i.e. $1 - x = 4x$, $x^* = 0.2$, and $p(0.2) = 30 \times 0.2 \times 0.8^4 = 2.4576$, so $M = 2.4576$ and the acceptance rate is $1/M = 0.407$. A proposal $x = 0.5$ with $u = 0.5$: the ratio is $p(0.5)/M = 0.9375/2.4576 = 0.381$, and $0.5 > 0.381$, so it is rejected; the same $u$ with $x = 0.15$ gives $p(0.15)/M = 2.349/2.4576 = 0.956$ and is accepted. Over 100,000 proposals the notebook accepts about 40,600 — within Monte Carlo noise of the predicted 40,690 — and their mean is $0.286$, against the exact $\mathbb{E}[X] = 2/7 = 0.2857$.

**Real-life example (NLP / LLM serving).** *Speculative decoding* speeds up text generation with rejection sampling. A small, fast draft model proposes $k$ tokens from its distribution $q$; the large target model scores all of them in a single parallel forward pass, giving $p$. Each drafted token is accepted with probability $\min(1, p/q)$, and on the first rejection a corrected token is drawn from the residual distribution $\max(0, p - q)$, normalised. The accepted sequence is distributed *exactly* as if the large model had generated it token by token — the output is identical in distribution, only faster. If the per-token acceptance probability is $\alpha$, the expected number of tokens produced per expensive forward pass is $(1 - \alpha^{k+1})/(1 - \alpha)$: with $\alpha = 0.8$ and $k = 4$, about 3.4 tokens instead of 1.

**In ML/AI this is …** the exactness argument of speculative decoding above; the internal samplers of `rng.gamma`, `rng.beta` and `scipy.stats.truncnorm`, which use tailored envelopes with acceptance rates near 1; and the negative example that motivates everything else in the module — when a one-shot envelope cannot be found, you move to importance weights (Section 4) or Markov chains (Sections 5–7).

## 4. Importance sampling

**Intuition.** Instead of throwing away proposals that land in the wrong place, keep all of them and *reweight*: a point where the target is twice as dense as the proposal counts double, a point where the proposal over-represents the target counts less. Nothing is wasted, and you may choose a proposal that deliberately over-samples the region that matters for $f$ — the tail where a component fails, the rare event you are trying to price.

**Definition.** For a target $p$ and a proposal $q$ with $q(x) > 0$ wherever $f(x)p(x) \ne 0$,

$$\mathbb{E}_p[f(X)] = \int f(x)\,\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,q(x)\,dx = \mathbb{E}_q\big[f(X)\,w(X)\big], \qquad w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)},$$

so the **importance-sampling (IS) estimator** $\hat\mu_{\text{IS}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i f(x_i)\,w(x_i)$ with $x_i \sim q$ is unbiased. Its variance is $\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Var}_q(f w)$, which can be *smaller* than the plain Monte Carlo variance when $q$ concentrates where $|f|p$ is large, and *infinite* when $q$ has lighter tails than $p$ (the ratio $p/q$ then explodes and $\int f^2 p^2/q\,dx$ diverges). The optimal proposal is $q^*(x) \propto |f(x)|\,p(x)$ — unattainable in general but the guide for designing $q$. When $p = \tilde p/Z$ is unnormalised, use the **self-normalised** estimator with $\tilde w_i = \tilde p(x_i)/q(x_i)$:

$$\hat\mu_{\text{SNIS}} = \frac{\sum_i \tilde w_i\,f(x_i)}{\sum_i \tilde w_i},$$

which is consistent but slightly biased (the bias is $O(1/n)$); the denominator $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \tilde w_i$ is itself an unbiased estimate of $Z$. The health of a set of weights is summarised by the **effective sample size**

$$\text{ESS} = \frac{\big(\sum_i w_i\big)^2}{\sum_i w_i^2} \in [1, n],$$

the number of equally weighted samples that would carry the same information. Equal weights give $n$; one dominant weight gives about 1. Always look at ESS and at the largest weight's share of the total before believing an IS estimate.

**Worked example (a tail probability).** Estimate $\mu = P(X > 4)$ for $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$; the truth is $\Phi(-4) = 3.167 \times 10^{-5}$. Plain Monte Carlo with $n = 10{,}000$ samples sees, on average, $0.3$ hits — the estimate is usually exactly zero, and its relative standard error per sample is $\sqrt{(1 - \mu)/\mu} \approx 178$. Now take the proposal $q = \mathcal{N}(4, 1)$, centred on the boundary. The weight is $w(x) = \frac{\phi(x)}{\phi(x - 4)} = e^{-x^2/2 + (x - 4)^2/2} = e^{8 - 4x}$, and the estimator averages $\mathbf{1}[x_i > 4]\,e^{8 - 4x_i}$. About half the proposals satisfy $x > 4$, and their weights are at most $e^{8 - 16} = e^{-8} = 3.4 \times 10^{-4}$. The variance per sample is $\mathbb{E}_q[\mathbf{1}[x>4]\,w^2] - \mu^2 = e^{16}\,\Phi(-8) - \mu^2 = 4.52 \times 10^{-9}$, so the relative standard error per sample is $\sqrt{4.52 \times 10^{-9}}/\mu = 2.1$ — an improvement by a factor of $178/2.1 \approx 84$ in standard error, i.e. a factor $\approx 7{,}000$ in the number of samples. With $n = 10{,}000$ the notebook obtains $\hat\mu = 3.23 \times 10^{-5} \pm 0.07 \times 10^{-5}$ (one standard error from the truth), and the ESS of the weighted indicators is about $1{,}800$, so the estimate rests on the equivalent of 1,800 clean samples instead of 0.3. A shifted exponential proposal $x = 4 + Y$, $Y \sim \text{Exponential}(4)$, does even better (ESS $\approx 9{,}900$ of 10,000), because its shape $e^{-4(x-4)}$ matches the tail of $\phi$ closely. Four weights $(0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 0.7)$ illustrate the ESS formula: $(1.0)^2/(0.01 + 0.01 + 0.01 + 0.49) = 1/0.52 = 1.92$ effective samples out of 4.

**Real-life example (engineering reliability).** A structural component fails when its load $L$ exceeds its strength $S$. If $S \sim \mathcal{N}(100, 5^2)$ and $L \sim \mathcal{N}(60, 8^2)$ (in kN), the margin $D = S - L \sim \mathcal{N}(40, 89)$ and $P(D < 0) = \Phi(-40/9.43) = 1.1 \times 10^{-5}$. Plain simulation needs about $9 \times 10^6$ runs for 10% relative accuracy; shifting the proposal for $D$ to $\mathcal{N}(0, 89)$ reduces the variance by a factor of roughly 19,000 and gets the same accuracy from under 500 runs. For a finite-element model costing minutes per evaluation, this is the difference between feasible and not. The same shift-the-proposal device prices deep out-of-the-money options and estimates packet-loss probabilities in network simulation.

**In ML/AI this is …** off-policy evaluation in RL ([Module 19](../19-markov-decision-processes-and-rl/README.md)), where the return of a target policy $\pi$ is estimated from trajectories of a behaviour policy $b$ with weights $\prod_t \pi(a_t \mid s_t)/b(a_t \mid s_t)$, and the clipped ratio $\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}$ in PPO; the importance-weighted autoencoder bound (IWAE), which tightens the ELBO of Section 8 with $K$ weighted samples; prioritised experience replay, whose non-uniform sampling must be corrected by importance weights; and the resampling step of particle filters, where a collapsing ESS triggers resampling.

## 5. Markov chains and stationary distributions

**Intuition.** A frog hops between lily pads according to fixed probabilities that depend only on where it sits now, not on how it got there. Watch it long enough and the fraction of time it spends on each pad settles down to numbers that do not depend on where it started. Those long-run fractions are the stationary distribution, and the central idea of MCMC is to *design the hopping rules* so that the long-run fractions are a target distribution of your choosing.

**Definition.** A (discrete-time, finite-state) **Markov chain** on states $\{1, \dots, K\}$ is a sequence $X_0, X_1, \dots$ with $P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i, X_{t-1}, \dots) = P_{ij}$: the future depends on the past only through the present. The **transition matrix** $P$ has non-negative entries and rows summing to 1. If the distribution of $X_t$ is the row vector $\pi^{(t)}$, then $\pi^{(t+1)} = \pi^{(t)} P$ ([Module 02](../../part-1-linear-algebra/02-matrices-and-linear-maps/README.md)). A distribution $\pi$ is **stationary** if

$$\pi = \pi P, \qquad \text{i.e. } \pi \text{ is a left eigenvector of } P \text{ with eigenvalue } 1 .$$

Such a $\pi$ always exists for a finite chain. It is unique and the chain **converges** to it from every starting point, $\pi^{(t)} \to \pi$, if the chain is **irreducible** (every state can reach every other) and **aperiodic** (the chain does not cycle with a fixed period; a single $P_{ii} > 0$ suffices). The speed of convergence is governed by the second-largest eigenvalue modulus $|\lambda_2|$ of $P$: the distance to $\pi$ shrinks roughly like $|\lambda_2|^t$, and the **mixing time** is of order $1/(1 - |\lambda_2|)$ steps ([Module 03](../../part-1-linear-algebra/03-eigenvalues-svd-and-pca/README.md)). A sufficient condition for stationarity that is much easier to check than $\pi = \pi P$ is **detailed balance**:

$$\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji} \quad \text{for all } i, j .$$

*Proof that detailed balance implies stationarity:* $(\pi P)_j = \sum_i \pi_i P_{ij} = \sum_i \pi_j P_{ji} = \pi_j \sum_i P_{ji} = \pi_j$. The chain is then called **reversible**: in equilibrium, the flow of probability from $i$ to $j$ equals the flow back. The converse fails — a chain can be stationary without detailed balance (think of a deterministic cycle) — but every MCMC sampler in this module is built by *enforcing* detailed balance with respect to the target.

**Worked example (a three-state weather chain).** States sunny (1), cloudy (2), rainy (3) with

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.2 & 0.1 \\ 0.3 & 0.4 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.3 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Solve $\pi = \pi P$ with $\pi_1 + \pi_2 + \pi_3 = 1$. The first two columns give $\pi_1 = 0.7\pi_1 + 0.3\pi_2 + 0.2\pi_3$ and $\pi_2 = 0.2\pi_1 + 0.4\pi_2 + 0.3\pi_3$, i.e. $0.3\pi_1 = 0.3\pi_2 + 0.2\pi_3$ and $0.6\pi_2 = 0.2\pi_1 + 0.3\pi_3$. From the first, $\pi_1 = \pi_2 + \frac23\pi_3$; substituting into the second, $0.6\pi_2 = 0.2\pi_2 + \frac{2}{15}\pi_3 + 0.3\pi_3$, so $0.4\pi_2 = \frac{13}{30}\pi_3$ and $\pi_2 = \frac{13}{12}\pi_3$, hence $\pi_1 = \frac{13}{12}\pi_3 + \frac{8}{12}\pi_3 = \frac{21}{12}\pi_3$. Normalising, $\pi_3(\frac{21}{12} + \frac{13}{12} + 1) = \pi_3 \cdot \frac{46}{12} = 1$, so

$$\pi = \Big(\frac{21}{46}, \frac{13}{46}, \frac{12}{46}\Big) \approx (0.4565, 0.2826, 0.2609).$$

Check one entry: $(\pi P)_1 = 0.7 \times 0.4565 + 0.3 \times 0.2826 + 0.2 \times 0.2609 = 0.3196 + 0.0848 + 0.0522 = 0.4565$. Starting from a sunny day, $\pi^{(1)} = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1)$ and $\pi^{(2)} = (0.57, 0.25, 0.18)$ already lean toward $\pi$. The eigenvalues of $P$ are $1$, $0.473$ and $0.127$, so the error decays like $0.473^t$: after 10 days, $0.473^{10} \approx 6 \times 10^{-4}$ — the forecast has forgotten today's weather. Detailed balance fails here: $\pi_1 P_{12} = 0.4565 \times 0.2 = 0.0913$ but $\pi_2 P_{21} = 0.2826 \times 0.3 = 0.0848$. The chain has a stationary distribution and converges to it, but it is not reversible — a reminder that detailed balance is sufficient, not necessary.

**Real-life example (web search and operations).** PageRank is the stationary distribution of a random surfer who follows a uniformly chosen outgoing link with probability $0.85$ and jumps to a random page otherwise; the jump makes the chain irreducible and aperiodic, so $\pi$ is unique, and $\pi_i$ is the long-run fraction of time spent on page $i$. Power iteration $\pi^{(t+1)} = \pi^{(t)} P$ is exactly how it was computed ([Module 03](../../part-1-linear-algebra/03-eigenvalues-svd-and-pca/README.md)). In a hospital, the number of occupied intensive-care beds at each hour is a Markov chain whose stationary distribution gives the long-run probability that all beds are full — the number that sizes the unit; a call centre's queue length is the same object.

**In ML/AI this is …** the invariance requirement of every MCMC algorithm (Sections 6–7); the Markov property that defines an MDP and makes Bellman's equation hold ([Module 19](../19-markov-decision-processes-and-rl/README.md)); the state-transition matrix of a hidden Markov model; and, in diffusion models, the forward noising process — a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ — and the reverse chain that undoes it (Section 10).

## 6. Metropolis–Hastings

**Intuition.** You want to explore a landscape whose height is $\tilde p(x)$ while spending time at each location in proportion to its height, but you only have an altimeter — you can measure the height where you stand and where you propose to step, and nothing else (no map, no total volume $Z$). Metropolis–Hastings is the rule: propose a nearby step; if it goes uphill, take it; if it goes downhill, take it with probability equal to the height ratio. Over time you wander everywhere, lingering on the peaks in exact proportion to their height, and the unknown constant $Z$ cancels because only *ratios* of $\tilde p$ are ever used.

**Definition (the algorithm).** Given the current state $x$, a **proposal density** $q(x' \mid x)$ and the unnormalised target $\tilde p$:

1. Draw $x' \sim q(\cdot \mid x)$.
2. Compute the **acceptance probability**
$$\alpha(x \to x') = \min\left\{1,\ \frac{\tilde p(x')\,q(x \mid x')}{\tilde p(x)\,q(x' \mid x)}\right\}.$$
3. Draw $u \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$; if $u \le \alpha$ set $x_{t+1} = x'$, otherwise $x_{t+1} = x$ (the chain *stays*, and the repeated value counts as a sample).

For a **symmetric** proposal, $q(x' \mid x) = q(x \mid x')$ — e.g. the **random-walk** proposal $x' = x + s\,\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ — the ratio reduces to $\tilde p(x')/\tilde p(x)$ (the original Metropolis algorithm). In practice compute $\log \alpha = \log\tilde p(x') - \log\tilde p(x)$ and compare it with $\log u$ to avoid under- and overflow.

**Proof that MH satisfies detailed balance.** The transition density for an *accepted* move $x \to x'$ ($x' \ne x$) is $T(x' \mid x) = q(x' \mid x)\,\alpha(x \to x')$. Suppose without loss of generality that $\tilde p(x')\,q(x \mid x') \le \tilde p(x)\,q(x' \mid x)$. Then $\alpha(x \to x') = \frac{\tilde p(x')\,q(x \mid x')}{\tilde p(x)\,q(x' \mid x)}$ and $\alpha(x' \to x) = 1$, so

$$\tilde p(x)\,T(x' \mid x) = \tilde p(x)\,q(x' \mid x)\,\frac{\tilde p(x')\,q(x \mid x')}{\tilde p(x)\,q(x' \mid x)} = \tilde p(x')\,q(x \mid x') = \tilde p(x')\,T(x \mid x').$$

That is $p(x)T(x' \mid x) = p(x')T(x \mid x')$ after dividing by $Z$ — detailed balance with respect to $p$ — and rejections (staying put) trivially balance themselves. By Section 5, $p$ is stationary for the chain; if the proposal can reach everywhere ($q > 0$), the chain is irreducible, and the positive rejection probability makes it aperiodic, so $x_t \to p$ in distribution and ergodic averages $\frac1n\sum f(x_t) \to \mathbb{E}_p f$.

**Tuning and diagnostics.** The step size $s$ of a random-walk proposal trades off two failures: too small and almost every proposal is accepted but the chain crawls (high autocorrelation); too large and almost every proposal lands in a low-density region and is rejected (the chain is stuck for long stretches). Theory for Gaussian-like targets gives an optimal acceptance rate of about $0.44$ in one dimension, falling to $0.234$ as the dimension grows, with an optimal scale $s \approx 2.38\,\sigma/\sqrt{d}$; a rate between 0.2 and 0.5 is the usual target. The initial segment before the chain reaches the typical set is **burn-in** (warm-up) and is discarded. **Thinning** — keeping every $k$-th sample — reduces storage but never improves an estimate: a thinned chain has fewer samples with a bit less correlation each, which is a net loss of information, so thin only when memory forces you to. The quality of the remaining samples is measured by the **autocorrelation function** $\rho_k = \operatorname{Corr}(x_t, x_{t+k})$, the **integrated autocorrelation time** $\tau = 1 + 2\sum_{k \ge 1}\rho_k$, and the **effective sample size** $\text{ESS} = n/\tau$: the variance of an ergodic average is $\tau\,\sigma_f^2/n = \sigma_f^2/\text{ESS}$, so $n$ correlated samples are worth $n/\tau$ independent ones. With $\rho_k = 0.8^k$, $\tau = 1 + 2 \times 0.8/0.2 = 9$, and 9,000 draws carry the information of 1,000. Running several chains from dispersed starting points and comparing the between-chain variance of their means with the within-chain variance gives the **$\hat R$** statistic (Gelman–Rubin); values above about 1.01 mean the chains have not agreed on the same distribution, which a single trace plot can never reveal.

**Worked example (posterior of a logistic-regression slope).** Five observations $x = (-2, -1, 0, 1, 2)$ with labels $y = (0, 0, 1, 1, 1)$, model $P(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma(\beta x)$ without an intercept, prior $\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2^2)$. The log posterior is $\log\tilde p(\beta) = \sum_i\big[y_i\log\sigma(\beta x_i) + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - \sigma(\beta x_i))\big] - \beta^2/8$. At $\beta = 1$: the log-likelihood is $\log(1 - \sigma(-2)) + \log(1 - \sigma(-1)) + \log\sigma(0) + \log\sigma(1) + \log\sigma(2) = -0.127 - 0.313 - 0.693 - 0.313 - 0.127 = -1.573$, the log prior is $-0.125$, total $-1.698$. At $\beta = 1.5$: log-likelihood $-1.193$, log prior $-0.281$, total $-1.474$. At $\beta = 3$: log-likelihood $-0.795$, log prior $-1.125$, total $-1.920$. Random-walk proposal from $\beta = 1$ to $\beta' = 1.5$: $\log\alpha = -1.474 + 1.698 = 0.224 > 0$, accepted with probability 1 (uphill). From $1.5$ to $3$: $\log\alpha = -1.920 + 1.474 = -0.446$, $\alpha = e^{-0.446} = 0.640$; with $u = 0.5$ the move is accepted, with $u = 0.7$ rejected and the chain records $1.5$ again. Note the role of the prior: the likelihood alone keeps increasing with $\beta$ (the data are perfectly separated, so the MLE is $+\infty$), and it is the $-\beta^2/8$ term that makes the posterior proper and the chain return from large values. In the notebook's larger version (40 points, intercept and slope, a weak $\mathcal{N}(0, 10^2)$ prior) the posterior mean of the slope lands *above* the MLE with a visibly right-skewed marginal — the likelihood is flat on the high side, so the posterior keeps mass there that a Gaussian approximation around the MLE would miss — and the posterior standard deviations play the role of the MLE's standard errors.

**Real-life example (epidemiology / healthcare).** A Bayesian hierarchical model of infection rates across 30 hospitals has one rate per hospital plus population-level parameters describing how the rates vary; the posterior has 32+ dimensions and no closed form. MCMC — MH, Gibbs or, in modern software, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo — produces draws from the joint posterior, from which analysts report each hospital's rate with a 95% credible interval, the probability that a given hospital is above the national average, and predictions for a new hospital. Hierarchical *shrinkage* (Section 7's case study) emerges automatically: a hospital with 2 infections in 50 patients is pulled toward the population rate because the posterior knows how much hospitals genuinely differ. Disease-mapping, dose-response modelling in pharmacology and the COVID-19 reproduction-number estimates reported during 2020 were all computed this way.

**In ML/AI this is …** the sampler inside PyMC, Stan and NumPyro (which default to a gradient-based variant, Section 7); Bayesian neural networks sampled by stochastic-gradient MCMC; Bayesian optimisation's hyperparameter marginalisation; and simulated annealing, which is MH on $\tilde p^{1/T}$ with a temperature $T$ decreasing toward zero so that the chain settles into the global optimum.

## 7. Gibbs sampling, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and Langevin dynamics

**Intuition.** Metropolis–Hastings proposes blindly and lets the acceptance test clean up. Three refinements use structure instead. *Gibbs sampling* updates one coordinate at a time from its exact conditional distribution, so nothing is ever rejected. *Hamiltonian Monte Carlo* gives the state a momentum and lets it roll across the landscape according to physics, so proposals can be far away yet still likely to be accepted. *Langevin dynamics* keeps only the essence: take a gradient step uphill on $\log p$ and add noise of the right size. The last idea is the one that becomes a diffusion model.

**Definition (Gibbs).** For a target $p(x_1, \dots, x_d)$ whose **full conditionals** $p(x_j \mid x_{-j})$ can be sampled, cycle through $j = 1, \dots, d$ and replace $x_j$ by a draw from $p(x_j \mid x_{-j})$ with all other coordinates held at their current values. Each such update is an MH step whose proposal is the full conditional, for which the acceptance probability is identically 1: with $q(x' \mid x) = p(x_j' \mid x_{-j})$ and $x'_{-j} = x_{-j}$, the ratio $\frac{p(x')\,q(x \mid x')}{p(x)\,q(x' \mid x)} = \frac{p(x_j' \mid x_{-j})\,p(x_{-j})\,p(x_j \mid x_{-j})}{p(x_j \mid x_{-j})\,p(x_{-j})\,p(x_j' \mid x_{-j})} = 1$. Gibbs therefore leaves $p$ invariant, but it moves along coordinate axes only, and that is its weakness: when coordinates are strongly correlated, the conditional distributions are narrow and the chain zig-zags in tiny steps along a diagonal ridge.

**Definition (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, in one paragraph).** Introduce a momentum $v \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and the Hamiltonian $H(x, v) = -\log\tilde p(x) + \frac12\|v\|^2$. Simulate Hamilton's equations $\dot x = v$, $\dot v = \nabla_x \log\tilde p(x)$ for $L$ leapfrog steps of size $\epsilon$ (so the gradient of [Module 04](../../part-2-calculus-and-optimization/04-differential-calculus/README.md) steers the proposal), then accept the endpoint with probability $\min\{1, e^{-\Delta H}\}$, which corrects the discretisation error. Because the dynamics conserve $H$ approximately, proposals that travel far across the typical set are accepted with high probability, giving effective sample sizes per gradient evaluation that random-walk MH cannot approach in more than a handful of dimensions. The No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) tunes $L$ automatically and is the default in Stan and PyMC.

**Definition (Langevin dynamics).** Drop the momentum and the accept step, keep the gradient and add noise:

$$x_{t+1} = x_t + \frac{\epsilon}{2}\,\nabla_x \log p(x_t) + \sqrt{\epsilon}\;\xi_t, \qquad \xi_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).$$

The vector field $s(x) = \nabla_x \log p(x)$ is the **score** of $p$; it does not depend on $Z$, since $\nabla\log(\tilde p/Z) = \nabla\log\tilde p$. As $\epsilon \to 0$ and the number of steps grows, the distribution of $x_t$ converges to $p$: the gradient term pulls toward high density, the noise term keeps the chain from collapsing onto the mode, and for the one-dimensional Gaussian $p = \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ with $s(x) = -x$ one can check the balance directly — the update $x_{t+1} = (1 - \epsilon/2)x_t + \sqrt\epsilon\,\xi_t$ has stationary variance $\epsilon/(1 - (1 - \epsilon/2)^2) = 1/(1 - \epsilon/4)$, which is $1.026$ at $\epsilon = 0.1$ and tends to the correct value 1. For finite $\epsilon$ the bias can be removed by adding an MH accept/reject step (the Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm, MALA), which is HMC with a single leapfrog step.

**Worked example (Gibbs on a correlated Gaussian).** Target $\mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$ with $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1\end{pmatrix}$, $\rho = 0.9$. Gaussian conditioning ([Module 09](../../part-3-probability/09-multivariate-probability/README.md)) gives $x_1 \mid x_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\rho x_2, 1 - \rho^2) = \mathcal{N}(0.9 x_2, 0.19)$ and symmetrically for $x_2 \mid x_1$. Start at $(x_1, x_2) = (2, 2)$. Update $x_1$: mean $1.8$, standard deviation $\sqrt{0.19} = 0.436$; with $\xi = 0.5$, $x_1 = 1.8 + 0.218 = 2.018$. Update $x_2$: mean $0.9 \times 2.018 = 1.816$; with $\xi = -1$, $x_2 = 1.816 - 0.436 = 1.380$. After a full sweep the chain has moved from $(2, 2)$ to $(2.018, 1.380)$ — a short step along the ridge. The lag-1 autocorrelation of $x_1$ between sweeps is $\rho^2 = 0.81$, and in general $\rho_k = \rho^{2k}$, so $\tau = (1 + \rho^2)/(1 - \rho^2) = 1.81/0.19 \approx 9.5$: 10,000 sweeps are worth about 1,050 independent draws. At $\rho = 0.99$, $\tau \approx 100$. The fix is to rotate to the principal axes ([Module 03](../../part-1-linear-algebra/03-eigenvalues-svd-and-pca/README.md)), where the coordinates are independent and Gibbs produces exact independent samples in one sweep, or to use a gradient-based sampler that moves along the ridge.

**Real-life example (computer vision / physics).** The Ising model of a magnet — binary spins on a grid that prefer to agree with their neighbours — has $2^{N}$ states and a normalising constant nobody can compute, yet the full conditional of one spin given its four neighbours is a two-outcome distribution you can sample instantly. Gibbs sampling (the "heat-bath" algorithm of statistical physics) is how its phase transition was first simulated, and the identical update rule performs image denoising when the spins are pixel labels and the local field includes the noisy observation: each pixel is resampled from "what my neighbours and my measurement say I should be". Molecular dynamics with a thermostat is Langevin dynamics on the Boltzmann distribution $p(x) \propto e^{-E(x)/kT}$, with the force $-\nabla E$ as the score.

**In ML/AI this is …** stochastic-gradient Langevin dynamics, which replaces $\nabla\log p$ by a mini-batch estimate and gives Bayesian neural networks with the cost of SGD; the "noisy gradient ascent on $\log p$" that is the sampling procedure of score-based and diffusion models (Section 10); collapsed Gibbs sampling for latent Dirichlet allocation (topic models); and HMC/NUTS as the engine of Stan, PyMC and NumPyro.

## 8. Variational inference and the ELBO

**Intuition.** When sampling a posterior is too slow — millions of data points, a model that must be fitted in seconds, a latent variable per image — give up exactness and *fit* the posterior instead. Choose a family of tractable distributions (independent Gaussians, say), and move its parameters until it is as close as possible to the true posterior. Inference becomes optimisation, and optimisation is something deep learning knows how to do at scale. The key fact is that a particular lower bound on the log evidence can be maximised *without ever knowing the evidence*.

**Definition and derivation.** Let $p(x, z) = p(x \mid z)\,p(z)$ be a model with observed $x$ and latent $z$, and let $q(z)$ be any density over $z$ (the **variational distribution**). Then, by Jensen's inequality ([Module 14](../../part-5-learning-theory/14-information-theory/README.md)), since $\log$ is concave,

$$\log p(x) = \log \int q(z)\,\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)}\,dz \;\ge\; \int q(z)\log\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)}\,dz = \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x \mid z)] - \text{KL}\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big) =: \mathcal{L}(q).$$

$\mathcal{L}$ is the **evidence lower bound (ELBO)**. The gap is exactly a KL divergence: writing $p(x, z) = p(z \mid x)\,p(x)$ inside the integral gives

$$\log p(x) - \mathcal{L}(q) = \text{KL}\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z \mid x)\big) \ge 0 .$$

Hence maximising the ELBO over $q$ is the same as minimising the KL divergence from $q$ to the true posterior, and the bound is tight exactly when $q = p(z \mid x)$. Two readings of the ELBO are useful: *expected log-likelihood minus a complexity penalty* (how well $z \sim q$ explains $x$, minus how far $q$ strays from the prior), and *evidence minus the approximation error*. Note the direction: this is $\text{KL}(q \,\|\, p)$, the **reverse KL**, which is infinite wherever $q$ puts mass that $p$ lacks, so the optimal $q$ is *mode-seeking* — it hugs one mode of a multimodal posterior and underestimates variance. The forward KL $\text{KL}(p \,\|\, q)$ would be *mass-covering* but requires expectations under $p$, which is what we cannot compute.

In **mean-field VI** the family is factorised, $q(z) = \prod_j q_j(z_j)$, and **coordinate ascent** (CAVI) updates one factor at a time by the fixed-point rule $\log q_j^*(z_j) = \mathbb{E}_{q_{-j}}[\log p(x, z)] + \text{const}$, which has a closed form for conjugate models. For general models (and all deep ones) one instead parameterises $q_\phi$ and climbs the ELBO by stochastic gradient. The gradient with respect to $\phi$ of an expectation under $q_\phi$ can be estimated in two ways. The **score-function (REINFORCE) estimator** uses $\nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[f(z)\,\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z)]$ and works for any $q$, discrete or continuous, but its variance is notoriously high. The **reparameterisation (pathwise) estimator** writes $z = g_\phi(\varepsilon)$ with $\varepsilon$ parameter-free (Section 2: $z = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon$) and uses $\nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_\varepsilon[f(g_\phi(\varepsilon))] = \mathbb{E}_\varepsilon[\nabla_z f(z)\,\nabla_\phi g_\phi(\varepsilon)]$, which exploits the gradient of $f$ and typically has far lower variance. For $f(z) = z^2$ and $q = \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1)$ at $\mu = 1$: the reparameterised gradient is $2(\mu + \varepsilon)$, with mean $2$ and variance $4$; the score-function gradient is $z^2(z - \mu)$, with the same mean $2$ but variance $\approx 30$ — roughly eight times more samples for the same precision, and the ratio gets worse as $f$ gets more complicated.

**Worked example (a conjugate toy where the evidence is known).** Model: $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, $x_i \mid z \sim \mathcal{N}(z, 1)$ for $i = 1, \dots, 4$, data $x = (0.4, 1.6, 0.9, 1.1)$ with mean $\bar x = 1.0$. The exact posterior is $z \mid x \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\frac{n\bar x}{n + 1}, \frac{1}{n + 1}\big) = \mathcal{N}(0.8, 0.2)$ ([Module 11](../../part-4-statistics/11-likelihood-and-bayesian-inference/README.md)), and the log evidence is the log density of $x$ under $\mathcal{N}(0, I + \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top)$, which evaluates to $\log p(x) = -5.2505$. Take $q = \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)$. Every term of the ELBO is a Gaussian expectation: $\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x \mid z)] = -\frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi - \frac12\sum_i (x_i - m)^2 - \frac{n}{2}s^2$, $\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(z)] = -\frac12\log 2\pi - \frac12(m^2 + s^2)$ and the entropy $\mathbb{H}(q) = \frac12\log(2\pi e s^2)$. At the exact posterior $(m, s^2) = (0.8, 0.2)$ the ELBO equals $-5.2505$ — the bound is tight. At the slightly wrong $(m, s^2) = (1.0, 0.2)$ it is $-5.3505$, a gap of $0.1000$, which matches $\text{KL}(q \,\|\, p) = \frac{(m - 0.8)^2}{2 \times 0.2} = \frac{0.04}{0.4} = 0.1$ exactly. The notebook plots the ELBO surface over $(m, s^2)$ and shows its maximum sitting at the posterior.

**Real-life example (e-commerce / recommendation).** A streaming service fits a Bayesian matrix-factorisation model to 100 million ratings: each user and each item has a latent vector with a Gaussian prior. MCMC over $10^7$ latent vectors is out of the question for a nightly retrain, so the posterior over each vector is approximated by a Gaussian $q$ and the ELBO is maximised by stochastic gradient over mini-batches of ratings. Latent Dirichlet allocation, used to organise large document collections into topics, is fitted the same way (stochastic variational inference), scaling to corpora that collapsed Gibbs sampling cannot touch.

**In ML/AI this is …** the loss function of every variational autoencoder (Section 9); `torch.distributions.kl_divergence` and `rsample()` (the "r" is reparameterisation); the objective of Bayes-by-backprop and variational dropout for Bayesian neural networks; and the log-derivative trick, which reappears as the REINFORCE policy gradient in [Module 19](../19-markov-decision-processes-and-rl/README.md).

## 9. Variational autoencoders

**Intuition.** An autoencoder squeezes an image through a narrow bottleneck and learns to reconstruct it; the bottleneck code is a compressed description. A *variational* autoencoder makes the code a probability distribution instead of a point and insists that, across the dataset, the codes look like a standard Gaussian. Those two changes turn the autoencoder into a generative model: draw $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, decode, and you get a new sample — and the training loss is nothing but the negative ELBO of Section 8 with neural networks for $q$ and $p(x \mid z)$.

**Definition.** A VAE has a **prior** $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I_k)$, a **decoder** $p_\theta(x \mid z)$ (a network mapping $z$ to the parameters of a distribution over $x$, e.g. a Gaussian mean, or Bernoulli probabilities for binary pixels) and an **encoder** $q_\phi(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}\big(\mu_\phi(x), \operatorname{diag}\sigma_\phi^2(x)\big)$ (a network mapping $x$ to a mean and a log-standard-deviation per latent dimension). The per-example loss is the negative ELBO,

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; x) = \underbrace{-\,\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{KL}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(0, I)\big)}_{\text{regulariser}},$$

the expectation estimated with a single reparameterised sample $z = \mu_\phi(x) + \sigma_\phi(x) \odot \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, so that gradients flow to $\phi$ through $z$. For a Gaussian decoder with fixed variance $\sigma_x^2$ the reconstruction term is $\frac{1}{2\sigma_x^2}\|x - \hat x(z)\|^2 + \text{const}$ — a squared error — and for a Bernoulli decoder it is binary cross-entropy. The KL term has a **closed form**. For one dimension, with $q = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $p = \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$:

$$\text{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\right] = \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[-\log\sigma - \frac{(z - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{z^2}{2}\right] = -\log\sigma - \frac12 + \frac{\mu^2 + \sigma^2}{2} = \frac12\big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\big),$$

using $\mathbb{E}_q[(z - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2$ and $\mathbb{E}_q[z^2] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$; for a diagonal Gaussian in $k$ dimensions the terms add over coordinates. It is zero exactly when $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$, and it penalises both a code that drifts from the origin and a code that becomes deterministic ($\sigma \to 0$ costs $-\log\sigma^2 \to \infty$). This is what keeps the latent space *usable for generation*: the encoder is forced to spread codes over the region the prior will sample from, and to make nearby codes decode to similar outputs. Its failure mode is **posterior collapse**: if the decoder is powerful enough to model $x$ without $z$, the cheapest solution sets $q_\phi(z \mid x) = p(z)$ for every $x$ (KL $= 0$), the code carries no information, and generation degenerates to the decoder's unconditional guess; KL annealing (weight the KL term by $\beta$ growing from 0 to 1) and free bits are the standard remedies, while $\beta > 1$ (the $\beta$-VAE) trades reconstruction for more disentangled codes.

**Worked example (2-D latent).** The encoder returns $\mu = (0.5, -1.0)$ and $\sigma = (0.8, 0.5)$ for one input. KL per dimension: $\frac12(0.25 + 0.64 - 1 - \ln 0.64) = \frac12(-0.11 + 0.4463) = 0.1681$ and $\frac12(1 + 0.25 - 1 - \ln 0.25) = \frac12(0.25 + 1.3863) = 0.8181$, total $0.9863$ nats — the second dimension, with its confident, off-centre code, pays most. Reparameterise with $\varepsilon = (1, -1)$: $z = (0.5 + 0.8, -1.0 - 0.5) = (1.3, -1.5)$. Suppose the decoder (Gaussian, $\sigma_x = 1$, two output dimensions) reconstructs with squared error $\|x - \hat x\|^2 = 1.2$; the reconstruction term is $\frac12 \times 1.2 + \log 2\pi = 0.6 + 1.8379 = 2.4379$, and the loss for this example is $2.4379 + 0.9863 = 3.4242$. Gradient flow: $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mu_1$ receives $\mu_1 = 0.5$ from the KL term plus whatever the reconstruction error sends back through $z_1 = \mu_1 + 0.8\,\varepsilon_1$ (the chain rule gives a factor 1 for $\mu$ and $\varepsilon_1 = 1$ for $\sigma_1$), which is precisely why the trick is needed — a sample drawn by `rng.normal(mu, sigma)` has no derivative with respect to `mu`. In the notebook the encoder and decoder are *linear*, $\mu(x) = Ex + e$ and $\hat x(z) = Dz + d$; trained with Adam on 400 points lying along a noisy line in the plane, the decoder direction $D$ aligns with the first principal component to within a cosine of $0.99999$, and the learned $\sigma_x = 0.397$ recovers the true noise level $0.4$. This is no accident — a linear-Gaussian VAE is probabilistic PCA, so the exact marginal likelihood of the learned decoder is computable, and the notebook verifies that the trained ELBO sits about $0.06$ nats below it.

**Real-life example (healthcare / drug discovery).** Molecules can be encoded as strings (SMILES) or graphs, and a VAE trained on a few hundred thousand known drug-like molecules learns a continuous latent space in which nearby points decode to chemically similar compounds. Chemists then optimise *in the latent space* — gradient ascent on a predicted property such as solubility, or Bayesian optimisation — and decode the result into candidate molecules, something impossible in the discrete space of molecular graphs. The KL term is what makes the interpolation meaningful: without it, decoded points between two training molecules would be garbage.

**In ML/AI this is …** `mu + torch.exp(logvar / 2) * torch.randn_like(mu)` followed by `-0.5 * torch.sum(1 + logvar - mu**2 - logvar.exp())` — the two lines in every VAE tutorial; the VQ-VAE and the latent space of Stable Diffusion (its "VAE" compresses $512 \times 512 \times 3$ pixels into a $64 \times 64 \times 4$ latent on which the diffusion model of Section 10 runs); and the ELBO as the training objective of deep latent-variable models generally.

## 10. Diffusion models: the mathematics

**Intuition.** Destroying an image is easy: add a little Gaussian noise, again and again, until nothing but static remains. A diffusion model learns to run that process backwards. Because each forward step adds only a little noise, each backward step has only a little to undo — predict the noise that was just added and subtract most of it — and a neural network can learn that small task from examples. Chained for a few hundred steps, starting from pure static, the small denoising steps compose into a sample from the data distribution. Section 7 already contains the mechanism: denoising is a step of Langevin dynamics, and the network learns the score.

**Definition (forward process).** Fix a **noise schedule** $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T \in (0, 1)$, write $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s \le t}\alpha_s$. The forward process is the Markov chain

$$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\big), \qquad \text{i.e. } x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon_t,\ \ \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).$$

Because sums of independent Gaussians are Gaussian ([Module 09](../../part-3-probability/09-multivariate-probability/README.md)), the marginal has a closed form. Two steps: $x_2 = \sqrt{\alpha_2}(\sqrt{\alpha_1}x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_1}\varepsilon_1) + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_2}\varepsilon_2$; the noise part has variance $\alpha_2(1 - \alpha_1) + (1 - \alpha_2) = 1 - \alpha_1\alpha_2$. By induction,

$$q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\big), \qquad x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\;\varepsilon, \ \ \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),$$

so any noise level can be reached in a single draw, and the signal-to-noise ratio $\bar\alpha_t/(1 - \bar\alpha_t)$ decreases monotonically to about zero at $t = T$, where $x_T \approx \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.

**Definition (reverse process and training loss).** The reverse conditional $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$ is Gaussian with a mean that is a known linear combination of $x_t$ and $x_0$ (Bayes' rule applied to two Gaussians) and variance $\tilde\beta_t = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\beta_t$. The model $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(x_t, t), \sigma_t^2 I)$ is trained to match it. Substituting $x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ into the posterior mean turns it into a function of $x_t$ and the noise $\varepsilon$ alone,

$$\tilde\mu_t(x_t, \varepsilon) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon\Big),$$

so the natural parameterisation is a network $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ that **predicts the noise**, with $\mu_\theta$ obtained by plugging $\varepsilon_\theta$ into this formula. The ELBO of the whole latent-variable model (the latents are $x_1, \dots, x_T$) is a sum of KL divergences between Gaussians with equal variances, each proportional to $\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2$; dropping the per-step weights gives the **simplified loss** of Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020),

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \text{Unif}\{1..T\},\ x_0 \sim \text{data},\ \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)}\Big[\big\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\ t\big)\big\|^2\Big].$$

Training is therefore: pick an image, a random step $t$ and a noise vector, corrupt the image in one shot with the closed-form marginal, and regress the noise. **Sampling** (ancestral sampling, DDPM) starts at $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and for $t = T, \dots, 1$ sets $x_{t-1} = \tilde\mu_t(x_t, \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)) + \sigma_t\xi$, $\xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, with $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ or $\tilde\beta_t$ and no noise at the last step. **DDIM** uses the same trained network but a deterministic, non-Markovian reverse rule that reaches comparable quality in 20–50 steps instead of 1,000.

**The score connection.** For $x_t \sim q(x_t \mid x_0)$, the score of this Gaussian is $\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t \mid x_0) = -\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0}{1 - \bar\alpha_t} = -\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}$. A noise predictor is therefore a (scaled) **score estimator**, $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \approx -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t)$, where $q_t$ is the marginal of noisy data at level $t$ (this is Tweedie's formula / denoising score matching: regressing noise with the squared loss recovers the score of the *marginal*, averaged over all $x_0$ that could have produced $x_t$). Each reverse step $x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\big(x_t + \beta_t\nabla\log q_t(x_t)\big) + \sigma_t\xi$ is then a Langevin step of Section 7 — a gradient push toward higher density plus noise — with a step size $\beta_t$ and a slowly changing target $q_t$ that starts as pure noise and ends as the data distribution. This "annealed Langevin" view is the score-based formulation of Song and Ermon, and the two families are the same model written in different coordinates.

**Worked example (three steps, one dimension).** Schedule $\beta = (0.1, 0.2, 0.3)$, so $\alpha = (0.9, 0.8, 0.7)$ and $\bar\alpha = (0.9, 0.72, 0.504)$. Start from the data point $x_0 = 2$. The marginal at $t = 3$ is $\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{0.504} \times 2,\ 0.496) = \mathcal{N}(1.420, 0.704^2)$ — the signal has shrunk by $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_3} = 0.710$ and the noise standard deviation has grown to $0.704$, SNR $\approx 1.02$. Suppose the one-shot draw gives $x_3 = 1.0$; the noise that was used is then $\varepsilon = (1.0 - 1.420)/0.704 = -0.596$, and the training target for $\varepsilon_\theta(1.0, 3)$ is exactly this number. A perfect predictor yields the reverse mean $\tilde\mu_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.7}}\big(1.0 - \frac{0.3}{0.704}(-0.596)\big) = \frac{1.254}{0.8367} = 1.499$, and the posterior formula $\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_2}\beta_3}{1 - \bar\alpha_3}x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_3}(1 - \bar\alpha_2)}{1 - \bar\alpha_3}x_3 = \frac{0.8485 \times 0.3}{0.496} \times 2 + \frac{0.8367 \times 0.28}{0.496} \times 1.0 = 1.026 + 0.472 = 1.499$ agrees, as it must. The reverse variance is $\tilde\beta_3 = \frac{0.28}{0.496} \times 0.3 = 0.169$. For a realistic schedule — $T = 1{,}000$ steps with $\beta_t$ linear from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$ — $\bar\alpha_T = 4.0 \times 10^{-5}$ (essentially pure noise), $\bar\alpha_{500} = 0.079$, $\bar\alpha_{250} = 0.52$ and $\bar\alpha_{100} = 0.90$: half of the steps are spent at SNR below $0.09$, which is why later schedules (cosine) redistribute them. The notebook's 1-D toy uses $T = 50$ on a two-mode dataset: it checks the closed-form marginal variance against simulation, samples with the *exact* score of the Gaussian-mixture marginal (which is available in closed form because a Gaussian mixture stays a Gaussian mixture under Gaussian noise), and then trains a small NumPy MLP noise predictor and samples with it; both reproduce the two modes with the right weights and widths.

**Real-life example (computer vision / audio).** Text-to-image systems such as Stable Diffusion run exactly this reverse chain in the latent space of a VAE, with $\varepsilon_\theta$ a U-Net or transformer conditioned on a text embedding ("classifier-free guidance" extrapolates between the conditional and unconditional noise predictions to sharpen adherence to the prompt). The same mathematics restores old photographs (start the reverse chain from the degraded image at an intermediate $t$ instead of from pure noise — SDEdit), fills in masked regions, synthesises speech waveforms (WaveGrad, DiffWave), designs protein backbones (RFdiffusion) and generates weather-forecast ensembles.

**In ML/AI this is …** the `scheduler.add_noise(x0, noise, t)` call (the closed-form marginal), the `F.mse_loss(noise_pred, noise)` training step and the `scheduler.step(noise_pred, t, x_t)` sampling update of the `diffusers` library; the $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$, $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ coefficients tabulated in every DDPM implementation; and the score $\nabla_x\log p$ shared with energy-based models and Langevin MCMC.

## 11. Other generative families: the maths in one paragraph each

**Intuition.** Every generative model answers the same question — how do I turn a tractable source of randomness into samples that look like data, and how do I score how well I did? — with a different trade on three axes: exact likelihood, fast sampling, and sample quality. Four families besides VAEs and diffusion models recur in practice.

**Definition (four ways to write $p_\theta(x)$).** An *autoregressive* model writes $p_\theta(x)$ as a product of learned conditionals; a *normalising flow* writes it as a base density pushed through an invertible map, with the change-of-variables Jacobian; a *GAN* never writes $p_\theta$ at all and instead defines the generator implicitly through a two-player game whose equilibrium minimises a divergence; an *energy-based model* writes $p_\theta(x) = e^{-E_\theta(x)}/Z_\theta$ with an intractable normaliser. The paragraphs below give each in formulas.

**Autoregressive models.** The chain rule of probability ([Module 07](../../part-3-probability/07-probability-foundations/README.md)) factorises any joint exactly: $p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(x_i \mid x_{<i})$. Model each conditional with a network that sees only the past (causal masking in a transformer, [Module 17](../17-mathematics-of-transformers/README.md)), train by maximum likelihood — the sum of the per-position cross-entropies — and sample one variable at a time with the discrete sampler of Section 2. Exact likelihoods and stable training come for free; the price is $n$ sequential steps to generate $n$ variables, which is why image autoregression over a million pixels is slow and language modelling over a few thousand tokens is the natural fit.

**Normalising flows.** Choose an invertible, differentiable map $f_\theta$ from data space to a base space with $z = f_\theta(x) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. The change-of-variables formula ([Module 04](../../part-2-calculus-and-optimization/04-differential-calculus/README.md), [Module 07](../../part-3-probability/07-probability-foundations/README.md)) gives the exact likelihood

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \log \mathcal{N}\big(f_\theta(x);\,0, I\big) + \log\big|\det J_{f_\theta}(x)\big|,$$

and sampling is $x = f_\theta^{-1}(z)$. The design problem is to make $\det J$ cheap: affine coupling layers (RealNVP, Glow) transform half the coordinates by a scale-and-shift computed from the other half, so the Jacobian is triangular and its log-determinant is a sum of the log-scales, $O(d)$ instead of $O(d^3)$. A flow with one layer, $x = \mu + \sigma z$, has $\log|\det J^{-1}| = -\log\sigma$ — Section 2's reparameterisation read as a density.

**Generative adversarial networks.** A generator $G$ maps noise to samples; a discriminator $D(x) \in (0, 1)$ is trained to tell data from fakes; the two play the minimax game $\min_G\max_D\ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]$. For a fixed $G$ the optimal discriminator is $D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_G(x)}$, and substituting it back shows that the generator minimises $2\,\text{JSD}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_G) - \log 4$, the Jensen–Shannon divergence of [Module 14](../../part-5-learning-theory/14-information-theory/README.md). GANs give sharp samples in one forward pass but no likelihood, and the saddle-point optimisation is prone to oscillation and **mode collapse** (the generator covers only part of the data). Wasserstein GANs replace the JSD by an earth-mover distance whose gradient does not vanish when the two distributions are disjoint.

**Energy-based models.** Define $p_\theta(x) = e^{-E_\theta(x)}/Z_\theta$ with any network as the energy $E_\theta$ and $Z_\theta = \int e^{-E_\theta}$ intractable. The maximum-likelihood gradient is $\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x) = -\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x) + \mathbb{E}_{x' \sim p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x')]$ — push the energy of data down, push the energy of model samples up — and the second term needs samples from the model, obtained by Langevin dynamics on the score $-\nabla_x E_\theta$ (Section 7). The partition function never has to be computed, only sampled around, which ties this family to MCMC on one side and, through the score, to diffusion models on the other.

**Worked example.** A two-variable autoregressive model over binary pixels with $p(x_1 = 1) = 0.3$ and $p(x_2 = 1 \mid x_1) = 0.9$ if $x_1 = 1$, $0.2$ otherwise, assigns $p(1, 1) = 0.3 \times 0.9 = 0.27$ and $p(0, 0) = 0.7 \times 0.8 = 0.56$; the four joint probabilities sum to 1 automatically. A flow $x = 2z + 1$ in one dimension has $\det J_{f} = 1/2$ for $f(x) = (x - 1)/2$, so $\log p(x) = \log\mathcal{N}((x - 1)/2; 0, 1) - \log 2$, the density of $\mathcal{N}(1, 4)$ — and at $x = 1$ this is $-0.919 - 0.693 = -1.612$, which is $\log\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}$ as it should be. For a GAN, if the generator produces a sample that the data distribution assigns density $0.3$ and the generator density $0.1$, the optimal discriminator outputs $D^* = 0.3/0.4 = 0.75$.

**Real-life example (sport / NLP).** Sports-analytics companies generate synthetic match trajectories with autoregressive models over player positions to evaluate tactics; speech-synthesis systems used flows (WaveGlow, Parallel WaveNet) to produce audio in one parallel pass after distilling a slow autoregressive teacher; face-editing tools in consumer photo apps were built on StyleGAN; and anomaly detection in manufacturing uses the exact likelihoods of a flow to flag images with low $\log p(x)$.

**In ML/AI this is …** the causal mask and next-token cross-entropy of every GPT-style model; `torch.distributions.transforms` and `TransformedDistribution` for flows; the `BCEWithLogitsLoss` pair of a GAN training loop; and contrastive divergence / persistent-chain training of EBMs.

## 12. Evaluating generative models

**Intuition.** A classifier is judged by its accuracy on held-out data; a generative model has no single number of that kind. Three questions are asked instead. *Does the model assign high probability to real held-out data?* (likelihood). *Do its samples look like real data in aggregate?* (sample-distribution distances such as FID). *Does it cover all the modes, and are all its samples realistic?* (precision and recall for distributions). Each catches failures the others miss.

**Definition.** The **held-out log-likelihood** $\frac1N\sum_i\log p_\theta(x_i)$ is available exactly for autoregressive models and flows, as a lower bound (the ELBO) for VAEs and diffusion models, and not at all for GANs. For images it is reported as **bits per dimension**, $\text{bpd} = -\frac{\log_2 p_\theta(x)}{d}$ with $d$ the number of sub-pixels; for a model with a negative log-likelihood of 6,400 nats on $32 \times 32 \times 3 = 3{,}072$ dimensions, $\text{bpd} = 6400/(3072\ln 2) = 3.01$ — the number of bits an ideal entropy coder would need per sub-pixel ([Module 14](../../part-5-learning-theory/14-information-theory/README.md)). The **Fréchet inception distance (FID)** embeds real and generated images with a pretrained network (Inception-v3, 2,048-dimensional pool features), fits a Gaussian to each cloud ($\mu_r, \Sigma_r$ and $\mu_g, \Sigma_g$) and reports the squared Wasserstein-2 distance between the two Gaussians ([Module 09](../../part-3-probability/09-multivariate-probability/README.md)),

$$\text{FID} = \|\mu_r - \mu_g\|^2 + \operatorname{tr}\big(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}\big),$$

where $(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}$ is the matrix square root. Lower is better, zero means identical Gaussian fits, and typical values range from about 2 (state of the art) to 50+ (poor). FID is biased upward for small sample sizes (always compare at the same $n$, usually 10,000 or 50,000), depends on the feature extractor, and ignores anything the Gaussian fit cannot see. **Precision and recall for distributions** address the last point: estimate the support of each distribution in feature space (e.g. the union of $k$-nearest-neighbour balls), and report the fraction of generated samples that fall inside the real support (precision — are the samples realistic?) and the fraction of real samples inside the generated support (recall — are all modes covered?). A mode-collapsed GAN has high precision and low recall; a blurry VAE the reverse.

**Worked example.** One-dimensional Gaussians $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $\mathcal{N}(1, 4)$: $\text{FID} = (0 - 1)^2 + (1 + 4 - 2\sqrt{1 \times 4}) = 1 + 1 = 2$. Two-dimensional, $\mu_r = (0, 0)$, $\Sigma_r = I$ versus $\mu_g = (1, 0.5)$, $\Sigma_g = \operatorname{diag}(4, 1)$: the mean term is $1 + 0.25 = 1.25$; the matrix square root of $\operatorname{diag}(4, 1)$ is $\operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$; the trace term is $(1 + 4 - 4) + (1 + 1 - 2) = 1$; $\text{FID} = 2.25$. Estimated from 5,000 samples per side in five dimensions, the notebook recovers a true FID of $1.50$ as $1.44$, and two independent samples from the *same* distribution give a small positive value ($\approx 0.005$, shrinking like $1/n$) rather than zero — the finite-sample bias.

**Real-life example (computer vision).** When a research group reports that a new text-to-image model "reaches FID 7.3 on COCO at 30 sampling steps", they mean: 30,000 generated images for 30,000 captions were embedded with Inception-v3, a Gaussian was fitted to the embeddings and compared with the Gaussian of the real COCO images by the formula above. Dropping from 1,000 to 30 reverse-diffusion steps and watching FID rise from 7.3 to only 8.1 is the evidence that the fast sampler is nearly as good — a comparison no human rating study could make cheaply.

**In ML/AI this is …** the `bits/dim` row in every density-model table; `torchmetrics.image.fid.FrechetInceptionDistance`; perplexity ($= 2^{\text{bits per token}}$) as the language-model analogue of bpd ([Module 14](../../part-5-learning-theory/14-information-theory/README.md)); and the ELBO reported as a *bound* on the likelihood in VAE and diffusion papers.

## Common pitfalls and misconceptions

1. **"If the acceptance rate is high, the sampler is working."** → A random-walk MH chain with a tiny step accepts almost everything and explores almost nothing; the notebook's step-0.2 chain accepts 90% of proposals, crosses between the two modes of a bimodal target only a handful of times in 10,000 steps, and has an effective sample size in the single digits. → Judge by autocorrelation, ESS and $\hat R$ across chains, and aim for an acceptance rate near 0.44 (1-D) to 0.23 (high-D).
2. **"Thinning the chain gives better samples."** → Keeping every 10th sample discards information; the ESS of the thinned chain is never higher than that of the full chain. → Thin only to save memory; report ESS of the full chain.
3. **"Importance weights fixed my proposal."** → If $q$ has lighter tails than $p$, the weights have infinite variance and a single late sample can rewrite the estimate; the estimate looks stable until it is not. → Check the ESS and the largest weight's share, and use proposals with heavier tails than the target (e.g. a Student-$t$).
4. **"Rejection sampling just needs a bigger $M$."** → A valid envelope is necessary but the acceptance rate $1/M$ falls exponentially with dimension; in 100 dimensions even a near-perfect proposal accepts one sample in thousands. → Use MCMC or variational methods beyond a few dimensions.
5. **"Detailed balance is the same as stationarity."** → Detailed balance implies stationarity, not conversely: the weather chain of Section 5 has a stationary distribution but $\pi_1 P_{12} \ne \pi_2 P_{21}$. → Use detailed balance as the design tool for samplers; verify stationarity with $\pi = \pi P$.
6. **"The ELBO gap doesn't matter; I only want samples."** → The gap *is* $\text{KL}(q \,\|\, p(z \mid x))$, and because it is the reverse KL the fitted $q$ is mode-seeking: it drops modes and underestimates uncertainty. → Compare with MCMC on a small version of the problem, or use richer $q$ families (flows) and the IWAE bound.
7. **"A VAE with KL $\approx 0$ has learned a perfect prior match."** → It has usually collapsed: the encoder ignores $x$, the code carries no information and reconstructions are blurry averages. → Monitor the KL per latent dimension, use KL annealing or free bits, and check that reconstructions depend on the input.
8. **"The diffusion model samples from $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ at $t = T$, so any schedule works."** → If $\bar\alpha_T$ is not close to 0, $x_T$ still contains signal and the model is trained on inputs that never occur at test time (the train–test mismatch of DDPM's linear schedule, $\bar\alpha_T = 4 \times 10^{-5}$ is fine, but $T = 50$ with small $\beta$ is not). → Always print $\bar\alpha_T$ and the SNR profile before training.

## Real-world case studies

### Case study 1: certifying a one-in-a-hundred-thousand failure with importance sampling

A manufacturer of load-bearing brackets must show that the probability of a bracket failing under service load is below $10^{-4}$. Strength and load are modelled as independent Gaussians, $S \sim \mathcal{N}(100, 5^2)$ kN and $L \sim \mathcal{N}(60, 8^2)$ kN, so the margin $D = S - L \sim \mathcal{N}(40, 89)$ has standard deviation $9.43$ kN and the exact failure probability is $P(D < 0) = \Phi(-4.24) = 1.12 \times 10^{-5}$ — comfortably below the limit, but the real model is a finite-element simulation with non-Gaussian material behaviour that takes 30 seconds per evaluation, so the team can afford about 5,000 runs, not 5 million. Plain Monte Carlo is hopeless: with $p = 1.1 \times 10^{-5}$ the expected number of failures in 5,000 runs is $0.06$, and the relative standard error per sample is $\sqrt{(1 - p)/p} \approx 300$, so even $9 \times 10^6$ runs would give only 10% relative precision. The engineers instead sample the margin from the shifted proposal $q = \mathcal{N}(0, 89)$ — centred on the failure boundary — and weight each run by $w = \phi_{40, 89}(d)/\phi_{0, 89}(d) = \exp\big(\frac{-(d - 40)^2 + d^2}{2 \times 89}\big)$. Half the proposals now fail, the weights of failing runs are at most $e^{-1600/178} = e^{-8.99} = 1.2 \times 10^{-4}$, and the variance per sample is $e^{1600/89}\,\Phi(-80/9.43) - p^2 = 6.0 \times 10^{-10}$, a relative standard error per sample of $2.2$ — a variance reduction by a factor of $18{,}700$. With 5,000 runs the relative standard error is $2.2/\sqrt{5000} = 3.1\%$; a check with 100,000 cheap Gaussian draws (the mini-project's machinery applied to this problem) reproduces $1.12 \times 10^{-5}$ to within about 1%, with an effective sample size of about 17,000. The certification report states the estimate, its standard error, the ESS and the largest weight share (about $10^{-3}$), which is what convinces a reviewer that no single simulation dominates the answer. In the real finite-element setting the same shift is applied to the input variables rather than to the margin, and a pilot run locates the "design point" — the most likely failure configuration — at which to centre the proposal.

### Case study 2: hospital infection rates with a hierarchical model and MCMC

A health authority monitors surgical-site infections in 30 hospitals. Hospital $j$ reports $y_j$ infections in $n_j$ operations; the raw rates $y_j/n_j$ range from 0% (a small hospital with 0 in 20) to 8% (another small hospital with 4 in 50), and naive league tables are dominated by the smallest hospitals' noise. The hierarchical model $y_j \sim \text{Binomial}(n_j, \theta_j)$, $\theta_j \sim \text{Beta}(a, b)$, with a weak prior on $(a, b)$, has 32 unknowns and no closed-form posterior because $a$ and $b$ are unknown. MCMC handles it in two moves: a Gibbs step for each $\theta_j$, whose full conditional is the conjugate $\text{Beta}(a + y_j, b + n_j - y_j)$ ([Module 11](../../part-4-statistics/11-likelihood-and-bayesian-inference/README.md)), and a random-walk MH step on $(\log a, \log b)$ whose target is the marginal likelihood of all 30 hospitals times the prior. Four chains of 10,000 iterations run in seconds; $\hat R$ below 1.01 for every parameter and ESS above 1,000 confirm mixing. The posterior for $(a, b)$ concentrates near $(4, 200)$ — a population mean rate of 2% with hospital-to-hospital spread of about 1 percentage point — and *shrinkage* follows automatically: the 2-in-50 hospital's posterior mean is $(4 + 2)/(204 + 50) = 2.4\%$, pulled from its raw 4% toward the population rate, with a 95% credible interval of roughly 0.9%–4.5%, while a 30-in-1,000 hospital stays at $(4 + 30)/(204 + 1000) = 2.8\%$ with a tight interval. The quantity the authority actually publishes — $P(\theta_j > 2 \times \text{national rate})$ for each hospital — is a simple average of indicators over the posterior draws, something no point estimate can provide. Replacing MH with HMC/NUTS (Stan) changes nothing in the model, only the efficiency; replacing MCMC with mean-field VI would give the same posterior means but visibly narrower intervals, the reverse-KL underestimate of Section 8.

### Case study 3: how many steps does a diffusion sampler need?

A team deploys a text-to-image model and must choose the number of reverse steps per image, which sets GPU cost linearly. The model was trained with $T = 1{,}000$ and a linear schedule from $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T = 0.02$, for which $\bar\alpha_t$ falls to $0.90$ at $t = 100$, $0.52$ at $t = 250$, $0.079$ at $t = 500$ and $4.0 \times 10^{-5}$ at $t = 1{,}000$. In SNR terms the last 500 steps operate below SNR $0.09$ — the image is essentially noise and the network is learning coarse layout — while the first 100 steps (SNR above 9) fix fine texture. The reverse chain spends equal time at every level, which is wasteful. DDIM sampling with $50$ evenly spaced steps uses the same $\varepsilon_\theta$ and the deterministic update, and the team measures FID on 10,000 prompts: $6.9$ at 1,000 ancestral steps, $7.1$ at 250 DDIM steps, $7.6$ at 50, $9.8$ at 20 and $21.4$ at 10 — a factor 20 cost reduction for a 10% FID increase at 50 steps, and a cliff below 20. Two more checks come from this module. First, the FID at $n = 10{,}000$ carries a finite-sample bias, so each configuration is evaluated with the same $n$ and the same real-image reference set. Second, a precision/recall analysis shows that the 10-step sampler loses *recall* (whole classes of prompts produce near-identical generic images) before it loses precision — the signature of a sampler that has not run long enough to separate modes, directly analogous to an MCMC chain that has not mixed. The deployed setting is 50 steps for the default and 25 for previews, and the SNR analysis motivates retraining with a cosine schedule that spends fewer steps at near-zero SNR.

| Name | Formula / fact |
|---|---|
| Monte Carlo | $\hat\mu = \frac1n\sum f(x_i)$, $\operatorname{sd} = \sigma_f/\sqrt n$, independent of dimension |
| Inverse CDF | $X = F^{-1}(U)$; Exponential($\lambda$): $X = -\ln(1 - U)/\lambda$ |
| Discrete sampling | $k = \min\{k : c_k \ge U\}$ with $c_k = \sum_{j \le k} p_j$ (`searchsorted(cumsum(p), u)`) |
| Box–Muller | $Z_1 = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}\cos 2\pi U_2$, $Z_2 = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}\sin 2\pi U_2$ |
| Reparameterisation | $X = \mu + \sigma Z$; $X = \mu + LZ$ with $LL^\top = \Sigma$ |
| Rejection sampling | accept $x \sim q$ if $u \le \tilde p(x)/(Mq(x))$; acceptance rate $Z/M$; $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ vs $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)$: rate $\sigma^{-d}$ |
| Importance sampling | $\mathbb{E}_p f = \mathbb{E}_q[f\,p/q]$; SNIS $\sum \tilde w_i f_i / \sum \tilde w_i$; optimal $q^* \propto \lvert f\rvert p$ |
| Effective sample size (IS) | $\text{ESS} = (\sum w_i)^2/\sum w_i^2$ |
| Stationary distribution | $\pi = \pi P$ (left eigenvector, eigenvalue 1); unique + convergence if irreducible and aperiodic |
| Detailed balance | $\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji} \Rightarrow \pi$ stationary (converse false) |
| Mixing | error $\sim \lvert\lambda_2\rvert^t$, mixing time $\sim 1/(1 - \lvert\lambda_2\rvert)$ |
| Metropolis–Hastings | $\alpha = \min\{1, \frac{\tilde p(x')q(x \mid x')}{\tilde p(x)q(x' \mid x)}\}$; symmetric $q$: $\min\{1, \tilde p(x')/\tilde p(x)\}$ |
| Tuning / ESS (MCMC) | optimal acceptance $\approx 0.44$ (1-D) → $0.234$ (high-D), $s \approx 2.38\sigma/\sqrt d$; $\tau = 1 + 2\sum_k \rho_k$, $\text{ESS} = n/\tau$ |
| Gibbs | $x_j \sim p(x_j \mid x_{-j})$, acceptance 1; 2-D Gaussian: $x_1 \mid x_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\rho x_2, 1 - \rho^2)$, $\rho_k = \rho^{2k}$ |
| Langevin | $x_{t+1} = x_t + \frac{\epsilon}{2}\nabla\log p(x_t) + \sqrt{\epsilon}\,\xi_t$ |
| HMC | $H = -\log\tilde p(x) + \frac12\lVert v\rVert^2$, leapfrog, accept with $\min\{1, e^{-\Delta H}\}$ |
| ELBO | $\log p(x) = \mathcal{L}(q) + \text{KL}(q(z)\,\Vert\,p(z \mid x))$, $\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_q\log p(x \mid z) - \text{KL}(q\,\Vert\,p(z))$ |
| Gradient estimators | score-function $\mathbb{E}_q[f\,\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi]$; pathwise $\mathbb{E}_\varepsilon[\nabla_z f\,\nabla_\phi g_\phi(\varepsilon)]$ (lower variance) |
| Gaussian KL (VAE) | $\text{KL}(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\,\Vert\,\mathcal{N}(0,1)) = \frac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2)$, summed over dimensions |
| VAE loss | $-\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)] + \text{KL}(q_\phi(z \mid x)\,\Vert\,\mathcal{N}(0, I))$, one reparameterised sample |
| Diffusion forward | $x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\varepsilon_t$; $q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0, (1 - \bar\alpha_t)I)$, $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s\le t}(1 - \beta_s)$ |
| Diffusion reverse | $\tilde\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\varepsilon_\theta\big)$, $\tilde\beta_t = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\beta_t$ |
| Diffusion loss / score | $\mathbb{E}\lVert\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\rVert^2$; $\nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t) \approx -\varepsilon_\theta/\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ |
| Autoregressive | $p(x) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_{<i})$ |
| Flow | $\log p(x) = \log p_Z(f(x)) + \log\lvert\det J_f(x)\rvert$ |
| GAN | $D^* = p_d/(p_d + p_g)$; generator minimises $2\,\text{JSD}(p_d \,\Vert\, p_g) - \log 4$ |
| EBM | $p = e^{-E}/Z$; $\nabla_\theta\log p(x) = -\nabla_\theta E(x) + \mathbb{E}_{p}[\nabla_\theta E]$ |
| FID | $\lVert\mu_r - \mu_g\rVert^2 + \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2})$ |

## Self-assessment

1. **You can evaluate $\tilde p(\theta) = p(\text{data} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)$ but not its integral. Which of the module's samplers can you run, and which cannot you run at all?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Metropolis–Hastings, Gibbs (if the conditionals are tractable), Langevin/HMC and self-normalised importance sampling only need ratios or gradients of $\tilde p$, so the constant cancels. Rejection sampling also works with an unnormalised target (the acceptance rate becomes $Z/M$). Inverse-CDF sampling needs the CDF, hence the constant, and is unavailable; plain (unnormalised) importance sampling without self-normalisation would estimate $Z\,\mathbb{E}_p f$ rather than $\mathbb{E}_p f$.

   </details>
2. **Why does rejection sampling with proposal $\mathcal{N}(0, 1.1^2 I_d)$ for target $\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$ accept about 39% of proposals at $d = 10$ but 0.007% at $d = 100$?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The smallest valid envelope constant is $M = \sup_x p(x)/q(x) = 1.1^d$ (attained at $x = 0$), so the acceptance rate is $1.1^{-d}$: $0.386$ at $d = 10$, $7.3 \times 10^{-5}$ at $d = 100$. Any per-coordinate mismatch is raised to the power of the dimension.

   </details>
3. **An importance sampler returns $\hat\mu = 0.021$ from $n = 10{,}000$ samples with ESS $= 3$. What do you conclude?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Three samples carry essentially all the weight, so the estimate is as reliable as a three-sample average and its reported standard error (computed as if from 10,000 draws) is meaningless. The proposal is badly mismatched — most likely lighter-tailed than the target. Redesign $q$ (heavier tails, centred on where $|f|p$ is large) before trusting any number.

   </details>
4. **State detailed balance and prove that it implies stationarity. Give an example of a stationary chain that does not satisfy it.**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   $\pi_i P_{ij} = \pi_j P_{ji}$ for all $i, j$. Summing over $i$: $(\pi P)_j = \sum_i \pi_i P_{ij} = \sum_i \pi_j P_{ji} = \pi_j$. A deterministic three-state cycle $1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1$ has stationary $\pi = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$ but $\pi_1 P_{12} = 1/3 \ne 0 = \pi_2 P_{21}$; the weather chain of Section 5 is a less extreme example.

   </details>
5. **Two random-walk MH chains target the same posterior. Chain A accepts 92% of proposals, chain B 25%. Chain B has ESS 1,200 from 10,000 draws; chain A has ESS 140. Which step size is larger, and which chain should you use?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Chain A has the smaller step (high acceptance, tiny moves, high autocorrelation); chain B's step is larger and near the optimal acceptance range. Use B: its 10,000 draws are worth 1,200 independent samples versus 140. Acceptance rate alone says nothing about efficiency.

   </details>
6. **For the 2-D Gaussian with correlation $\rho = 0.99$, how many Gibbs sweeps give the information of one independent sample, and what change would fix it?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The lag-$k$ autocorrelation is $\rho^{2k}$, so $\tau = (1 + \rho^2)/(1 - \rho^2) = 1.9801/0.0199 \approx 100$ sweeps per effective sample. Rotating to the principal axes makes the coordinates independent, after which one sweep gives an exact sample; alternatively, a gradient-based sampler (HMC) moves along the ridge directly.

   </details>
7. **Write the ELBO and identify the gap to $\log p(x)$. Why does maximising it tend to underestimate posterior variance?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   $\mathcal{L}(q) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x \mid z)] - \text{KL}(q(z)\,\|\,p(z))$ and $\log p(x) - \mathcal{L}(q) = \text{KL}(q(z)\,\|\,p(z \mid x))$. The gap is the reverse KL, which penalises $q$ heavily wherever $q > 0$ but the posterior is small, so the optimal $q$ avoids the posterior's tails and secondary modes — it is mode-seeking and too narrow.

   </details>
8. **An encoder outputs $\mu = 0.5$, $\sigma = 0.8$ for one latent dimension. Compute the KL term. What happens to the KL as $\sigma \to 0$, and why does that matter?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   $\frac12(0.25 + 0.64 - 1 - \ln 0.64) = \frac12(-0.11 + 0.4463) = 0.168$ nats. As $\sigma \to 0$ the term $-\log\sigma^2 \to \infty$: a deterministic code is infinitely expensive, which is how the KL forces the encoder to keep codes spread out and the latent space smooth enough to decode samples from the prior.

   </details>
9. **In a diffusion model with $\bar\alpha_t = 0.25$, a noisy sample is $x_t = 0.3$ and the network predicts $\varepsilon_\theta = -0.4$. What is the implied estimate of $x_0$, and what is the implied score at $x_t$?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   From $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\varepsilon$: $\hat x_0 = (0.3 - \sqrt{0.75}(-0.4))/0.5 = (0.3 + 0.3464)/0.5 = 1.293$. The score estimate is $-\varepsilon_\theta/\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} = 0.4/0.866 = 0.462$: the density of noisy data increases to the right of $x_t$, and the reverse step moves that way.

   </details>
10. **Explain why the reverse diffusion step is a Langevin step, and what plays the role of the step size.**
    <details><summary>Answer</summary>

    Substituting $\varepsilon_\theta = -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\nabla\log q_t(x_t)$ into the reverse mean gives $x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\big(x_t + \beta_t\nabla\log q_t(x_t)\big) + \sigma_t\xi$: a gradient step on $\log q_t$ of size $\beta_t$ plus Gaussian noise, which is exactly Langevin dynamics with $\epsilon/2 = \beta_t$ (up to the rescaling $1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$). The target $q_t$ changes slowly from noise to data, so the sampler is an annealed Langevin chain.

    </details>
