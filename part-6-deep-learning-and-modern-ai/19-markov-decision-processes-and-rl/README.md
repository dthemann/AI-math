# Module 19 — Markov Decision Processes and Reinforcement Learning

> After this module you can formalise a sequential decision problem as a Markov decision process, write down and solve its Bellman equations, choose between dynamic programming, Monte Carlo, temporal-difference and policy-gradient methods with a clear idea of what each one assumes and what it costs — and read the objective of PPO, RLHF or DPO as a few lines of mathematics you have derived yourself.

## Why this matters for ML and AI

Most of this course has been about *prediction*: given an input, output a label, a number, a distribution. Reinforcement learning is about *decisions whose consequences arrive later*. A warehouse system that orders stock today pays for it now and sells it next week; a treatment plan chosen in month one changes the patient's state in month six; a chess move only reveals its quality twenty moves on. The mathematical object that handles "act now, pay or profit later, under uncertainty" is the Markov decision process (MDP), and the single equation at its heart — Bellman's equation — says that the value of a situation equals the immediate reward plus the discounted value of where you end up. Everything in this module, from value iteration to the training loop behind a chatbot, is a way of solving or approximating that equation.

The modern relevance is hard to overstate. Deep Q-networks learned to play Atari games from pixels by fitting Bellman's equation with a convolutional network. AlphaGo and its successors combine Monte Carlo tree search — a bandit algorithm applied at every node — with learned value and policy networks. Large language models are *aligned* with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF): a reward model is fitted to human preference comparisons, and the language model is then updated with a policy-gradient method (PPO) to raise that reward while a KL penalty keeps it close to the pre-trained model. Direct preference optimisation (DPO) short-circuits that loop with a closed-form result about the optimal KL-regularised policy that you will derive in Section 9. Recommender systems, ad auctions, data-centre cooling, chip floor-planning and robot control all run on the same ideas.

Reinforcement learning also has a reputation for being fragile, and the mathematics explains why: estimates built on other estimates (bootstrapping) can diverge when combined with function approximation and off-policy data; policy-gradient estimates have a variance that grows with the horizon; a reward function that is slightly wrong is optimised *exactly* as written. Understanding the derivations is what lets you tell a genuine advance from a lucky seed — and that is the aim of this module.

## 1. Sequential decisions under uncertainty

**Intuition.** An **agent** repeatedly observes the **state** of an **environment**, chooses an **action**, receives a numerical **reward**, and finds itself in a new state. Unlike supervised learning there is no teacher saying which action was right; there is only the reward signal, and it may arrive long after the action that earned it. The agent's goal is not to maximise the next reward but the **return**: the total reward it will collect from now on. Two things make this hard. The consequences of an action are *delayed* (the credit-assignment problem), and the agent's own choices determine which states it gets to see (its data distribution is not fixed in advance).

**Definition.** At time $t$ the agent is in state $s_t$, takes action $a_t$, receives reward $r_{t+1}$ and moves to $s_{t+1}$ (the reward carries the index of the step on which it arrives, following Sutton and Barto). With a **discount factor** $\gamma \in [0, 1)$, the return from time $t$ is

$$G_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma\, r_{t+2} + \gamma^2 r_{t+3} + \cdots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k\, r_{t+k+1},$$

which satisfies the recursion $G_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma\, G_{t+1}$ — the seed of every Bellman equation. Rewards are bounded, $|r| \le R_{\max}$, so the series converges: $|G_t| \le R_{\max}/(1 - \gamma)$. The factor $\gamma$ does two jobs. It guarantees convergence for never-ending tasks, and it expresses a *preference for sooner rewards* — a reward $k$ steps away is worth $\gamma^k$ of the same reward now. Because the weights $\gamma^k$ sum to $1/(1 - \gamma)$, that quantity is the **effective horizon**: with $\gamma = 0.9$ the agent effectively looks about 10 steps ahead, with $\gamma = 0.99$ about 100. An equivalent reading: $\gamma$ is the probability that the episode *continues* at each step, so the expected episode length is $1/(1 - \gamma)$ and the discounted return is the expected undiscounted return of a randomly truncated episode. For **episodic** tasks that end in a terminal state, $\gamma = 1$ is allowed.

**Worked example.** An agent receives the rewards $1, 0, 0, 4, 2$ and the episode ends. With $\gamma = 0.5$ the return from the start is

$$G_0 = 1 + 0.5 \cdot 0 + 0.25 \cdot 0 + 0.125 \cdot 4 + 0.0625 \cdot 2 = 1 + 0.5 + 0.125 = 1.625 ,$$

while with $\gamma = 0.9$ it is $1 + 0.729 \cdot 4 + 0.6561 \cdot 2 = 1 + 2.916 + 1.312 = 5.228$. The big reward at step four is worth only $0.5$ at $\gamma = 0.5$ but $2.916$ at $\gamma = 0.9$: a short-sighted agent would barely notice it. The half-life of a reward — the delay after which it counts for half — is $\ln 2 / \ln(1/\gamma)$: about 6.6 steps at $\gamma = 0.9$ and 69 steps at $\gamma = 0.99$. At $\gamma = 0.99$ a reward 100 steps away still retains $0.99^{100} \approx 0.366$ of its value — close to $1/e$, as it must, since $(1 - \tfrac{1}{n})^n \to e^{-1}$; at $\gamma = 0.9$ the same reward retains $0.9^{100} \approx 0.00003$.

**Real-life examples.** *Inventory control* (manufacturing): the state is the stock on hand, the action the order quantity, the reward sales revenue minus ordering, holding and lost-sale costs — the case study in this module solves such a problem exactly. *Ad bidding* (e-commerce): the state is the remaining budget and the time left in the day, the action the bid, the reward the value of a click; spending the budget by noon is a bad policy even if every individual bid looked fine. *Treatment regimes* (healthcare): a dynamic treatment regime maps the patient's current measurements to the next dose; the reward is a health outcome months later, and the Markov state must summarise the relevant history. *Game playing*: Go, chess and Atari games are MDPs with deterministic or stochastic rules and a reward only at the end. *Language-model fine-tuning*: in RLHF the state is the prompt plus the tokens generated so far, the action is the next token, and the reward — delivered once the response is complete — comes from a model trained on human preferences; a KL penalty keeps the fine-tuned model close to the original (Section 9).

**In ML/AI this is …** the `gamma` argument of every RL library, the `discount` in TD-Gammon, DQN and AlphaZero, and the `(state, action, reward, next_state, done)` tuples stored in a replay buffer. The return $G_t$ is the quantity whose expectation every RL algorithm maximises, exactly as the expected loss was the quantity every supervised algorithm minimised in [Module 07](../../part-3-probability/07-probability-foundations/README.md).

## 2. Multi-armed bandits: exploration versus exploitation

**Intuition.** Strip the MDP down to a single state. You face $K$ slot machines ("arms"); pulling arm $a$ yields a random reward with unknown mean $\mu_a$. Each round you choose one arm. Pull the arm that looks best so far and you may be stuck with a mediocre arm whose first few rewards happened to be high; keep sampling every arm and you waste pulls on arms you already know are bad. The **exploration–exploitation dilemma** is the irreducible tension of learning while acting. Bandit algorithms resolve it by making exploration *principled*: explore an arm in proportion to how uncertain you still are about it.

**Definitions.** Let $\mu^* = \max_a \mu_a$ and let $\Delta_a = \mu^* - \mu_a$ be the **gap** of arm $a$. After $T$ rounds in which arm $a_t$ was pulled, the (pseudo-)**regret** is

$$R_T = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \big(\mu^* - \mu_{a_t}\big) = \sum_a \Delta_a\, N_a(T),$$

where $N_a(T)$ counts the pulls of arm $a$. Regret measures what you lost by not knowing the best arm from the start. A good algorithm has *sublinear* regret, $R_T / T \to 0$; the best achievable rate is logarithmic, $R_T = O(\log T)$ (Lai and Robbins, 1985). Write $\hat\mu_a$ for the empirical mean reward of arm $a$ and $N_a$ for its pull count.

- **ε-greedy.** With probability $1 - \varepsilon$ pull $\arg\max_a \hat\mu_a$, otherwise a uniformly random arm. Simple, but with constant $\varepsilon$ it never stops exploring bad arms: a fraction $\varepsilon/K$ of all pulls goes to each arm forever, so $R_T \approx \varepsilon\, T \cdot \frac{1}{K}\sum_a \Delta_a$ grows *linearly*. Decaying $\varepsilon_t \propto 1/t$ repairs this.
- **Optimistic initial values.** Initialise every $\hat\mu_a$ to a value above any possible reward and act greedily. Each arm's estimate falls toward its true mean as it is sampled, so unsampled arms keep looking attractive. Cheap and effective for stationary problems, useless if rewards drift.
- **UCB1** (Auer, Cesa-Bianchi and Fischer, 2002). Pull the arm with the largest **upper confidence bound**

$$\text{UCB}_a(t) = \hat\mu_a + \sqrt{\frac{2 \ln t}{N_a}} .$$

  *Derivation from Hoeffding.* For rewards in $[0, 1]$, Hoeffding's inequality ([Module 15](../../part-5-learning-theory/15-generalization-and-learning-theory/README.md)) gives $P(\mu_a \ge \hat\mu_a + \epsilon) \le e^{-2 N_a \epsilon^2}$. Choosing $\epsilon = \sqrt{2 \ln t / N_a}$ makes the right-hand side $e^{-4 \ln t} = t^{-4}$, so with probability at least $1 - t^{-4}$ the true mean lies below the index. The index is "optimism in the face of uncertainty": each arm is judged by the best it could plausibly be. An arm is pulled either because it is genuinely good or because its bonus is large — and every pull shrinks the bonus. This argument gives $R_T \le \sum_{a : \Delta_a > 0} \frac{8 \ln T}{\Delta_a} + \big(1 + \tfrac{\pi^2}{3}\big)\sum_a \Delta_a$. The constant $\sqrt 2$ is conservative; in practice a smaller exploration constant $c$ in $c\sqrt{\ln t / N_a}$ often does better, as the lesson notebook shows.
- **Thompson sampling** (Thompson, 1933). Keep a posterior over each $\mu_a$; each round *sample* one value from each posterior and pull the arm with the largest sample. For Bernoulli rewards with a $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$ prior, the posterior after $s_a$ successes and $f_a$ failures is $\text{Beta}(1 + s_a, 1 + f_a)$ ([Module 11](../../part-4-statistics/11-likelihood-and-bayesian-inference/README.md)): a one-line update. An arm is pulled with exactly the posterior probability that it is the best — *probability matching* — so exploration fades automatically as posteriors sharpen. Its regret is also $O(\log T)$ (Agrawal and Goyal, 2012), for Bernoulli rewards it matches the Lai–Robbins lower bound asymptotically (Kaufmann, Korda and Munos, 2012), and in simulations it is usually the strongest of the three.

**Worked example.** Three arms, $t = 10$ rounds played, with pull counts $N = (5, 3, 2)$ and successes $(3, 2, 1)$, so $\hat\mu = (0.600, 0.667, 0.500)$. A greedy agent would pull arm 2. UCB1's bonuses are $\sqrt{2 \ln 10 / N_a} = \sqrt{4.605 / N_a} = (0.960, 1.239, 1.517)$, giving indices $(1.560, 1.906, 2.017)$: UCB1 pulls arm **3**, the arm with the *lowest* empirical mean, because it has been tried least and might still be the best. Thompson sampling holds the posteriors $\text{Beta}(4, 3)$, $\text{Beta}(3, 2)$ and $\text{Beta}(2, 2)$, with means $0.571, 0.600, 0.500$ and standard deviations $0.175, 0.200, 0.224$; simulating the three draws many times shows that it pulls arm 1 with probability about $0.33$, arm 2 about $0.43$ and arm 3 about $0.25$ — the under-sampled arm still gets a quarter of the pulls. An ε-greedy agent with $\varepsilon = 0.1$ pulls arm 2 with probability $0.9 + 0.1/3 \approx 0.933$ and each other arm with probability $0.033$, regardless of how little it knows about them.

**Real-life examples.** *Headline testing* (e-commerce/media): a news site shows one of five headlines to each visitor and observes clicks; a bandit shifts traffic to the winner within hours instead of waiting for a fixed-horizon A/B test to end, and the regret is lost clicks. *Adaptive clinical trials* (healthcare): response-adaptive randomisation assigns more patients to the arm that is doing better. Case study 1 quantifies the trade-off: fewer patients receive the inferior treatment, but the trial's power to declare a winner drops. *Recommendation*: each candidate item is an arm and the click is the reward; because the reward of an unshown item is never observed, the exploration bonus is what gives new items a chance. **Contextual bandits** generalise all of these: before choosing, the agent sees a context $x_t$ (user features, time of day) and the arm means depend on it, $\mu_a(x) = \theta_a^\top x$ in the linear case. LinUCB replaces $\hat\mu_a$ with a ridge-regression prediction and the bonus with $\alpha\sqrt{x^\top A_a^{-1} x}$, the posterior standard deviation of that prediction. Contextual bandits are MDPs whose actions do not affect the next state — which is exactly what makes them tractable at web scale.

**In ML/AI this is …** the UCB rule inside Monte Carlo tree search (UCT and AlphaGo's PUCT select children by mean value plus an exploration bonus); Thompson sampling in Bayesian optimisation of hyperparameters and in production recommender systems; and the ε-greedy exploration schedule in DQN, which decays $\varepsilon$ from $1.0$ to $0.1$ over the first million frames.

## 3. Markov decision processes

**Intuition.** Add states back. Now an action changes not only the immediate reward but also where you will be next, so a good action is one that leads to *valuable* states. The Markov property — the future depends on the past only through the present state — is what keeps this tractable: the agent needs to remember nothing beyond $s_t$. Whether the property holds is a modelling choice. The position of all pieces is a Markov state for chess; the current frame of a video game is not (velocities are invisible), which is why DQN stacks four consecutive frames.

**Definition.** A (finite) **Markov decision process** is a tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$: a finite state set, a finite action set, **transition probabilities** $P(s' \mid s, a) = \Pr(s_{t+1} = s' \mid s_t = s, a_t = a)$ with $\sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) = 1$, an **expected reward** $R(s, a) = \mathbb{E}[r_{t+1} \mid s_t = s, a_t = a]$, and the discount $\gamma$. The **Markov property** states that $\Pr(s_{t+1}, r_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t, s_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, \dots) = \Pr(s_{t+1}, r_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$. A **policy** is a rule for acting: deterministic, $a = \pi(s)$, or stochastic, $\pi(a \mid s)$. Fixing a policy turns the MDP into a Markov chain ([Module 18](../18-sampling-mcmc-and-generative-models/README.md)) with transition matrix $P^\pi(s' \mid s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) P(s' \mid s, a)$ and rewards $R^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) R(s, a)$. A **trajectory** is a realisation $s_0, a_0, r_1, s_1, a_1, r_2, \dots$. The two central objects are the **state-value function** and the **action-value function** of a policy,

$$V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\big[G_t \mid s_t = s\big], \qquad Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\big[G_t \mid s_t = s, a_t = a\big],$$

related by $V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s)\, Q^\pi(s, a)$. $Q^\pi(s, a)$ is the value of taking $a$ now and following $\pi$ afterwards; it is what you need in order to *improve* a policy.

**Worked example (a machine that wears out).** A production machine is in one of three states — **G**ood, **W**orn, **B**roken — and each morning the operator chooses **run** or **service**.

| state $s$ | action | next state probabilities $(G, W, B)$ | expected reward $R(s, a)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| G | run | $(0.75, 0.25, 0)$ | $+10$ (full output) |
| W | run | $(0, 0.5, 0.5)$ | $+6$ (reduced output) |
| B | run | $(0, 0, 1)$ | $0$ |
| G or W | service | $(1, 0, 0)$ | $-2$ (a day of routine service) |
| B | service | $(1, 0, 0)$ | $-12$ (a repair) |

Take $\gamma = 0.8$ and the policy $\pi_0$ = "always run". Its value function must satisfy $V^{\pi_0}(s) = R^{\pi_0}(s) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P^{\pi_0}(s' \mid s)\, V^{\pi_0}(s')$ (derived in Section 4), which is three linear equations:

$$V_B = 0 + 0.8\, V_B, \qquad V_W = 6 + 0.8\,(0.5\, V_W + 0.5\, V_B), \qquad V_G = 10 + 0.8\,(0.75\, V_G + 0.25\, V_W).$$

The first gives $V_B = 0$. The second becomes $0.6\, V_W = 6$, so $V_W = 10$. The third becomes $0.4\, V_G = 10 + 0.2 \cdot 10 = 12$, so $V_G = 30$. In matrix form ([Module 02](../../part-1-linear-algebra/02-matrices-and-linear-maps/README.md)), $V = (I - \gamma P^{\pi_0})^{-1} R^{\pi_0}$ with

$$I - 0.8\, P^{\pi_0} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.4 & -0.2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.6 & -0.4 \\ 0 & 0 & 0.2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R^{\pi_0} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ 6 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad V^{\pi_0} = \begin{pmatrix} 30 \\ 10 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Now the action values under $\pi_0$, $Q^{\pi_0}(s, a) = R(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) V^{\pi_0}(s')$: servicing from any state leads to G, worth $30$, so $Q^{\pi_0}(G, \text{service}) = -2 + 0.8 \cdot 30 = 22 < 30$, $Q^{\pi_0}(W, \text{service}) = 22 > 10$ and $Q^{\pi_0}(B, \text{service}) = -12 + 24 = 12 > 0$. Running is right in G, but in W and B servicing beats the current policy — the seed of policy improvement in Section 5.

**Real-life example (healthcare).** A dynamic treatment regime for a chronic disease has states built from the latest lab values and treatment history, actions such as "continue", "increase dose" or "switch drug", and a reward that penalises symptoms and side effects. The Markov property is the modelling crux: if the response to a drug depends on what the patient took last year, last year's treatment must be part of the state or the "MDP" is misspecified and every value estimate inherits the error. Statisticians fit such regimes from observational registries with exactly the $Q$-function machinery of this module (Q-learning for dynamic treatment regimes), with the added difficulty that the data were collected under doctors' policies, not the one being evaluated — the off-policy problem of Sections 6 and 9.

**In ML/AI this is …** the `env.step(action) -> (next_state, reward, done)` interface of every RL environment; the frame stacking in DQN that manufactures a Markov state; and the $Q(s, a)$ table or network whose argmax is the agent's decision. In an LLM the state is the prompt plus the generated prefix, the action is the next token, $P$ is deterministic (append the token), and the policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ *is* the language model's softmax output ([Module 17](../17-mathematics-of-transformers/README.md)).

## 4. Bellman equations and the contraction argument

**Intuition.** The return decomposes as "first reward plus discounted rest", $G_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1}$. Taking expectations turns this one-line identity into a system of equations that the value function must satisfy: the value of a state is the expected immediate reward plus $\gamma$ times the expected value of the next state. For a fixed policy that system is linear and can be solved outright. For the *best* policy a maximum enters and the system becomes nonlinear — but applying its right-hand side repeatedly still converges, because each application shrinks errors by a factor $\gamma$.

**Definition and derivation (Bellman expectation equation).** Condition on $s_t = s$ and follow $\pi$:

$$V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[r_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1} \mid s_t = s] = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s)\Big[R(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\, \mathbb{E}_\pi[G_{t+1} \mid s_{t+1} = s']\Big],$$

and by the Markov property the inner expectation is $V^\pi(s')$ — the future from $s'$ does not care how we got there. Hence

$$V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s)\Big[R(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\, V^\pi(s')\Big], \qquad Q^\pi(s, a) = R(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \sum_{a'} \pi(a' \mid s')\, Q^\pi(s', a').$$

Stacking the states into vectors gives the **matrix form** $V^\pi = R^\pi + \gamma P^\pi V^\pi$, so

$$V^\pi = (I - \gamma P^\pi)^{-1} R^\pi .$$

The inverse exists because $P^\pi$ is a stochastic matrix: its eigenvalues have modulus at most 1 ([Module 03](../../part-1-linear-algebra/03-eigenvalues-svd-and-pca/README.md)), so those of $\gamma P^\pi$ have modulus at most $\gamma < 1$, and $I - \gamma P^\pi$ has no zero eigenvalue. The Neumann series $(I - \gamma P^\pi)^{-1} = \sum_{k \ge 0} \gamma^k (P^\pi)^k$ is the return written out: reward now, plus $\gamma$ times the reward one step on, and so forth.

**Definition (Bellman optimality).** Define $V^*(s) = \max_\pi V^\pi(s)$ and $Q^*(s, a) = \max_\pi Q^\pi(s, a)$. For a finite discounted MDP there exists an optimal policy that is **deterministic and stationary** and that is simultaneously optimal in every state — a statement whose proof hinges on the policy improvement theorem of Section 5. The optimal values satisfy the **Bellman optimality equations**

$$V^*(s) = \max_a \Big[R(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\, V^*(s')\Big], \qquad Q^*(s, a) = R(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\, \max_{a'} Q^*(s', a'),$$

and the greedy policy $\pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a Q^*(s, a)$ is optimal. The maximum makes the system nonlinear, so there is no inverse to compute; instead define the **Bellman optimality operator** $(TV)(s) = \max_a [R(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) V(s')]$, so that $V^*$ is the fixed point $TV^* = V^*$.

**The operator is a $\gamma$-contraction (four lines).** For any two value vectors $U, V$ and any state $s$, using $|\max_a f(a) - \max_a g(a)| \le \max_a |f(a) - g(a)|$:

$$|(TU)(s) - (TV)(s)| \le \max_a \Big|\gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\,\big(U(s') - V(s')\big)\Big| \le \gamma \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\, \|U - V\|_\infty = \gamma\, \|U - V\|_\infty .$$

So $\|TU - TV\|_\infty \le \gamma \|U - V\|_\infty$. By the **Banach fixed-point theorem**, a contraction on a complete space has exactly one fixed point, and iterating $V_{k+1} = TV_k$ from *any* start converges to it with $\|V_k - V^*\|_\infty \le \gamma^k \|V_0 - V^*\|_\infty$. That is **value iteration**, and the same argument (without the max) shows that iterative policy evaluation $V \leftarrow R^\pi + \gamma P^\pi V$ converges to $V^\pi$. A useful by-product: if two successive iterates differ by at most $\epsilon$ then $\|V_{k+1} - V^*\|_\infty \le \epsilon\gamma/(1 - \gamma)$, which turns a stopping rule into an error guarantee.

**Worked example.** Run value iteration on the machine MDP from $V_0 = (0, 0, 0)$. The first application gives $V_1 = \max_a R(s, a) = (10, 6, 0)$. The second: in G, run gives $10 + 0.8\,(0.75 \cdot 10 + 0.25 \cdot 6) = 17.2$ and service gives $-2 + 0.8 \cdot 10 = 6$, so $V_2(G) = 17.2$; in W, run gives $6 + 0.8 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 6 = 8.4$ and service $6$, so $V_2(W) = 8.4$; in B, run gives $0$ and service $-12 + 8 = -4$, so $V_2(B) = 0$. The third: $V_3 = (22, 11.76, 1.76)$ — and in W and B servicing has now overtaken running. The optimal values turn out to be $V^* = (40, 30, 20)$ (Section 5), so the errors $\|V_k - V^*\|_\infty$ are $40, 30, 22.8, 18.24, 14.45, \dots$, each at most $0.8$ times the previous one, as the contraction promises; the ratio settles at exactly $\gamma = 0.8$ once the greedy actions stop changing. Reaching an accuracy of $10^{-6}$ takes about $\ln(4 \times 10^7)/\ln(1.25) \approx 79$ iterations.

**Real-life example (finance).** The Bellman expectation equation is a pricing formula. A bond issuer migrates between credit ratings A and B or defaults (D) with yearly transition probabilities

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.8 & 0.2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{rows and columns ordered A, B, D}),$$

pays a coupon of 5 per year while not in default and nothing afterwards; cash flows are discounted at $\gamma = 0.9$. Then $V_D = 0$, $V_B = 5 + 0.9 \cdot 0.8\, V_B$ gives $V_B = 5/0.28 = 17.86$, and $V_A = 5 + 0.9\,(0.9 V_A + 0.1 \cdot 17.86)$ gives $V_A = (5 + 1.607)/0.19 = 34.77$. A risk-free perpetuity of 5 would be worth $5/(1 - 0.9) = 50$; the gap, about 15 for an A-rated bond and 32 for a B-rated one, is the price of default risk, computed with nothing but a linear solve.

**In ML/AI this is …** the TD target $r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a'; \theta^-)$ in the DQN loss — the right-hand side of the Bellman optimality equation with a network in place of the table; the `np.linalg.solve(np.eye(n) - gamma * P, R)` call that evaluates a policy exactly in the lesson notebook; and the contraction constant $\gamma$ that dictates how many sweeps value iteration needs and, through $1/(1 - \gamma)$, how far errors in a learned $Q$ can propagate.

## 5. Dynamic programming: policy iteration and value iteration

**Intuition.** If you know the model $(P, R)$ you can plan without ever acting. Two loops do the job. *Policy iteration* alternates between asking "how good is my current policy?" (evaluation) and "where would a different action do better?" (improvement) until nothing changes. *Value iteration* skips exact evaluation and simply applies the optimality operator over and over; the policy is read off at the end. Both rely on the same fact: acting greedily with respect to an accurate value function can only make the policy better.

**Definitions and statements.**

- **Iterative policy evaluation.** Repeat $V \leftarrow R^\pi + \gamma P^\pi V$ until the largest change is below a tolerance; or solve the linear system exactly in $O(|\mathcal{S}|^3)$.
- **Policy improvement theorem.** Let $\pi'$ be greedy with respect to $Q^\pi$: $\pi'(s) = \arg\max_a Q^\pi(s, a)$. Then $V^{\pi'}(s) \ge V^\pi(s)$ for every $s$, with strict inequality somewhere unless $\pi$ is already optimal. *Sketch:* by construction $Q^\pi(s, \pi'(s)) \ge V^\pi(s)$, i.e. switching to $\pi'$ for *one* step and then following $\pi$ is no worse. Unroll: $V^\pi(s) \le Q^\pi(s, \pi'(s)) = \mathbb{E}[r_{t+1} + \gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1}) \mid \pi'] \le \mathbb{E}[r_{t+1} + \gamma Q^\pi(s_{t+1}, \pi'(s_{t+1})) \mid \pi'] \le \dots \le V^{\pi'}(s)$, applying the same inequality at each step and letting the number of steps grow.
- **Policy iteration.** Evaluate $\pi_k$ exactly, improve greedily to $\pi_{k+1}$, stop when $\pi_{k+1} = \pi_k$. Each iteration strictly improves the policy (or stops), there are only $|\mathcal{A}|^{|\mathcal{S}|}$ deterministic policies, so it terminates — in practice in a handful of iterations. Cost per iteration: a linear solve, $O(|\mathcal{S}|^3)$, plus an improvement step, $O(|\mathcal{S}|^2 |\mathcal{A}|)$.
- **Value iteration.** Repeat $V \leftarrow TV$; cost $O(|\mathcal{S}|^2 |\mathcal{A}|)$ per sweep, and about $\ln(1/\epsilon)/\ln(1/\gamma)$ sweeps for accuracy $\epsilon$, which grows like $1/(1 - \gamma)$ as $\gamma \to 1$. Cheaper per iteration than policy iteration, but many more iterations when $\gamma$ is close to 1. These sweep counts are a worst case, attained by continuing tasks such as the machine MDP. In an *episodic* task where the policy soon reaches a terminal state, errors are carried forward only along paths that have not yet ended: the asymptotic factor per sweep is $\gamma\rho$, with $\rho < 1$ the spectral radius of the policy's transition matrix among the non-terminal states, and the lesson's gridworld needs only 36 sweeps at $\gamma = 0.99$ ($\rho \approx 0.44$) instead of the roughly 2,000 the worst case allows. *Modified policy iteration* — a few evaluation sweeps instead of an exact solve — sits between the two.
- The **curse of dimensionality**: a gridworld has a few dozen states, chess about $10^{44}$, a robot with continuous joints infinitely many. Tabular dynamic programming is the conceptual template; everything from Section 6 on is about doing without the table or the model.

**Worked example (policy iteration on the machine MDP).** Start from $\pi_0$ = always run, with $V^{\pi_0} = (30, 10, 0)$ and the action values $Q^{\pi_0}$ computed in Section 3. The greedy policy is $\pi_1 = (\text{run}, \text{service}, \text{service})$. Evaluate it: $V_W = -2 + 0.8\, V_G$, $V_B = -12 + 0.8\, V_G$ and $V_G = 10 + 0.8\,(0.75\, V_G + 0.25\, V_W) = 10 + 0.6\, V_G + 0.2\,(-2 + 0.8\, V_G) = 9.6 + 0.76\, V_G$, so $V_G = 9.6/0.24 = 40$, $V_W = 30$, $V_B = 20$. Improve again: $Q^{\pi_1}(G, \text{run}) = 10 + 0.8\,(0.75 \cdot 40 + 0.25 \cdot 30) = 40$ versus $Q^{\pi_1}(G, \text{service}) = -2 + 32 = 30$; $Q^{\pi_1}(W, \text{run}) = 6 + 0.8\,(0.5 \cdot 30 + 0.5 \cdot 20) = 26$ versus $30$ for service; $Q^{\pi_1}(B, \text{run}) = 0 + 0.8 \cdot 20 = 16$ versus $20$. The greedy policy is unchanged, so $\pi_1$ is optimal and $V^* = (40, 30, 20)$: servicing a worn machine costs 2 today and is worth $30 - 26 = 4$ in discounted value. Policy iteration needed two evaluations; value iteration needs about 80 sweeps for six-decimal accuracy but each sweep is three maximisations. The lesson notebook repeats this comparison on a $5 \times 5$ gridworld with walls, a pit and slippery moves: policy iteration converges in 4 iterations, value iteration in about 40 sweeps, and the two agree to machine precision.

**Real-life examples.** *Optimal stopping* (finance/e-commerce): you must sell an item within three days; each day's offer is 8, 10 or 12 with equal probability and an offer not taken is lost. On day 3 you accept whatever comes, $V_3 = 10$. On day 2 you accept an offer $p$ if $p \ge V_3$: $V_2 = \mathbb{E}[\max(p, 10)] = (10 + 10 + 12)/3 = 10.67$. On day 1, $V_1 = \mathbb{E}[\max(p, 10.67)] = (10.67 + 10.67 + 12)/3 = 11.11$. The optimal policy — accept only 12 on days 1 and 2, anything on day 3 — is worth 11.11 against 10 for selling immediately; the same backward induction prices American options and sets reservation prices in job search. *Maintenance scheduling* (manufacturing): the machine MDP is a toy version of the condition-based maintenance problems solved for wind turbines, aircraft engines and rail tracks, where states come from sensor-derived health indices and the optimal policy is typically a *threshold rule* — service once the health index drops below a level — just as our policy services at "worn".

**In ML/AI this is …** the planning backbone of model-based RL: AlphaZero's search is approximate value iteration over a tree, and Dyna-style agents run value-iteration-like updates on a learned model. Policy improvement is also the logic of *generalised policy iteration*, the pattern behind actor–critic methods (Section 8): the critic evaluates, the actor improves, and the two chase each other toward the fixed point.

## 6. Learning from experience without a model

**Intuition.** Usually nobody hands you $P$ and $R$; you only get to act and observe. Two ideas replace the model. **Monte Carlo**: run an episode to the end, record the actual return, and average such returns for each state — unbiased but noisy, and only available after the episode finishes. **Temporal difference (TD)**: after a single step, move the value of $s$ toward $r + \gamma V(s')$, using your *current estimate* of the next state's value as a stand-in for the rest of the return. This is **bootstrapping** — learning a guess from a guess — and it is the idea that makes online, incremental RL possible.

**Definitions.**

- **First-visit Monte Carlo prediction.** For each episode and each state $s$ visited, take the return $G$ following the *first* visit to $s$ and update $V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha\,(G - V(s))$. With $\alpha = 1/N(s)$ this is the running average of i.i.d. unbiased samples, so $V(s) \to V^\pi(s)$ by the law of large numbers ([Module 08](../../part-3-probability/08-distributions-and-limit-theorems/README.md)). The variance of $G$ is the variance of a sum over the whole remaining episode.
- **TD(0).** After each transition $(s, r, s')$,

$$V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha\, \underbrace{\big[r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)\big]}_{\delta,\ \text{the TD error}} .$$

  The target $r + \gamma V(s')$ is *biased* while $V(s')$ is wrong, but its variance involves only one reward and one transition. In the language of [Module 13](../../part-5-learning-theory/13-linear-models-and-regularization/README.md): MC is high-variance and unbiased, TD is lower-variance and biased, and TD usually wins on finite data because its bias shrinks as $V$ improves. TD also exploits the Markov structure — it converges to the value function of the maximum-likelihood Markov model of the data — and works in continuing tasks where episodes never end.
- **SARSA** (on-policy control). Act with an exploratory policy (ε-greedy in $Q$) and update toward the action you *actually* take next: $Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha\,[r + \gamma Q(s', a') - Q(s, a)]$. It learns $Q$ of the policy being followed, exploration included.
- **Q-learning** (off-policy control; Watkins, 1989). Update toward the *best* next action regardless of what you do: $Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha\,[r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') - Q(s, a)]$. It learns $Q^*$ directly while following any sufficiently exploratory behaviour policy; the sample target is a stochastic version of the Bellman optimality operator.
- **Convergence.** Tabular TD(0), SARSA and Q-learning converge with probability one if every state (or state–action pair) is visited infinitely often and the step sizes satisfy the **Robbins–Monro conditions** $\sum_t \alpha_t = \infty$ and $\sum_t \alpha_t^2 < \infty$ (for instance $\alpha_t = 1/t$ or $1/t^{0.6}$, not a constant); SARSA additionally needs the behaviour policy to become greedy in the limit. A constant $\alpha$ keeps tracking in non-stationary problems at the price of never settling, exactly as a constant learning rate in SGD ([Module 05](../../part-2-calculus-and-optimization/05-optimization/README.md)).

**Worked example.** In the machine MDP the agent's current estimates are $V(G) = 35$ and $V(W) = 28$. It runs the machine in G, earns $r = 10$ and lands in W. With $\gamma = 0.8$ and $\alpha = 0.1$ the TD target is $10 + 0.8 \cdot 28 = 32.4$, the TD error is $\delta = 32.4 - 35 = -2.6$, and $V(G) \leftarrow 35 - 0.26 = 34.74$: the transition was slightly disappointing relative to the current belief, and the belief moves a tenth of the way toward it. For control, suppose $Q(W, \text{run}) = 20$, the agent runs a worn machine, gets $r = 6$, lands in B where its current estimates are $Q(B, \text{run}) = 14$ and $Q(B, \text{service}) = 18$, and uses $\alpha = 0.5$. **Q-learning** uses the maximum: target $6 + 0.8 \cdot 18 = 20.4$, $\delta = 0.4$, $Q(W, \text{run}) \leftarrow 20.2$. **SARSA** uses the action the ε-greedy policy actually picks next; if that exploratory action was "run" ($14$), the target is $6 + 0.8 \cdot 14 = 17.2$, $\delta = -2.8$, and $Q(W, \text{run}) \leftarrow 18.6$. The same experience moves the two estimates in opposite directions: Q-learning credits the state with the best continuation, SARSA with the continuation the exploring agent will really experience. A Monte Carlo estimate of $V^{\pi^*}(G) = 40$ from 5,000 simulated episodes in the lesson notebook gives $40.02 \pm 0.08$; the standard deviation of a single return is about 5.4, so one episode alone is a poor estimate — the variance that TD trades away.

**Real-life example (sport).** Win-probability models in basketball or football are TD learners in disguise. Before each possession the model holds an estimate $V(s)$ of the home team's chance of winning from the current score, time and possession; after the possession it sees the new state and nudges the old estimate toward the new one. The TD error is the "swing" shown on broadcast charts, and a big error flags a decisive play. The classic computational success is Tesauro's TD-Gammon (1992), which learned backgammon at near-championship level by applying TD(λ) to self-play with a small neural network — the first demonstration that bootstrapping from a learned value function works at scale. In neuroscience, the firing of dopamine neurons tracks the TD error $\delta$ remarkably closely (Schultz, Dayan and Montague, 1997).

**In ML/AI this is …** the `loss = (r + gamma * Q_target(s').max() - Q(s, a))**2` of DQN, which is TD(0)'s Q-learning update written as a regression; the cliff-walking demonstration in the lesson notebook, where Q-learning learns the shortest, riskiest path while SARSA — accounting for its own exploration — takes the safe route and earns more during training; and the $\lambda$-returns of TD(λ) and GAE that interpolate between the MC and TD extremes.

## 7. Function approximation and the deadly triad

**Intuition.** A table with one entry per state is impossible when states are images, sensor vectors or token sequences. Replace the table by a parametrised function $\hat V(s; w)$ and learn $w$ by (semi-)gradient descent on the TD error. Generalisation now comes for free — similar states share parameters — but so does a new danger: an update that improves one state's estimate changes every other state's estimate too, and the bootstrapped target moves under your feet.

**Definitions.** With features $\phi(s) \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the **linear value function** is $\hat V(s; w) = w^\top \phi(s)$. If the true $V^\pi$ were known we would minimise the weighted squared error $\sum_s d^\pi(s)\,(V^\pi(s) - w^\top\phi(s))^2$ under the on-policy state distribution $d^\pi$, with gradient $-2\,(V^\pi(s) - \hat V(s; w))\,\phi(s)$. **Semi-gradient TD(0)** replaces $V^\pi(s)$ by the bootstrapped target and ignores the target's own dependence on $w$:

$$w \leftarrow w + \alpha\,\big[r + \gamma\, w^\top\phi(s') - w^\top \phi(s)\big]\,\phi(s).$$

"Semi" because a true gradient of the squared TD error would also differentiate through $w^\top\phi(s')$; doing so gives the residual-gradient method, which converges to a different, usually worse, solution. Semi-gradient TD(0) with linear features and *on-policy* data converges (Tsitsiklis and Van Roy, 1997) to the **TD fixed point** $w_{TD}$, the solution of $A w = b$ with $A = \mathbb{E}[\phi(s)(\phi(s) - \gamma\phi(s'))^\top]$, $b = \mathbb{E}[r\,\phi(s)]$, whose error is within a factor $1/(1 - \gamma)$ of the best possible approximation in the $d^\pi$-weighted norm. For a tabular (one-hot) $\phi$ it reduces to TD(0). The **deadly triad** (Sutton and Barto) is the combination of (i) **bootstrapping**, (ii) **off-policy** data, where the update distribution is not $d^\pi$, and (iii) **function approximation**. Any two are safe; all three together can diverge, as Baird's 1995 counterexample shows with a seven-state MDP and a *linear* approximator whose weights grow without bound. The mathematical reason: the projected Bellman operator $\Pi T$ is a contraction in the $d^\pi$-weighted norm but need not be in any other norm, and off-policy updates use a different weighting.

Deep RL lives inside the triad, and two devices keep it stable. A **target network** $\theta^-$, a frozen copy of the parameters refreshed only every so often (every 10,000 updates in the original DQN), makes the regression target $r + \gamma\max_{a'} Q(s', a'; \theta^-)$ a fixed function for a while, so each phase of training is ordinary supervised regression toward a fixed Bellman backup rather than a chase after a moving target. An **experience replay buffer** stores transitions and samples minibatches uniformly from the past, which breaks the strong temporal correlation between consecutive samples (SGD's convergence analysis assumes roughly independent samples, [Module 05](../../part-2-calculus-and-optimization/05-optimization/README.md)) and spreads each update's distribution over many past policies instead of the current one alone. Neither device removes the triad; both reduce the speed at which the three ingredients can feed on each other.

**Worked example.** A two-feature approximator has $w = (0.5, 0.1)$, $\phi(s) = (1, 2)$ and $\phi(s') = (1, 3)$; the transition gives $r = 0$ with $\gamma = 1$ and $\alpha = 0.1$. Then $\hat V(s) = 0.5 + 0.2 = 0.7$, $\hat V(s') = 0.5 + 0.3 = 0.8$, the TD error is $\delta = 0 + 0.8 - 0.7 = 0.1$, and $w \leftarrow (0.5, 0.1) + 0.1 \cdot 0.1 \cdot (1, 2) = (0.51, 0.12)$. The new estimate of $s$ is $0.75$ — but $\hat V(s')$ has also moved, to $0.87$, because the two states share both features. On the 20-state random walk of the lesson notebook ($\gamma = 1$), a cubic polynomial in the normalised position contains the true, linear value function in its span, so its TD fixed point is exact; after 2,000 episodes it is within a root-mean-square error of $0.08$, the rest being slow progress along the nearly collinear features $x^2$ and $x^3$. Coarse aggregation into four blocks of five states cannot represent the slope — and TD does not even find the best staircase: the best approximation (the $d^\pi$-weighted projection of $V^\pi$) has error $0.14$, the TD fixed point $0.41$, and the learned weights stall there ($0.42$). For state aggregation the TD fixed point is the exact value function of an *aggregated* chain whose block-to-block transitions are averaged over the states in each block; in it, a walker in the leftmost block exits with probability $0.03$ per step instead of $0.5$ from state 0, so its values are pulled toward the middle. The tabular one-hot features reach $0.04$. The representation — and, with bootstrapping, the TD fixed point — bound the achievable accuracy before any learning happens.

**Real-life example (physics/engineering).** Controlling a chemical reactor or a building's heating system means acting on continuous temperatures, pressures and flow rates; there is no table to fill in. Classical approaches use radial-basis or tile-coding features, so that nearby states share parameters and a few hundred transitions from one operating region inform the whole neighbourhood; modern ones use neural networks. The same trade-off governs both: richer features reduce approximation error but increase variance and the risk of instability, and the control engineer's safeguard — never update the controller on data from a very different regime without re-weighting — is the deadly triad in operational clothing.

**In ML/AI this is …** the DQN story in three sentences. Mnih et al. (2015) trained one convolutional network per game to predict $Q(s, \cdot)$ from the last four $84 \times 84$ frames, with a target network and a replay buffer of a million transitions, and ε-greedy exploration decaying from 1 to 0.1. The same architecture and hyperparameters reached the paper's "human-level" threshold — at least 75% of a professional tester's score — on 29 of 49 Atari games, after about 200 million emulator frames per game, some 38 days of continuous play. The mathematical content was Section 6's Q-learning update made into a regression loss, and the engineering content was taming the deadly triad.

## 8. Policy gradient methods

**Intuition.** Value-based methods learn $Q$ and act greedily. Policy-based methods skip the detour and adjust the policy's parameters directly in the direction that increases expected return. The obstacle is that the return is an expectation over actions *sampled* from the policy, so the parameters influence the objective through a probability distribution, not through a differentiable function of the action. The **log-derivative trick** removes the obstacle: the gradient of an expectation can be written as an expectation of (return × gradient of a log-probability), which a sample average can estimate. Raise the log-probability of actions that led to high returns and lower it for actions that led to low ones — weighted by how much better or worse than expected they were.

**Definitions and derivations.** Let $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ be a differentiable stochastic policy — for discrete actions typically a softmax over scores, $\pi_\theta(a \mid s) = e^{h_\theta(s, a)}/\sum_b e^{h_\theta(s, b)}$; for continuous actions a Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(s), \sigma^2)$ with $\nabla_\theta \log \pi = \frac{(a - \mu_\theta(s))}{\sigma^2}\nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s)$. The objective is $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}[G(\tau)]$, the expected return of a trajectory.

*The log-derivative (score-function) trick.* For any random variable $x \sim p_\theta$ and function $f$ that does not depend on $\theta$,

$$\nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\theta}[f(x)] = \nabla_\theta \sum_x p_\theta(x) f(x) = \sum_x f(x)\, \nabla_\theta p_\theta(x) = \sum_x p_\theta(x)\, f(x)\, \frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\theta}\big[f(x)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)\big].$$

*Applied to trajectories.* The probability of a trajectory is $p_\theta(\tau) = \rho(s_0)\prod_t \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$. Taking the log turns the product into a sum, and the environment terms do not depend on $\theta$, so $\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau) = \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ — *the model is not needed*. Hence

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\Big[\sum_{t} \gamma^t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\; G_t\Big],$$

where only the rewards from step $t$ on multiply the step-$t$ score: rewards earned before $a_t$ cannot depend on it, so their contribution has expectation zero (the same cancellation as for baselines below), and the remaining ones, $\sum_{k \ge t}\gamma^k r_{k+1}$, are exactly $\gamma^t G_t$. Many practical implementations drop the factor $\gamma^t$ and weight every step's score by $G_t$ alone — a biased but common simplification that weights all time steps equally (it is exact only for $\gamma = 1$; the lesson notebook and the exercises use it and say so). This is the **policy gradient theorem** in its sample form; its textbook statement (Sutton, McAllester, Singh and Mansour, 2000), $\nabla_\theta J = \sum_s d^{\pi_\theta}(s) \sum_a \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, Q^{\pi_\theta}(s, a)$, replaces $G_t$ by its expectation $Q^{\pi_\theta}(s_t, a_t)$ and the weighted sum over time by the discounted state-visitation distribution $d^{\pi_\theta}(s) = \sum_t \gamma^t \Pr(s_t = s)$, which is where the $\gamma^t$ lives; the proof hinges on unrolling $\nabla V^\pi(s) = \sum_a [\nabla\pi(a \mid s)\, Q^\pi(s, a) + \pi(a \mid s)\, \gamma\sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\, \nabla V^\pi(s')]$ and noticing that the gradient of the dynamics never appears.

*REINFORCE* (Williams, 1992) is stochastic gradient ascent on this estimator: sample an episode, compute the returns $G_t$, and update $\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \sum_t \gamma^t G_t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ (or, in the common simplified form, without the $\gamma^t$). For the softmax policy the score has the familiar form $\nabla_h \log \pi(a) = \mathbf{e}_a - \pi$, the "one-hot minus probabilities" of the softmax–cross-entropy gradient ([Module 16](../16-neural-networks-from-scratch/README.md)).

*Baselines do not bias the estimator (proof).* Subtract any function $b(s)$ of the state from the return. The extra term has expectation zero because

$$\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)}\big[b(s)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\big] = b(s) \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)} = b(s)\, \nabla_\theta \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = b(s)\, \nabla_\theta 1 = 0 .$$

The variance, however, changes: $\operatorname{Var}[(G - b)\nabla\log\pi]$ is a quadratic in $b$ minimised near the expected return, so a good baseline is an estimate of $V^\pi(s)$, and $G_t - V^\pi(s_t)$ estimates the **advantage** $A^\pi(s, a) = Q^\pi(s, a) - V^\pi(s)$: how much better the action was than the policy's average. **Actor–critic** methods learn the baseline, a *critic* $\hat V(s; w)$ by TD, alongside the *actor* $\pi_\theta$, and replace $G_t$ by the one-step advantage estimate $r + \gamma \hat V(s') - \hat V(s)$ — the TD error — trading REINFORCE's variance for TD's bias. **Generalised advantage estimation** (Schulman et al., 2016) interpolates between the two with a parameter $\lambda$, exactly as TD(λ) does for prediction.

**Worked example.** A three-armed bandit has deterministic rewards $\mu = (1, 2, 3)$ and a softmax policy with scores $\theta = (0, 0, 0)$, so $\pi = (\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)$ and $J = 2$. The exact gradient is $\nabla_\theta J = \sum_a \mu_a \nabla_\theta \pi_a = \pi \odot (\mu - J) = (-\tfrac13, 0, \tfrac13)$: push the score of arm 3 up, arm 1 down, leave arm 2 alone. The REINFORCE estimate from a single pull $a$ is $(\mu_a - b)(\mathbf{e}_a - \pi)$. Its mean is the exact gradient for every $b$, but its total variance (summed over the three coordinates) is $\tfrac{2}{9}\sum_a (\mu_a - b)^2 - \tfrac{2}{9}$: with $b = 0$ it is $26/9 \approx 2.89$, with $b = 1$ or $b = 3$ it is $8/9 \approx 0.89$, and with $b = J = 2$ it is $2/9 \approx 0.22$ — thirteen times smaller than without a baseline, at identical bias. A single update step shows the mechanics: with two arms, $\theta = (0, 0)$, step size 0.5, arm 1 is pulled and earns $r = 1$; the score is $\mathbf{e}_1 - \pi = (0.5, -0.5)$, so $\theta \leftarrow (0.25, -0.25)$ and $\pi_1$ rises to $0.622$. Next, arm 2 is pulled and earns $r = 0$. Without a baseline the update is zero — "nothing happened". With baseline $b = 0.5$ the advantage is $-0.5$, the score is $\mathbf{e}_2 - \pi = (-0.622, 0.622)$, and $\theta \leftarrow (0.25, -0.25) + 0.5 \cdot (-0.5) \cdot (-0.622, 0.622) = (0.406, -0.406)$, so $\pi_1$ rises further to $0.692$: a zero reward where $0.5$ was expected is *bad news about arm 2*, and the baseline is what lets the estimator say so.

**Real-life example (physics/engineering).** A robot arm learning to place parts on a conveyor uses a Gaussian policy over joint torques, $a \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(s), \sigma^2 I)$. The score function $(a - \mu_\theta(s))/\sigma^2 \cdot \nabla_\theta\mu_\theta(s)$ says: if a torque perturbation above the mean led to a better-than-expected placement, shift the mean torque in that direction. This is finite-difference optimisation done with random perturbations and no model of the arm's dynamics — which is why policy gradients were the first methods to work on real robots — and also why they need many trials: the variance of the estimator grows with the dimension of the action and the length of the episode, which is where baselines and critics earn their keep.

**In ML/AI this is …** `loss = -(log_prob * advantage).mean()` in every policy-gradient implementation, the gradient of which is the negative REINFORCE estimator in its common $\gamma^t$-free form, with an advantage estimate in place of the return; the token-level log-probabilities of a language model summed over a response to form $\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ in RLHF; and the gradient of the softmax policy, $\mathbf{e}_a - \pi$, which is the same expression as the cross-entropy gradient of a classifier with the sampled action as the "label" and the advantage as the sample weight.

## 9. Modern connections: PPO, RLHF, DPO, planning and offline RL

**Intuition.** The methods that train today's largest systems are small modifications of Sections 6–8: an importance ratio so that one batch of experience can be reused for several gradient steps, a clipping rule to stop the policy from changing too fast, and a KL penalty that keeps a fine-tuned model close to a trusted reference. Each has a precise mathematical form, and one of them — the optimal KL-regularised policy — has a closed form that turns an RL problem into a classification problem.

**Definitions and statements.** Each of the following paragraphs states one objective precisely; the worked numbers follow in the DPO paragraph and in the clipping example.

*PPO: importance ratios and clipping.* A policy-gradient update is only valid for data sampled from the current policy, so after one update the batch is stale. Importance sampling ([Module 18](../18-sampling-mcmc-and-generative-models/README.md)) repairs this: with $\rho_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)$, the surrogate $\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{\text{old}}}[\rho_t(\theta)\, \hat A_t]$ has, at $\theta = \theta_{\text{old}}$, the same gradient as the policy-gradient estimator of Section 8 (in the $\gamma^t$-free form that practical implementations use), because $\nabla_\theta \rho_t = \rho_t\, \nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ and $\rho_t = 1$ there. Maximising it naively drives $\rho_t$ to extreme values and the policy off a cliff; proximal policy optimisation (Schulman et al., 2017) instead maximises

$$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\min\big(\rho_t \hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,\, 1 - \epsilon,\, 1 + \epsilon)\, \hat A_t\big)\Big], \qquad \epsilon \approx 0.2 .$$

With $\hat A = +2$ and $\rho = 1.3$, the two terms are $2.6$ and $2.4$ and the minimum, $2.4$, is constant in $\rho$: the gradient vanishes and the optimiser stops increasing the probability of an already-boosted action. With $\hat A = -2$ and $\rho = 1.3$, the terms are $-2.6$ and $-2.4$ and the minimum is the *unclipped* $-2.6$, which still has a gradient — the probability of a bad action that has grown is pushed back down. The clip only ever removes the incentive to move further *in the direction the advantage suggests*; it is a pessimistic lower bound on the surrogate, and that asymmetry is the whole trick.

*KL-regularised RL and RLHF.* Fine-tuning a language model $\pi_\theta$ by maximising a learned reward $r(x, y)$ alone destroys it: the reward model is only accurate near the data it was trained on, and an unconstrained optimiser finds its blind spots (Section 10). RLHF therefore maximises

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\big[r(x, y)\big] - \beta\, \mathbb{E}_x\big[\operatorname{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big)\big],$$

with $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ the supervised fine-tuned model and $\beta$ a temperature-like coefficient (KL divergence from [Module 14](../../part-5-learning-theory/14-information-theory/README.md)). The reward model itself is fitted to human comparisons with the **Bradley–Terry** model, $P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big)$ — logistic regression on reward differences ([Module 13](../../part-5-learning-theory/13-linear-models-and-regularization/README.md)): a reward gap of 1 means the preferred response wins 73% of the time, a gap of 3 about 95%. The policy is then optimised with PPO, treating $r(x, y) - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$ as the per-response reward. This is the pipeline of InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022).

*DPO's closed form (derivation).* Fix a prompt $x$ and ask which distribution $\pi$ over responses maximises $\mathbb{E}_\pi[r(y)] - \beta\operatorname{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$. Write the objective as

$$\sum_y \pi(y)\Big[r(y) - \beta\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}\Big] = -\beta\sum_y \pi(y)\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\,e^{r(y)/\beta}} = -\beta\,\operatorname{KL}\big(\pi\,\|\,\pi^*\big) + \beta\log Z, \qquad \pi^*(y) = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\,e^{r(y)/\beta}}{Z}, \quad Z = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\,e^{r(y)/\beta}.$$

KL divergence is non-negative and zero only at $\pi = \pi^*$, so the optimum is $\pi^*$ — the reference policy *re-weighted by a Boltzmann factor* of the reward — and the optimal value is $\beta\log Z$. Inverting, $r(y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} + \beta\log Z$. Substituting into the Bradley–Terry likelihood, the $\log Z$ terms cancel in the difference, and the reward disappears entirely:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)}\Big)\Big].$$

Direct preference optimisation (Rafailov et al., 2023) trains the language model on preference pairs with this loss — a logistic regression whose "logit" is a scaled log-likelihood-ratio — with no reward model, no sampling and no PPO.

**Worked example (DPO's closed form on four responses).** Take $\pi_{\text{ref}} = (0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1)$, rewards $r = (1, 2, 0, 3)$ and $\beta = 1$. The unnormalised weights $\pi_{\text{ref}}\, e^{r}$ are $(0.4 e, 0.3 e^2, 0.2, 0.1 e^3) = (1.087, 2.217, 0.200, 2.009)$, so $Z = 5.513$ and $\pi^* = (0.197, 0.402, 0.036, 0.364)$. Its objective is $\mathbb{E}_{\pi^*}[r] - \operatorname{KL}(\pi^* \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = 2.095 - 0.388 = 1.707 = \log 5.513$, against $1.3$ for the reference; the lesson notebook confirms by gradient ascent on the simplex that no other distribution does better. The DPO identity checks out too: $r_4 - r_1 = 2$ and $\beta[\log(0.364/0.1) - \log(0.197/0.4)] = 1.292 + 0.708 = 2.000$. With $\beta = 0.5$ the policy sharpens to $(0.049, 0.274, 0.003, 0.674)$ at a KL cost of $1.14$ nats; with $\beta = 2$ it stays at $(0.311, 0.384, 0.094, 0.211)$, KL $0.10$. For the PPO clip with $\epsilon = 0.2$: an advantage of $+2$ and a ratio of $1.3$ give $\min(2.6, 2.4) = 2.4$ with zero gradient, while an advantage of $-2$ and the same ratio give $\min(-2.6, -2.4) = -2.6$ with the full gradient.

**Real-life example (NLP — aligning an assistant).** A team fine-tunes a chat model on 50,000 human comparisons of two responses to the same prompt. The RLHF route fits a reward model to those comparisons (Bradley–Terry), then runs PPO on fresh samples from the policy with the KL penalty, monitoring the KL to the reference and stopping when it reaches a budget of a few nats per response; the DPO route trains directly on the 50,000 pairs with the loss above and a single hyper-parameter $\beta$. Both routes move the model along the same reward–KL frontier (Case study 3), and both can over-shoot it: a model that always answers at great length may score well with a reward model that was trained on raters who liked thoroughness, which is the reward hacking of Section 10 wearing a tie.

*Model-based RL and planning.* If a model is available or can be learned, planning replaces experience: AlphaZero's **Monte Carlo tree search** expands a tree from the current position, selects children by a UCB-style score (mean value plus a prior-weighted exploration bonus, Section 2), evaluates leaves with a value network, and backs values up the tree exactly as value iteration does; the visit counts at the root become the improved policy and the training target for the policy network — generalised policy iteration with search as the improvement operator. Learned models (Dyna, MuZero, world models) let the agent generate imagined transitions, multiplying the data; their failure mode is that planning *exploits model errors* as eagerly as it exploits real structure.

*Offline RL and off-policy evaluation.* Hospitals, ad platforms and robot fleets have logs collected under a behaviour policy $b$ and want the value of a new policy $\pi$ without deploying it. **Importance sampling** gives an unbiased estimate, $\hat V^\pi = \frac1n\sum_i \Big[\prod_{t} \frac{\pi(a_t^{(i)} \mid s_t^{(i)})}{b(a_t^{(i)} \mid s_t^{(i)})}\Big] G^{(i)}$, but the product of per-step ratios explodes with the horizon: ten steps in which $\pi$ assigns $0.9$ to actions $b$ chose with probability $0.5$ give a weight of $1.8^{10} \approx 357$, and the reverse case a weight of $0.0028$ — a handful of trajectories dominate the average. Per-decision and weighted importance sampling, doubly robust estimators that combine a model with corrections, and fitted Q-evaluation reduce the variance at the cost of bias. **Offline RL** algorithms (conservative Q-learning and its relatives) add a penalty that keeps the learned policy close to the data distribution — the same instinct as RLHF's KL term — because Q-learning from a fixed dataset otherwise overestimates the value of actions it has never seen.

**In ML/AI this is …** the `ratio = exp(logp_new - logp_old)` and `torch.clamp(ratio, 1 - eps, 1 + eps)` lines in a PPO implementation; the `beta` and `ref_model` arguments of a DPO trainer; the PUCT formula $Q(s, a) + c\, P(s, a)\sqrt{\sum_b N(s, b)}/(1 + N(s, a))$ in AlphaZero's search; and the importance-weighted estimators behind every "counterfactual evaluation" of a recommender system.

## 10. Pitfalls and evaluation: what RL does and does not promise

**Intuition.** Every method in this module maximises *exactly* the reward you wrote down, in *exactly* the environment it was trained in, with *exactly* the data its own behaviour generated. Each of those three "exactly"s is a place where the mathematics is faithful and the practitioner's intention is not.

**Definitions.** **Reward hacking** (specification gaming) is the optimisation of a proxy reward in a way its designer did not intend; **non-stationarity** is the fact that the data distribution $d^{\pi_\theta}$ changes as $\theta$ changes, so the i.i.d. assumptions behind SGD and generalisation bounds ([Module 15](../../part-5-learning-theory/15-generalization-and-learning-theory/README.md)) do not hold; **sample efficiency** is the number of environment interactions needed to reach a performance level; **safe exploration** asks that the agent's exploratory actions never violate a constraint during learning. On evaluation: because returns depend on the random seed through exploration, initialisation and environment noise, a single training run is one sample from a distribution with heavy tails, and conclusions require several seeds and confidence intervals ([Module 10](../../part-4-statistics/10-estimation-and-sampling/README.md)) — Henderson et al. (2018) showed that the same algorithm with different seeds can produce learning curves that look like different algorithms.

**Worked example.** An algorithm is run with five seeds and the final returns are $210, 190, 40, 230, 205$. The mean is $175$, the median $205$, the sample standard deviation $76$ and the standard error of the mean $34$: a competing method reporting $200 \pm 10$ cannot be distinguished from this one, and quoting the best seed ($230$) or dropping the "broken" run ($40$) would both be misreporting. The broken seed is information — one run in five diverged — not noise to be removed. On the cost side: the DQN result of Section 7 used about 200 million frames per game, which at 60 frames per second is 38 days of play, against the two hours of practice the human comparison received; the gap is the sample-efficiency problem in one number.

**Real-life examples.** *Reward hacking*: a boat-racing agent trained to maximise the game score learned to circle in a lagoon collecting respawning power-ups, scoring higher than human players while never finishing the race (OpenAI's CoastRunners example, 2016). In RLHF the analogue is **reward over-optimisation**: the KL-regularised policy's true quality rises with the proxy reward at first and then falls as the policy exploits the reward model's errors, which is why $\beta$ and early stopping matter. *Non-stationarity and safe exploration* (healthcare): a dosing agent cannot try a dangerous dose to see what happens, and the patients it treats change with the policy; constrained MDPs, offline RL from historical records and conservative policies that stay near clinical practice are the standard responses. *Non-stationarity* (e-commerce): a bandit that was optimal last month is wrong after a redesign changes click-through rates; sliding-window and discounted UCB forget old data to keep tracking.

**What RL promises — and does not.** With a correct model, dynamic programming finds the optimal policy with a guarantee. With a tabular representation, enough exploration and decaying step sizes, Q-learning converges to it. Policy gradients converge to a stationary point of $J$, not necessarily its maximum. Beyond that — function approximation, off-policy data, learned reward models — the theory offers conditions and bounds, not guarantees, and the empirical results are strong but seed-sensitive. RL is the right tool when decisions interact over time and a simulator or a lot of safe interaction is available; it is the wrong tool when a supervised or bandit formulation captures the problem, because those come with far smaller variance and far stronger guarantees.

**In ML/AI this is …** the `kl_coef` and reward-model-score monitoring in an RLHF run, the "mean ± standard deviation over 5 seeds" convention of RL papers, the reward-shaping and constraint terms added to a robotics objective, and the reason production recommender systems overwhelmingly deploy contextual bandits rather than full MDP solvers.

## Common pitfalls and misconceptions

1. **"The discount factor is just a mathematical convenience."** → It defines the objective: a reward 100 steps away is worth $0.366$ of its face value at $\gamma = 0.99$ and $0.00003$ at $\gamma = 0.9$, so the two agents are solving different problems and may prefer different policies. → Choose $\gamma$ from the problem's time scale ($1/(1 - \gamma)$ is the horizon you care about), then check that the optimal policy is stable under small changes of $\gamma$.
2. **"ε-greedy with a fixed ε eventually finds the best arm, so its regret is fine."** → It finds the best arm but keeps pulling the others a fraction $\varepsilon$ of the time forever: regret grows linearly in $T$, whereas UCB and Thompson sampling achieve $O(\log T)$. → Decay $\varepsilon$, or use an uncertainty-driven rule.
3. **"Q-learning is on-policy because it learns from the agent's own actions."** → The distinction is about the *target*: Q-learning bootstraps from $\max_{a'} Q(s', a')$, the value of the greedy policy, whatever action the agent then takes; SARSA bootstraps from the action actually taken. The data may come from any exploratory policy. → On the cliff-walking task this is why Q-learning's greedy path hugs the cliff while SARSA's stays safe and earns more during learning.
4. **"TD is biased, so Monte Carlo must be better."** → MC is unbiased but its variance is that of the whole remaining return; TD's bias shrinks as the value estimates improve and its variance is that of a single transition. On finite data TD usually has the lower mean-squared error, and it works without episode ends. → Use the bias–variance lens, and interpolate with TD(λ) when unsure.
5. **"A constant learning rate is fine for Q-learning, like in deep learning."** → Convergence to $Q^*$ needs $\sum \alpha_t = \infty$ and $\sum \alpha_t^2 < \infty$; with a constant $\alpha$ the estimates keep fluctuating by an amount proportional to $\alpha$. → Decay the step size in stationary problems, or accept tracking noise in non-stationary ones deliberately.
6. **"Subtracting a baseline from the return changes what the policy gradient estimates."** → $\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}[\nabla\log\pi(a \mid s)] = \nabla\sum_a \pi(a \mid s) = 0$, so any state-dependent baseline leaves the expectation untouched and only changes the variance. → Always use one; a learned $\hat V(s)$ is the standard choice. (A baseline that depends on the *action* would bias the estimate.)
7. **"Fitting a neural network to the TD target is just regression, so it converges."** → The target moves with the parameters (bootstrapping), the data are off-policy (replay) and the approximator generalises: the deadly triad, which can diverge even with linear features. → Target networks, replay, small learning rates, and monitoring of Q-value magnitudes are not optional.
8. **"A higher reward-model score means a better language model."** → The optimiser exploits the reward model's errors as readily as its signal; past some point the proxy keeps rising while human-judged quality falls. → Keep the KL penalty, monitor KL to the reference, and evaluate with held-out human judgements.

## Real-world case studies

### Case study 1: an adaptive clinical trial with Thompson sampling

Two treatments have true response rates $p_A = 0.4$ and $p_B = 0.6$, unknown to the trial. A conventional design randomises 200 patients 1:1, so 100 receive the inferior treatment and the expected number of responders is $100 \times 0.4 + 100 \times 0.6 = 100$. A response-adaptive design uses Thompson sampling: each patient is assigned by drawing $\tilde p_A \sim \text{Beta}(1 + s_A, 1 + f_A)$ and $\tilde p_B \sim \text{Beta}(1 + s_B, 1 + f_B)$ from the current posteriors and giving the treatment with the larger draw. Simulating this trial 200 times (seeded; the lesson notebook does it in Section 2) gives: on average only about **28** of the 200 patients receive treatment A (standard deviation across trials about 25; in 2% of trials an unlucky early run sends more than half the patients to A), and the expected number of responders rises to about **114** — fourteen more patients helped per trial. That is the ethical case for adaptive designs, and the mathematics is exactly the Beta–Bernoulli posterior of [Module 11](../../part-4-statistics/11-likelihood-and-bayesian-inference/README.md) plus the probability-matching rule of Section 2.

The cost appears at the end of the trial. Declare B superior if the posterior probability $P(p_B > p_A \mid \text{data}) > 0.95$. Under 1:1 randomisation this happens in about **89%** of simulated trials — what the textbook power calculation predicts, since a difference of 0.2 with standard error $\sqrt{2 \cdot 0.24/100} \approx 0.069$ is 2.9 standard errors and $\Phi(2.9 - 1.645) \approx 0.89$ — but under Thompson sampling in only about **74%**, because the inferior arm is sampled so rarely that its response rate is estimated from a few dozen patients and the posterior stays wide. Regret minimisation and hypothesis testing ([Module 12](../../part-4-statistics/12-hypothesis-testing-and-experiments/README.md)) are *different objectives*: a bandit optimises outcomes for the patients inside the trial, a fixed design optimises the information delivered to the patients outside it. Real adaptive trials compromise — capping the allocation imbalance, running a burn-in of equal allocation, or using "top-two" Thompson sampling, which deliberately keeps sampling the runner-up — and the regulator's power requirement is the constraint that decides where on that trade-off the design sits. The same arithmetic governs headline tests on a news site, with "patients" replaced by visitors and "power" by the publisher's wish to *learn* which headline style works rather than merely to serve the best one today.

### Case study 2: inventory control as an exact MDP

A small shop stocks one product with a shelf capacity of 4 units. Each morning it observes the stock $s \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$ and orders $a$ units at a cost of 4 each (orders beyond the capacity are truncated). Daily demand is 0, 1, 2 or 3 units with probabilities $0.2, 0.4, 0.3, 0.1$ (mean 1.3). Each unit sold earns 10, each unit left over at closing costs 1 to hold, and each unit of unmet demand costs 3 in lost goodwill. With $\gamma = 0.95$ this is an MDP with 5 states and 5 actions whose transition probabilities and expected rewards follow directly from the demand distribution: from $s = 0$ with $a = 2$, for instance, the expected reward is $-8 + (0.4 \cdot 10 + 0.3 \cdot 20 + 0.1 \cdot 20) - (0.2 \cdot 2 + 0.4 \cdot 1) \cdot 1 - 0.1 \cdot 1 \cdot 3 = -8 + 12 - 0.8 - 0.3 = 2.9$, and the next state is 2, 1, 0 or 0 with probabilities $0.2, 0.4, 0.3, 0.1$.

Value iteration converges in a few hundred sweeps (each a $5 \times 5 \times 5$ tensor contraction) to $V^* = (118.8, 122.8, 126.8, 130.6, 133.0)$ and the optimal policy: order 2 units when the shelf is empty, 1 unit when one is left, nothing otherwise — an **order-up-to-2** rule, the base-stock policy that inventory theory predicts for this cost structure. Evaluating fixed policies by the linear solve of Section 4 quantifies the alternatives: "fill the shelf to 4 every day" has $V(0) = 91.2$, and the plausible heuristic "order 2 whenever stock is at most 1" has $V(0) = 117.3$, within 1.5 of optimal but not equal to it. In per-day terms — multiply by $1 - \gamma$ — the optimal rule earns about 5.94 per day against 4.56 for filling the shelf: a 30% difference arising purely from holding costs on stock that the demand distribution says will not sell. Real supply chains have thousands of products, lead times (which enlarge the state with orders in transit), and demand that must be forecast rather than read off a table; the exact MDP of five states is the oracle against which the approximate methods of Sections 6–8 are validated, and the base-stock structure it reveals is what practitioners parametrise and tune.

### Case study 3: the reward–KL frontier in language-model alignment

Section 9's toy, $\pi_{\text{ref}} = (0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1)$ over four candidate responses with rewards $(1, 2, 0, 3)$, is small enough to display the whole trade-off that a KL-regularised fine-tuning run navigates. The reference model's expected reward is $0.4 + 0.6 + 0 + 0.3 = 1.3$. The optimal policy at $\beta = 2$ moves modestly, to $(0.311, 0.384, 0.094, 0.211)$, raising expected reward to $1.71$ at a KL cost of $0.10$ nats. At $\beta = 1$ it reaches $2.09$ at KL $0.39$; at $\beta = 0.5$, $2.62$ at KL $1.14$; and as $\beta \to 0$ it collapses onto the single best response for a reward of 3 and a KL of $\log(1/0.1) = 2.30$ nats. Plotting reward against KL traces a concave frontier: the first tenth of a nat buys $0.41$ of reward (4.0 per nat), the next $0.29$ nats buy $0.38$ (1.3 per nat), the next $0.76$ buy $0.52$ (0.7 per nat), and the last $1.16$ buy only $0.38$ (0.3 per nat) — diminishing returns that are a theorem, not an accident: $\beta$ plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier on a KL budget ([Module 05](../../part-2-calculus-and-optimization/05-optimization/README.md)), so the slope of the frontier at the $\beta$-optimal point is exactly $\beta$ — one more nat of KL buys $\beta$ units of reward, less and less as $\beta$ falls — and the chord slopes above ($1.3$ between $\beta = 2$ and $1$, $0.7$ between $1$ and $0.5$) sit between the endpoint slopes as a concave curve requires.

Now add the fact that the rewards are a *model's* estimates. Suppose response 4's true quality is 1.5, not 3 — the reward model has over-rated an answer that is, say, confidently wrong. The reference policy's *true* expected reward is $0.4 + 0.6 + 0 + 0.15 = 1.15$. At $\beta = 2$ the optimised policy achieves a true reward of $0.311 + 0.768 + 0 + 0.317 = 1.40$, an improvement; at $\beta = 1$, $0.197 + 0.804 + 0.546 = 1.55$; at $\beta = 0.5$, $0.049 + 0.548 + 1.011 = 1.61$; and at $\beta \to 0$ the policy puts everything on response 4 for a true reward of $1.5$ — *lower* than at $\beta = 0.5$ even though the proxy reward kept rising from $2.62$ to $3.0$. The proxy and the truth part ways once the policy has moved far enough from the reference that it lives where the reward model is wrong. That is reward over-optimisation in miniature; at scale it is why RLHF runs report both the reward-model score and the KL to the reference, stop when the KL budget is spent, and validate with human raters. DPO inherits the same frontier through its $\beta$: it fits the policy that would be optimal for the reward implied by the preference data, with the same Boltzmann re-weighting, and the same need to choose how far from the reference to go.

| Bellman expectation | $V^\pi = R^\pi + \gamma P^\pi V^\pi = (I - \gamma P^\pi)^{-1}R^\pi$ |
| Bellman optimality | $V^*(s) = \max_a [R(s,a) + \gamma\sum_{s'}P(s' \mid s,a)V^*(s')]$; $\pi^* = \arg\max_a Q^*$ |
| Contraction | $\lVert TU - TV\rVert_\infty \le \gamma\lVert U - V\rVert_\infty$; error after $k$ sweeps $\le \gamma^k\lVert V_0 - V^*\rVert_\infty$ |
| Stopping rule | $\lVert V_{k+1} - V_k\rVert_\infty \le \epsilon \Rightarrow \lVert V_{k+1} - V^*\rVert_\infty \le \epsilon\gamma/(1-\gamma)$ |
| Policy improvement | $\pi'(s) = \arg\max_a Q^\pi(s,a) \Rightarrow V^{\pi'} \ge V^\pi$ |
| MC update | $V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha(G_t - V(s))$ |
| TD(0) | $V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha[r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)]$ |
| SARSA / Q-learning | target $r + \gamma Q(s', a')$ (action taken) / $r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q(s', a')$ (greedy) |
| Robbins–Monro | $\sum_t \alpha_t = \infty$, $\sum_t \alpha_t^2 < \infty$ |
| Semi-gradient TD | $w \leftarrow w + \alpha[r + \gamma w^\top\phi(s') - w^\top\phi(s)]\phi(s)$ |
| Log-derivative trick | $\nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f] = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta]$ |
| Policy gradient | $\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\big[\sum_t \gamma^t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\,(G_t - b(s_t))\big]$, any $b(s)$; practical code often drops $\gamma^t$ (biased) |
| Softmax score | $\nabla_h \log\pi(a) = \mathbf{e}_a - \pi$ |
| Advantage | $A^\pi(s,a) = Q^\pi(s,a) - V^\pi(s)$; one-step estimate $r + \gamma\hat V(s') - \hat V(s)$ |
| PPO clip | $\min\big(\rho\hat A,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat A\big)$, $\rho = \pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}$ |
| RLHF objective | $\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[r] - \beta\,\operatorname{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}})$ |
| Optimal KL-regularised policy | $\pi^*(y) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)e^{r(y)/\beta}/Z$; optimal value $\beta\log Z$ |
| DPO loss | $-\log\sigma\big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}\big)$ |

## Self-assessment

1. **An agent with $\gamma = 0.9$ faces a choice between a reward of 10 now and a reward of 30 in ten steps. Which does it prefer, and at what $\gamma$ would it be indifferent?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The delayed reward is worth $30 \cdot 0.9^{10} \approx 30 \cdot 0.349 = 10.46 > 10$, so it prefers to wait — barely. Indifference requires $30\gamma^{10} = 10$, i.e. $\gamma = (1/3)^{1/10} \approx 0.896$. Below that the agent takes the 10 now. The discount factor is part of the problem specification, not a tuning knob.

   </details>
2. **Why does UCB1 use $\ln t$ rather than a constant in the bonus, and what happens to the bonus of an arm that is never pulled?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The confidence level tightens as $t$ grows ($t^{-4}$ in the Hoeffding derivation), so the bound stays valid simultaneously over all rounds; a constant bonus would stop exploring arms whose estimates happened to look bad. An arm that is never pulled keeps $N_a$ fixed while $\ln t$ grows, so its bonus increases without limit and it is eventually pulled again — every arm is sampled infinitely often, but at a logarithmic rate.

   </details>
3. **Thompson sampling with a $\text{Beta}(1,1)$ prior has pulled arm 1 ten times with 7 successes and arm 2 twice with 2 successes. Which arm is more likely to be pulled next, and why is the answer not obvious from the means alone?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Posteriors are $\text{Beta}(8, 4)$ (mean $0.667$, sd $\approx 0.13$) and $\text{Beta}(3, 1)$ (mean $0.75$, sd $\approx 0.19$). Arm 2 has the higher mean and the wider posterior, so it is pulled more often than not — but arm 1 is pulled whenever its draw exceeds arm 2's, which happens a substantial fraction of the time because the posteriors overlap heavily. Probability matching allocates pulls by the posterior probability of being best, which depends on the spread as well as the mean.

   </details>
4. **State the Bellman expectation equation for $V^\pi$ in matrix form and explain why $I - \gamma P^\pi$ is invertible.**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   $V^\pi = R^\pi + \gamma P^\pi V^\pi$, so $(I - \gamma P^\pi)V^\pi = R^\pi$. $P^\pi$ is row-stochastic, so all its eigenvalues have modulus at most 1 and those of $\gamma P^\pi$ have modulus at most $\gamma < 1$; hence $1$ is not an eigenvalue of $\gamma P^\pi$ and $I - \gamma P^\pi$ is non-singular. Its inverse is the Neumann series $\sum_k \gamma^k (P^\pi)^k$, which is the return written as a sum over time steps.

   </details>
5. **Value iteration on an MDP with $\gamma = 0.99$ starts with $\lVert V_0 - V^*\rVert_\infty = 100$. How many sweeps guarantee an error below $0.01$? What if $\gamma = 0.9$?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   We need $\gamma^k \cdot 100 \le 0.01$, i.e. $k \ge \ln(10^4)/\ln(1/\gamma)$. For $\gamma = 0.99$ that is $9.21/0.01005 \approx 917$ sweeps; for $\gamma = 0.9$ it is $9.21/0.1054 \approx 88$. The sweep count scales like $1/(1-\gamma)$ — a long horizon makes planning slow, which is one reason policy iteration (few expensive iterations) is attractive for large $\gamma$.

   </details>
6. **A learner says: "I ran value iteration until successive iterates differed by less than $10^{-3}$, so my values are accurate to $10^{-3}$." Is that right?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   Not quite. From $\lVert V_{k+1} - V_k\rVert_\infty \le \epsilon$ and the contraction property, $\lVert V_{k+1} - V^*\rVert_\infty \le \epsilon\gamma/(1-\gamma)$. With $\gamma = 0.9$ the guarantee is $9 \times 10^{-3}$, with $\gamma = 0.99$ it is $0.099$ — a hundred times the stopping threshold. The greedy policy is usually correct long before the values are, but the value accuracy claim needs the $1/(1-\gamma)$ factor.

   </details>
7. **In cliff walking, Q-learning's greedy path runs along the cliff edge while SARSA's stays several rows away, yet Q-learning's path is shorter. Which agent collects more reward *during training* with ε = 0.1, and why?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   SARSA. Q-learning learns $Q^*$, the value of the greedy policy, and its optimal path is the shortest one — but while training it still explores with probability 0.1, and random steps next to the cliff cost $-100$. SARSA learns the value of the ε-greedy policy it actually follows, so it accounts for its own slips and prefers the safe route; its training returns are higher even though its greedy path is longer. Once exploration is switched off, Q-learning's policy is the better one.

   </details>
8. **Why is TD(0) said to be biased, and why does that not stop it from converging?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   The target $r + \gamma V(s')$ uses the current estimate $V(s')$, which is wrong during learning, so the expected update direction is not the one that an unbiased sample of $V^\pi(s)$ would give. But the update is a stochastic approximation of the Bellman operator, which is a contraction with fixed point $V^\pi$; with Robbins–Monro step sizes and sufficient visitation the bias disappears in the limit. The variance saved by bootstrapping is what makes TD competitive with Monte Carlo on finite data.

   </details>
9. **Prove in two lines that subtracting a baseline $b(s)$ from the return leaves the policy gradient unbiased. Would a baseline $b(s, a)$ also be fine?**
   <details><summary>Answer</summary>

   $\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[b(s)\nabla\log\pi(a \mid s)] = b(s)\sum_a \nabla\pi(a \mid s) = b(s)\nabla\sum_a\pi(a \mid s) = b(s)\nabla 1 = 0$. An action-dependent $b(s, a)$ cannot be pulled out of the sum, so $\sum_a b(s, a)\nabla\pi(a \mid s)$ is generally non-zero and the estimate becomes biased (special constructions with a correction term exist, but a plain action-dependent baseline is not free).

   </details>
10. **Derive the optimal policy for maximising $\mathbb{E}_\pi[r] - \beta\operatorname{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})$ and explain what $\beta \to 0$ and $\beta \to \infty$ give.**
    <details><summary>Answer</summary>

    Write the objective as $-\beta\operatorname{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi^*) + \beta\log Z$ with $\pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\,e^{r/\beta}$; since KL is non-negative and zero only at $\pi = \pi^*$, the optimum is $\pi^*$. As $\beta \to \infty$ the exponent flattens and $\pi^* \to \pi_{\text{ref}}$ (no fine-tuning); as $\beta \to 0$ the Boltzmann factor concentrates on the highest-reward response with positive reference probability (greedy reward maximisation, maximal KL). DPO's loss is obtained by solving this form for $r$ and substituting into the Bradley–Terry likelihood, where $\log Z$ cancels.

    </details>
